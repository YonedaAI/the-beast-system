Who makes the rules? Part II of the Beast System series treats rule making as one pillar of a six-function model of governance, and separates two questions that are often run together: whether the function is necessary, and how its control is distributed.

From an axiom of plurality (members' preferences diverge, yet some choices bind everyone), together with environmental change and the persistence of the group, we prove that every persistent collective implements a rule-making function, though not any particular institution. In stateless and consensus societies it appears as camp discussion, lineage moots, oratory without command, and fission.

We measure the distribution of rule-making power with power indices over decision procedures. The key result is that necessity places no constraint on concentration: for a group of n members and every k from 1 to n, some procedure satisfies the necessity lemma with concentration exactly 1/k. Three conditional propositions then describe how rule making can turn into capture: monitoring incentives that vanish as organizations grow, veto structures that protect preferential status quo rules, and appointment channels that couple rule making to courts, security services and the economy.

Market capitalism, communist party-states, social democracy, Islamic states, Catholic monarchies and secular democracies are compared on identical questions, with seven hypotheses stating what would count against each claim. Revelation 13 and 17 are read typologically as an image of rule-making authority that admits no contest, applied to a structure and not to any group or state.

The ideology changes. The institutional functions remain.
