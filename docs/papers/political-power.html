1 Introduction

Every polity answers the question of who makes the laws. A Ju/’hoansi camp answers it by talk around the fire and by the option of moving away. The Soviet Union answered it with a Politburo, a Central Committee apparatus, and a legislature that ratified what the party had decided. Bourbon France answered it with royal ordinances that the Parlements registered or remonstrated against. The Islamic Republic of Iran answers it with an elected assembly whose bills pass to a council of jurists for review. The answers differ in who holds the power, how many share it, and how it can be contested. They do not differ in whether the question must be answered.

This observation is the rule-making instance of the thesis that organizes the series to which this paper belongs: the ideology changes, the institutional functions remain. The series distinguishes six functions that every governance system performs (control of resources, rule making, interpretation, legitimation, enforcement, and production) and calls the resulting structure the Hierarchy of the Beast System, after the image in Revelation 13 of authority, allegiance, and economic participation bound together (Largo 2026). The name designates a universal structure. It is never a label for a religion, a people, a profession, a regime, or a person.

Two claims must be kept apart. The first, which the series calls Proposition A, is that every functioning governance system contains the six functions and the dependencies among them. Part VII proves it in general from minimal axioms about collective life (Long 2026c). The second, Proposition B, is that the functions can become concentrated and coordinated so that independent accountability fails. Proposition B is contingent and measurable, and Proposition A does not imply it (Part VII). This paper supplies the rule-making piece of both arguments. It derives the necessity of the rule-making function from the axiom of plurality, and it studies the conditions under which rule making becomes an instrument of capture.

The source framework (Largo 2026) placed legislation, regulation, adjudication, taxation, licensing, and administration in a single function of rule production. The series splits that function. Rule making, the subject here, covers the production and change of general rules and of decisions that bind the collective as a whole: legislation, executive decree, administrative rule making, party resolutions that have the force of law, and the appointment of the officials who do these things. Interpretation and adjudication of rules in particular cases belong to Part III, whose determination procedure (Long 2026e, Definition 2.3) is the interpretive counterpart of the rule-making procedure defined here. The split matters because the two activities have different failure modes. A captured legislature produces preferential rules; a captured court applies neutral rules selectively.

The comparative literature on rule making is large but divided by regime. Elite and pluralist theory developed around Western democracies (Mosca 1939; Michels 1915; Dahl 1961; Mills 1956). Selectorate theory and the study of authoritarian institutions developed around autocracies (Bueno de Mesquita et al. 2003; Svolik 2012; Gandhi and Przeworski 2007). Classical theories of the caliphate and of divine-right monarchy were written by participants in those orders (al-Mawardi 1996; Bodin 1992; Bossuet 1990). These literatures rarely share a metric. A reader can learn how the United States Congress responds to affluent constituents (Bartels 2008; Gilens and Page 2014) and how the Soviet nomenklatura filled posts (Hough and Fainsod 1979; Voslensky 1984) without a common vocabulary in which to compare the two.

A common vocabulary for rule making can be built from the source framework’s multiplex network. Rule making becomes one layer of that network, with control shares in the layer measured by power indices over decision procedures and contestability split into five components specific to rule making. Most coupling between this layer and the others runs through an appointment channel, which this paper defines and which Parts III and V use for judges and security chiefs.

The central result is a separation. From the plurality axiom, together with environmental change and the persistence of the group, every persistent collective implements a rule-making function (Section 3). Elementary results on power indices show that this necessity is compatible with every level of concentration from \(1/n\) to \(1\) (Section 2), so the existence of rule makers says nothing about whether they can be contested. The stateless and consensus societies usually cited against the necessity claim display the function at its least concentrated, paired with cheap exit. Across six regime types the same function is held under very different sources of legitimacy, and its concentration and contestability vary independently of those sources (Section 5).

Three mechanisms recur in that comparison: monitoring incentives that weaken as organizations grow, vetoes held by the beneficiaries of preferential rules, and appointment chains run by a single coalition. Section 6 models each and identifies the check that interrupts it. Every one of those checks acts on contestability, not on the existence of rule makers.

The paper does not code any regime numerically. The concentration and contestability measures for rule making are defined and the properties of the concentration measure are proved, but every comparative statement about a real polity is qualitative and rests on the cited historical and political-science literature. Numerical values for actual countries require the preregistered coding protocol of the source framework (Largo 2026), which no Part of the series has yet carried out.

2 Rule making as a layer of the polity network

2.1 Shared objects

The objects in this subsection are defined in the source framework and are used here without modification (Largo 2026). The polity at time \(t\) is a directed multiplex network \(G_t=(V_t,E_t)\) whose nodes are organizations or coherent actor groups. Each pillar \(m\in\mathcal{M}=\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{J},\mathsf{I},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{W}\}\), with \(M=|\mathcal{M}|=6\), has an adjacency layer \(A^m_t\) with edge weights \(a^m_{ij,t}\in[0,1]\) that record the documented capacity of actor \(i\) to constrain a feasible action of actor \(j\) through mechanism \(m\). A many-to-many role map assigns each actor the pillars in which it acts. Actor \(i\) holds a normalized control share \(s^m_{i,t}\) in pillar \(m\), with \(\sum_i s^m_{i,t}=1\). Concentration is the Herfindahl-Hirschman index \(H_{m,t}=\sum_i (s^m_{i,t})^2\). Contestability \(Q_{m,t}\in[0,1]\) summarizes transparency, appeal rights, independence, competition, and credible replacement. Unaccountable concentration is the illustrative index \[\begin{equation} U_t=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\omega_m H_{m,t}(1-Q_{m,t}),\qquad \omega_m\ge 0,\quad \sum_m\omega_m=1, \label{eq:U} \end{equation}\] with weights fixed in advance. Cross-pillar integration \(C_{mn,t}\in[0,1]\) is the extent to which one coalition commands appointments, budgets, sanctions, or agenda in both \(m\) and \(n\), and the integration statistic is \(\mathcal{I}_t=\frac{2}{M(M-1)}\sum_{m<n}C_{mn,t}\), the mean over the fifteen pillar pairs. For couplings of rule making this paper uses the coalition-overlap measure \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\) of Equation [eq:overlap] as its operative definition. A capture link \((i\to j,m)\) requires privileged access or discretion, a resulting weakening of applicable accountability, and material evidence tying the asymmetry to an institutional output. A strong classification under the source’s criterion requires both high \(U_t\) and high \(\mathcal{I}_t\); differentiation alone never suffices (Largo 2026, Proposition 1). Pillar labels are written in sans-serif (\(\mathsf{P}\) for rule making) to keep them distinct from the integration statistic \(\mathcal{I}_t\) and from the role map. For the rule-making pillar the paper writes \(s^{\mathsf{P}}_i\), \(H^{\mathsf{P}}_t=H_{\mathsf{P},t}\), and \(Q^{\mathsf{P}}_t=Q_{\mathsf{P},t}\), dropping the time index when it plays no role. The letter \(n\) is reserved below for the number of members of a collective, so a pillar other than \(\mathsf{P}\) is written \(\ell\) and the couplings of rule making appear as \(C_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\).

Three terms are kept apart. Unaccountable concentration in rule making is the pillar’s term \(H^{\mathsf{P}}(1-Q^{\mathsf{P}})\) in \(U_t\). A capture link \((i\to j,\mathsf{P})\) is the dyadic relation defined above, with a general rule as its institutional output. Systemic capture is the joint condition of high \(U_t\), high \(\mathcal{I}_t\), and documented capture links that the series calls Proposition B. Capture, regulatory capture, and state capture as objects of the resource pillar are developed in Part I (Long 2026b, Sections 4 and 6).

2.2 The rule-making function

Definition 1 (Rule system and collective decision). Let \(N\) be the set of members of a collective, \(|N|=n\ge 2\). A rule system \(R\in\mathcal{R}\) is a set of general prescriptions that apply to classes of persons and situations rather than to named individuals or single events. A collective decision is a choice \(x\) from a set \(X\) of at least two alternatives such that the chosen alternative applies to all members at once (when to move camp, whether to go to war, which tax rate to levy). Each member \(i\) ranks the alternatives by a strict linear order \(\succ_i\) (complete and without indifference between distinct alternatives). Members’ preferences diverge over \(X\) if no alternative is unanimously best: for every \(x\in X\) some member \(i\) and some \(y\in X\) satisfy \(y\succ_i x\). Write \(R_t\) for the rule system in force at time \(t\), where time runs over the integers.

Definition 2 (Rule-making function). Let \(T\) be a window of consecutive periods. The rule-making function \(\mathsf{P}\) is implemented in a collective over \(T\) in sense (a) if some set of members or practices of the collective repeatedly selects collective decisions from sets of alternatives over which members’ preferences diverge, and in sense (b) if such a practice replaces the rule system in force, \(R_{t+1}\neq R_t\) for some \(t\) with \(t,t+1\in T\). The number of replacements in \(T\) measures how often sense (b) is exercised. The selecting or replacing practice is the collective’s rule-making procedure. Its rule-making profile records who may initiate a proposal, who must assent for it to take effect, who may block it, and who can be removed for its consequences.

The definition locates the function in an activity, not an office. A legislature, a king in council, a party congress, a meeting of the whole camp, and a gradual change of custom under social pressure all count, provided the activity occurs. This choice is deliberate and follows the standard objection to functional analysis: the need for a function does not establish the existence of any particular institution that performs it (Hempel 1959; Merton 1968). The definition also fixes the boundary with the interpretive pillar.

Definition 3 (Boundary between rule making and interpretation). An act belongs to \(\mathsf{P}\) if its output is a general rule or a collective decision, and to \(\mathsf{J}\) if its output is the application of a given general rule to a particular case or dispute. Mixed institutions are split by act, not by organization.

Table 1 applies the boundary to cases that recur in the comparisons below. The role map of the source is many-to-many, so a single organization can act in both pillars. A constitutional court that annuls a statute applies a higher rule to a lower one and acts in \(\mathsf{J}\); a legislature that responds with a constitutional amendment acts in \(\mathsf{P}\). Common-law courts that change a rule by precedent perform a rule-making act through an interpretive institution, and the role map records them in both pillars.

2.3 Control shares from decision procedures

The source framework defines \(s^m_{i,t}\) as a normalized share of decision-relevant control but leaves its measurement open. For rule making a natural baseline is available. A rule-making procedure is, at a minimum, a specification of which coalitions can enact a proposal against the status quo. The baseline below models each decision as such a binary vote. When a decision involves three or more alternatives, an agenda rule determines which proposals are put against the status quo and in what order; that rule is the object of agenda control (Definition 9) and is measured separately. Power indices from cooperative game theory turn the specification of winning coalitions into shares that sum to one.

Definition 4 (Simple game and Shapley-Shubik shares). A simple game on \(N\) is a family \(\mathcal{W}\) of winning coalitions with \(N\in\mathcal{W}\), \(\emptyset\notin\mathcal{W}\), and \(S\in\mathcal{W}\), \(S\subseteq S'\) implying \(S'\in\mathcal{W}\). The game is proper if \(S\in\mathcal{W}\) implies \(N\setminus S\notin\mathcal{W}\), so that two disjoint coalitions cannot both enact incompatible decisions; a rule-making procedure is represented by a proper simple game. An ordering of \(N\) is a bijection \(\pi:\{1,\dots,n\}\to N\), and \(B_\pi(i)=\{\pi(k):k<\pi^{-1}(i)\}\) is the set of members who precede \(i\). Member \(i\) is the pivot in \(\pi\) if \(B_\pi(i)\notin\mathcal{W}\) and \(B_\pi(i)\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{W}\). The Shapley-Shubik share of \(i\) is \(\varphi_i=|\{\pi: i \text{ is the pivot in } \pi\}|/n!\) (Shapley and Shubik 1954). Member \(i\) is a veto player if \(i\in S\) for every \(S\in\mathcal{W}\), and a dictator if \(\{i\}\in\mathcal{W}\) and every winning coalition contains \(i\).

The de jure baseline sets \(s^{\mathsf{P}}_i=\varphi_i\). Actual control can depart from the baseline when an agenda setter, an informal patron, or a donor shapes which proposals reach a vote, and the gap between de jure and de facto power is itself an object of study (Acemoglu and Robinson 2006). The baseline is still useful, because it ties concentration to the written or customary procedure and so gives a reference point against which informal concentration can be measured.

Proposition 5 (Concentration under standard procedures). Let \(\mathcal{W}\) be a simple game on \(N\) with \(|N|=n\), and set \(s^{\mathsf{P}}_i=\varphi_i\) and \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=\sum_i\varphi_i^2\).

  1. In every ordering exactly one member is the pivot, so \(\sum_i\varphi_i=1\).

  2. \(1/n\le H^{\mathsf{P}}\le 1\), with \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/n\) if and only if \(\varphi_i=1/n\) for every \(i\).

  3. If \(\mathcal{W}\) is invariant under every permutation of \(N\) (an anonymous procedure such as a quota rule with quota above \(n/2\), or unanimity among all members), then \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/n\).

  4. If \(\mathcal{W}=\{S: K\subseteq S\}\) for a nonempty \(K\subseteq N\) with \(|K|=k\) (unanimity among the members of \(K\)), then \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/k\). A dictatorship is the case \(k=1\).

  5. A veto player \(i\) satisfies \(\varphi_i\ge\varphi_j\) for every \(j\in N\).

Proof. (a) Fix \(\pi\) and add members in the order \(\pi\). The growing coalition starts at \(\emptyset\notin\mathcal{W}\) and ends at \(N\in\mathcal{W}\). By monotonicity, once the coalition is winning it remains winning, so it changes from losing to winning at exactly one step, and the member added at that step is the unique pivot. Summing over the \(n!\) orderings gives \(\sum_i\varphi_i=1\).

(b) By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(1=(\sum_i\varphi_i)^2\le n\sum_i\varphi_i^2\), with equality if and only if all \(\varphi_i\) are equal. The upper bound holds because \(\varphi_i\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_i\varphi_i=1\) imply \(\sum_i\varphi_i^2\le\sum_i\varphi_i=1\).

(c) If \(\sigma\) is a permutation of \(N\) and \(\mathcal{W}\) is invariant under \(\sigma\), then \(B_{\sigma\circ\pi}(\sigma(i))=\sigma(B_\pi(i))\), so \(i\) is the pivot in \(\pi\) if and only if \(\sigma(i)\) is the pivot in \(\sigma\circ\pi\). Composition with \(\sigma\) is a bijection on orderings, so \(\varphi_i=\varphi_{\sigma(i)}\). Every pair of members is exchanged by some permutation, so all shares are equal and (b) gives \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/n\).

(d) A member outside \(K\) is never the pivot, because adding a member outside \(K\) cannot complete \(K\). In any ordering the pivot is the last member of \(K\) to appear. By symmetry among the members of \(K\), each is last in a fraction \(1/k\) of orderings. Hence \(\varphi_i=1/k\) for \(i\in K\), \(\varphi_i=0\) otherwise, and \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=k\cdot(1/k)^2=1/k\).

(e) Let \(j\neq i\) and let \(j\) be the pivot in \(\pi\). Then \(B_\pi(j)\cup\{j\}\in\mathcal{W}\) contains the veto player \(i\), and \(i\neq j\), so \(i\in B_\pi(j)\). Let \(\pi'\) be \(\pi\) with the positions of \(i\) and \(j\) exchanged. In \(\pi'\) the set preceding \(i\) is \(B_\pi(j)\setminus\{i\}\cup\{j\}\), which does not contain \(i\) and is therefore losing. Adding \(i\) yields \(B_\pi(j)\cup\{j\}\), which is winning. So \(i\) is the pivot in \(\pi'\). The exchange is a bijection on orderings, so the number of orderings in which \(j\) is the pivot is at most the number in which \(i\) is the pivot. ◻

Properness is not needed for the proofs, and every game used below is proper. Part (d) connects concentration to the veto-player tradition: when only the members of \(K\) matter and each can block, concentration equals the reciprocal of the number of veto players (Tsebelis 2002). Part (c) shows that the anonymous procedures attributed to consensus societies produce the least concentrated rule making possible for their size. The next corollary records that the existence of a rule-making procedure says nothing about its concentration.

Corollary 6 (Necessity does not fix concentration). For every \(n\ge2\) and every \(k\in\{1,\dots,n\}\) there is a rule-making procedure on \(n\) members with \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/k\).

Proof. Take any \(K\subseteq N\) with \(|K|=k\) and the unanimity game on \(K\) from Proposition 5(d), with the status quo as the default when no proposal wins and proposals put against the status quo one at a time by a fixed agenda rule. Each vote has a determinate outcome, so the procedure selects a collective decision in every case, and so implements \(\mathsf{P}\) in the sense of Definition 2, whatever the value of \(k\). ◻

The corollary is the rule-making instance of the general non-implication proved in Part VII: the existence of the function (Proposition A) is compatible with any degree of concentration, so concentration (one ingredient of Proposition B) must be established by evidence about the particular polity.

2.4 Worked examples

The index can be computed for any procedure whose winning coalitions are known. Table 2 reports four stylized procedures, the last under two aggregations of actors, with organizations or parties treated as unitary actors. The first two show that the same chamber can produce opposite concentrations depending on the distribution of seats. If one party holds a majority in a chamber that is the only veto player and the party votes as a bloc, the party is a dictator and \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1\). If three parties hold 40, 35, and 25 percent of the seats under majority rule, any two of them form a majority, the game is anonymous among the three parties, and \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/3\) by Proposition 5(c). Two chambers and an executive with an absolute veto form a unanimity game on three actors, so \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/3\) by part (d).

The fourth procedure is modeled on the legislative articles of the constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran (Islamic Republic of Iran 1989). A bill passed by the Majles (\(M\)) becomes law if the Guardian Council (\(G\)) approves it, or, if the Council rejects it and the Majles insists, if the Expediency Council (\(E\)) sides with the Majles. The winning coalitions are therefore \(\{M,G\}\), \(\{M,E\}\), and \(\{M,G,E\}\), and the game is proper. Of the six orderings, \(M\) is the pivot in four and \(G\) and \(E\) in one each, so \(\varphi=(2/3,1/6,1/6)\) and \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/2\). The constitution also gives the Leader the selection of the six clerical members of the Guardian Council and the appointment of the members of the Expediency Council (Islamic Republic of Iran 1989). If \(G\) and \(E\) are treated as a single coalition through that appointment channel, the game becomes unanimity between the Majles and the coalition, and \(\varphi=(1/2,1/2)\). Concentration is again \(1/2\), but the share of the appointed bodies rises from \(1/3\) to \(1/2\). The example shows that \(H^{\mathsf{P}}\) depends on how actors are aggregated, which is why the actor list must be fixed before coding, and that the appointment channel can shift shares without changing the formal procedure. It omits the Leader’s power to set general policy and the Council’s screening of candidates for the Majles, both of which raise practical concentration above the de jure figure.

Values of \(H^{\mathsf{P}}\) are comparable only at a declared level of aggregation. At the member level the players are individuals, as in the consensus band of Proposition 17, where \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/n_a\). At the organ level the players are chambers, councils, parties voting as blocs, or the electorate in a referendum, as in Table 2. An organ-level figure of \(1/3\) and a member-level figure of \(1/40\) do not measure the same thing, and the comparisons in this paper are made within a level, never across levels. Where both levels matter, each organ’s decision can itself be modeled as a game among its members, so that member-level shares are computed through the organ-level procedure; the paper does not pursue that construction.

2.5 Contestability of rule making

The source’s contestability index \(Q_{m,t}\) is generic. For rule making five components can be distinguished and measured separately.

Definition 7 (Components of rule-making contestability). For a polity at time \(t\), let \(q_t=(q^{\mathrm{rep}},q^{\mathrm{ovs}},q^{\mathrm{ent}},q^{\mathrm{trn}},q^{\mathrm{ext}})\in[0,1]^5\) record, in order, the credibility of peaceful replacement of rule makers; the strength of oversight of rule makers by bodies they do not control; the freedom of entry into rule-making competition (forming parties, standing for office, petitioning); the transparency of rule making (publication of drafts, records of who proposed what); and the ease of exit from the jurisdiction or the group, so that cheap exit scores high. The rule-making contestability is \(Q^{\mathsf{P}}_t=g(q_t)\) for a preregistered aggregator \(g:[0,1]^5\to[0,1]\) that is nondecreasing in each argument with \(g(0,\dots,0)=0\) and \(g(1,\dots,1)=1\).

The exit component draws on the analysis of exit and voice that Part VI develops for the series (Long 2026f). Two aggregators bracket the reasonable choices. The arithmetic mean treats the components as substitutes, so strong exit can compensate for absent elections. The geometric mean treats them as complements, so that any component at zero sets \(Q^{\mathsf{P}}\) to zero. The choice changes rankings in exactly the cases that interest the comparison. A foraging band has very cheap exit and open entry into discussion but no formal oversight body; a single-party state may publish drafts and permit petitions while ruling out replacement. Under the geometric mean both score low unless oversight in the band is coded to include direct observation of everyone by everyone. This is a concrete form of the measurement-invariance problem that the source framework raises for all its constructs (Largo 2026), and it is why the aggregator must be fixed before coding begins.

2.6 Appointment channel and agenda control

Most coupling between rule making and the other pillars runs through two channels: the power to appoint the people who exercise the other functions, and the power to decide which questions reach a decision at all.

Definition 8 (Appointment channel). For a pillar \(\ell\in\mathcal{M}\) (the letter \(n\) is reserved for the size of a collective; see Section 2), let \(O_\ell\) be the set of offices that exercise decision-relevant control in \(\ell\), with weights \(w_o\ge0\) and \(\sum_{o\in O_\ell}w_o=1\) equal to each office’s control share. For a coalition \(S\) and an office \(o\), let \(a_{S,o}=1\) if \(S\) can unilaterally appoint or remove the holder of \(o\), and \(a_{S,o}=0\) otherwise. The appointment share of \(S\) in \(\ell\) is \[\alpha_{S,\ell}=\sum_{o\in O_\ell}w_o\,a_{S,o}.\] The appointment channel from \(\mathsf{P}\) to \(\ell\) is the map \(S\mapsto\alpha_{S,\ell}\).

The appointment channel is the instrument through which, in the source’s account, party-state control paths run from central appointment to allocation and enforcement (Largo 2026, Proposition 3). Parts III and V use the same object for judges and for security chiefs. The word “unilaterally” matters. Where an appointment requires the consent of a second body, as in legislative confirmation, no single coalition’s share includes that office unless it controls both bodies.

Definition 9 (Agenda control). An actor exercises agenda control in \(\mathsf{P}\) to the extent that it determines which proposals are considered by the rule-making procedure. A non-decision is a proposal that some members want considered and that is kept from consideration by an actor with agenda control (Bachrach and Baratz 1962).

Agenda control is the second face of power in the classification of Bachrach and Baratz, and the third face, the shaping of preferences themselves, belongs to Part IV (Lukes 2005). Agenda control is invisible to the Shapley-Shubik baseline, which takes the set of proposals as given. This is one reason why the de jure shares of Definition 4 are a lower bound on the concentration of actual rule-making power whenever agenda setting is itself concentrated.

2.7 Couplings to the other pillars

Under the series’ allocation of the fifteen pillar pairs, this paper gives the primary treatment of four: rule making with interpretation (\(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\)), with legitimation (\(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{I}}\)), with enforcement (\(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{C}}\)), and with production (\(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{W}}\)). The pair with resource control, \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{P}}\), belongs to Part I (Long 2026b). Each pair has a characteristic channel.

Whoever appoints and removes judges couples rule making to interpretation, and so does the traffic between statute and precedent. Statutes give courts rules to apply, and judicial applications generate precedents and doctrines that legislatures then codify, override, or leave in place. A coalition that appoints judges without confirmation by another body and can remove them at will drives \(\alpha_{S,\mathsf{J}}\) toward one.

Legitimation enters rule making through the rule of recognition, which Part IV defines (Long 2026d, Definition 2.2): rules count as law because officials and subjects accept criteria that identify them as law (Hart 1961). Rule makers seek legitimation through religious, ideological, and electoral claims, and they can also shape the information environment directly. Recent work argues that many contemporary autocrats rely more on the manipulation of information than on mass repression to sustain their hold on rule making (Guriev and Treisman 2019).

Civilian control of the coercive apparatus, the appointment of its commanders, and the rules that govern its use tie rule making to enforcement. Weber defined the state by its successful claim to a monopoly of legitimate force within a territory (Weber 1946), and the claim is made in rules. Part V analyzes the apparatus, including the coup-proofing strategies by which rulers divide it (Long 2026a, Proposition 6.4). This paper treats the appointment of security chiefs as part of the appointment channel and leaves coup-proofing and the conduct of policing to Part V.

The rules of work, taxation, and property, and the producers’ organizations that help to make them, connect rule making to production. Labor law, tax codes, and the legal status of unions are outputs of \(\mathsf{P}\) that set the terms on which the productive population works. In corporatist systems, organized labor and employers take part in drafting the rules that govern them, which raises \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{W}}\) by design and with the consent of the organized parties.

3 Necessity of the rule-making function

3.1 Assumptions of the lemma

The working axiom for this pillar in the series is plurality: members’ preferences diverge, yet collective choices are made. Part VII refines the full axiom set and proves the general theorem. Here the axiom is stated in the form needed for the rule-making lemma, together with two further assumptions that make its content explicit.

Assumption 10 (Plurality). The collective repeatedly faces collective decisions in the sense of Definition 1, and for each such decision the members’ preferences diverge over the alternatives: for every alternative \(x\in X\) some member strictly prefers another alternative \(y\in X\) to \(x\).

Assumption 11 (Environmental change). The environment \(e_t\in E\) of the collective varies over time. There is a viability correspondence \(\mathcal{V}:E\to 2^{\mathcal{R}}\), where \(\mathcal{V}(e)\) is the set of rule systems under which the collective survives a period in environment \(e\), and the realized path contains periods \(s_0<s_1<\dots<s_r\), with \(r\ge1\), such that consecutive viability sets are disjoint: \(\mathcal{V}(e_{s_{j-1}})\cap\mathcal{V}(e_{s_j})=\emptyset\) for \(j=1,\dots,r\).

Assumption 12 (Self-government and persistence). (i) The collective’s decisions and changes in its rules are produced by practices of its members rather than imposed from outside. (ii) The collective persists over a window \(T\) of consecutive periods that contains \(s_0\) and \(s_r\), so \(R_t\in\mathcal{V}(e_t)\) for all \(t\in T\).

The plurality assumption is empirical. It fails for a group whose members always agree on every collective choice, which no ethnography or history records for any group of more than a few people over any length of time. The change assumption formalizes the condition under which a regime of fixed rules becomes untenable. Hart called a regime of primary rules without a rule of change static and listed this as one of its three defects (Hart 1961); the assumption states when the defect becomes fatal rather than merely costly. The self-government clause excludes a group whose rules are set entirely by an outside power. Such a group does not falsify the necessity of rule making; it shows that the rule maker lies outside the boundary drawn around the group, and the boundary should be redrawn to include it.

Lemma 13 (Collective choice requires a procedure). Under Assumption 10 and clause (i) of Assumption 12, every realized collective decision is ranked below some other alternative by at least one member. The practice that produces the realized decision therefore selects among divergent preferences, and the collective implements part (a) of Definition 2.

Proof. Let \(x^\ast\) be the alternative realized in a collective decision. By Assumption 10 there is a member \(i\) and an alternative \(y\) with \(y\succ_i x^\ast\). Because the decision applies to all members at once, \(i\) cannot obtain \(y\) while others obtain \(x^\ast\). Some practice, whether a vote, a command, a discussion that ends when objectors fall silent, or a default to the status quo, determined that \(x^\ast\) rather than \(y\) would hold for everyone, and by clause (i) of Assumption 12 that practice is the members’ own. That practice selects among divergent preferences. Collective decisions recur by assumption, so such selections recur. ◻

Lemma 14 (Change requires a rule of change). Under Assumptions 11 and 12, the rule system is replaced at least \(r\) times on \(T\): for each \(j=1,\dots,r\) there is \(t\) with \(s_{j-1}\le t<s_j\) and \(R_{t+1}\neq R_t\). Each replacement is produced by a practice of the members.

Proof. Fix \(j\) and suppose \(R_t=R_{t+1}\) for every \(t\) with \(s_{j-1}\le t<s_j\). Then \(R_{s_{j-1}}=R_{s_j}=R\). Clause (ii) of Assumption 12 gives \(R\in\mathcal{V}(e_{s_{j-1}})\) and \(R\in\mathcal{V}(e_{s_j})\), which contradicts the disjointness in Assumption 11. So some \(t\) in \([s_{j-1},s_j)\) has \(R_{t+1}\neq R_t\), and both \(t\) and \(t+1\) lie in \(T\) because \(T\) consists of consecutive periods containing \(s_0\) and \(s_r\). The intervals \([s_{j-1},s_j)\) are disjoint, so the \(r\) replacements are distinct. By clause (i) of Assumption 12 each is produced by the members’ practices. ◻

Hart’s term for the practice whose existence Lemma 14 establishes is the rule of change. The series assigns this member of Hart’s triad of secondary rules to the rule-making pillar; the rule of adjudication belongs to Part III and the rule of recognition to Part IV (Definition 2.2 of that Part).

Definition 15 (Rule of change). A rule of change of a collective is a secondary rule, written or customary, that identifies who may introduce, alter, or repeal the collective’s general rules and by what procedure (Hart 1961). It is the rule-making procedure of Definition 2(b) considered as a rule accepted by the members rather than as a regularity observed from outside.

Theorem 16 (Necessity of rule making). A collective that satisfies Assumption 10 and clause (i) of Assumption 12 implements the rule-making function in sense (a). If it also satisfies Assumptions 11 and 12, it implements the function in sense (b), with at least \(r\) replacements of the rule system on \(T\). Whenever a replacement is chosen from the viable set \(\mathcal{V}(e_{t+1})\) and members’ preferences diverge over that set in the sense of Definition 1, the replacement is itself a selection among divergent preferences.

Proof. The first statement is Lemma 13. The second is Lemma 14. For the third, apply Lemma 13 to the choice of \(R_{t+1}\) from \(\mathcal{V}(e_{t+1})\), treating the choice of a rule system as a collective decision. ◻

The proofs are short because the work is done by the assumptions. This is the correct place for the work to be done. The theorem asserts that an activity must occur, not that an institution must exist. It would be false if a persistent collective could make its binding choices without any practice that selects among divergent preferences, or if it could survive incompatible environments without changing its rules. Each of these is an empirical possibility that the hard cases below test.

3.2 Scope of the lemma

The theorem makes \(\mathsf{P}\) a thin predicate, and a thin predicate invites the charge of vacuity. If anything that happens counts as rule making, the necessity claim is empty. Three things are excluded.

A collective decision made by a fixed external rule (a lottery imposed by an outside authority, or a decision by the environment itself, such as a drought that forces a move) is excluded by the self-government clause. A rule change that is purely exogenous, such as the imposition of colonial law on a subject people, is excluded for the same reason and relocates the rule maker rather than eliminating it. Most importantly, the predicate excludes a group whose rules never change while the environment does. The falsifier of the theorem is therefore specific: a long-lived group, satisfying the plurality assumption, whose rules never changed and could not be changed by any of its members, through a sequence of environments that no single rule system could survive.

The theorem says nothing about how rule making is distributed, how it is legitimated, or whether it is accountable. Corollary 6 shows that the same function can be performed with concentration anywhere from \(1/n\) to \(1\).

3.3 Minimal form in stateless and consensus societies

The societies most often cited as lacking government are the test set for the theorem. If rule making is necessary, each should show it in some informal, distributed form. If any shows no rule-making activity at all, the theorem is in trouble.

Among the Ju/’hoansi of the Kalahari, camp-level decisions about movement, sharing, and conflict are reached through extended public talk in which adults participate and no one has authority to command (Lee 2013). Wiessner’s analysis of recorded conversations shows that much of this talk consists of criticism and joking directed at people who violate norms of sharing and modesty (Wiessner 2005). When disagreement cannot be resolved, people move to another camp. Fission is a rule-change practice of a particular kind: it does not change the rules of the existing group but creates a new group whose rules differ.

Evans-Pritchard described the Nuer as an “ordered anarchy” with no government in the sense of a political authority able to command (Evans-Pritchard 1940). Their political order rested on the balanced opposition of lineage segments. Rules about bridewealth, compensation, and the use of grazing were maintained and adjusted through negotiation among kin groups, through the settlements of disputes, and through the influence of persons whose standing rested on persuasion rather than command. The leopard-skin chief who mediated in cases of homicide belongs to the interpretive pillar and is treated in Part III.

Clastres argued from Amazonian ethnography that the chief in many lowland South American societies had no power to command, that his speech was an obligation rather than an instrument of rule, and that a chief who tried to give orders would be abandoned (Clastres 1987). Clastres’s thesis is that such societies actively prevent political power from separating from the group. The thesis is consistent with the necessity theorem. Decisions are still made, by the group, and the chief’s oratory is part of how they are made.

Boehm’s survey of foraging societies found a reverse dominance hierarchy: the adult members, acting as a coalition, suppress any individual who tries to dominate, using ridicule, disobedience, ostracism, and in extreme cases killing (Boehm 1999). Boehm emphasizes that egalitarianism of this kind is a political achievement. The group decides collectively and enforces the decision against would-be leaders. Woodburn reached a compatible conclusion for immediate-return societies such as the Hadza: their equality is produced by institutions of leveling that prevent the accumulation of power and property (Woodburn 1982).

Ostrom’s study of long-lived common-pool resource institutions supplies a different kind of case: small communities that govern a shared resource without a state and without private property (Ostrom 1990). Among the design principles she identified is that most individuals affected by the operational rules can participate in modifying them. The principle is a rule-making arrangement in the exact sense of Definition 2(b). Reviews of later case studies have found broad support for the principles (Cox et al. 2010), and Ostrom’s later work describes how such arrangements nest within larger polycentric systems (Ostrom 2010).

Proposition 17 (Minimal form of rule making). Let \(N_a\subseteq N\) be the adult members of a group, \(|N_a|=n_a\ge2\), and let the group make collective decisions by consensus, modeled as unanimity among the adults (a proposal wins exactly when every member of \(N_a\) assents), with the status quo \(q\) as default. Let each adult \(i\) have a strict linear order \(\succ_i\) over alternatives and assent to a proposal \(x\) against \(q\) if and only if \(x\succ_i q\). Then:

  1. the de jure concentration of rule making is \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/n_a\), the minimum possible for a procedure in which only adults’ consent counts, and the minimum for the whole group when \(N_a=N\);

  2. the set of alternatives that can replace \(q\) is the set of strict Pareto improvements for the adults, \(\{x: x\succ_i q \text{ for all } i\in N_a\}\), which is empty whenever \(q\) is Pareto optimal among them;

  3. let the group be able to split, with each subgroup \(N_1\subseteq N_a\) of at least two adults deciding by consensus for itself. A subgroup can replace \(q\) if and only if all its members strictly prefer a common alternative \(x\) to \(q\), so some subgroup can do so if and only if at least two adults strictly prefer a common alternative to \(q\). This condition can hold when \(q\) is Pareto optimal among all adults.

Proof. (a) The procedure is the unanimity game on \(K=N_a\), so Proposition 5(d) gives \(\varphi_i=1/n_a\) for adults, \(\varphi_i=0\) for others, and \(H^{\mathsf{P}}=1/n_a\). Any procedure in which only adults can be the pivot has shares summing to one over \(N_a\), so Proposition 5(b) applied to \(N_a\) shows that \(1/n_a\) is the minimum. (b) Under unanimity an alternative replaces \(q\) only if every adult prefers it to \(q\), which is the definition of a strict Pareto improvement among the adults. If \(q\) is Pareto optimal among them, no alternative is strictly preferred to \(q\) by every adult, so the set is empty. (c) Apply (b) to \(N_1\): its consensus winset is \(\{x: x\succ_i q\text{ for all }i\in N_1\}\), which is nonempty exactly when its members share an alternative they all prefer to \(q\). If two adults \(i,j\) both prefer some \(x\) to \(q\), the subgroup \(\{i,j\}\) qualifies; conversely any qualifying subgroup contains two such adults. For the last claim, take three adults with \(x\succ_1 q\), \(x\succ_2 q\), and \(q\succ_3 y\) for every \(y\neq q\): then \(q\) is Pareto optimal among all three, yet \(\{1,2\}\) can adopt \(x\). ◻

The proposition gives a formal reason for an ethnographic regularity. Consensus produces the least concentrated rule making possible, and it also produces the most status-quo-biased rule making possible, because every adult is a veto player. A consensus society facing a changing environment therefore needs another way to change its rules when no change is a Pareto improvement. Fission supplies it. The pairing of consensus decision making with cheap exit, documented among the Ju/’hoansi and in Woodburn’s immediate-return societies, is what Proposition 17 predicts. In the vocabulary of Definition 7, such groups combine minimal concentration with very high exit and entry components of contestability.

Formal consensus sets only a lower bound on the concentration of rule making in these societies. Even where every adult can block a decision, differences in age, gender, skill, and kinship give some speakers more weight in discussion than others, so actual concentration exceeds the de jure figure \(1/n_a\). The equal-share coding describes the procedure, not the distribution of influence within it.

Across the ethnographic and historical record, collective decisions are made and rules change, while the concentration of rule making varies. Graeber and Wengrow argue that prehistoric societies moved deliberately between egalitarian and hierarchical arrangements, sometimes by season (Graeber and Wengrow 2021). Scott describes upland peoples of Southeast Asia who kept rule making dispersed to avoid incorporation by lowland states (Scott 2009), and early states in which it became concentrated (Scott 2017). Both readings are disputed, and the record itself overrepresents groups observed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many in marginal environments and in contact with states, among foragers who vary widely (Kelly 1995). None of these qualifications touches the pattern that matters here: what varies is who makes the rules, not whether they are made.

3.4 Custom without legislators

The strongest objection to the theorem is not a stateless society but an argument about how rules come into being. Hayek distinguished law that grows out of practice, discovered and articulated rather than made, from legislation deliberately enacted by an authority, and he argued that much of the order of a society rests on the former (Hayek 1973). Ellickson’s study of cattle ranchers in Shasta County, California, showed that neighbors settled trespass disputes by informal norms that diverged from the formal law and that the norms adjusted over time without recourse to it (Ellickson 1991). If rules can evolve without anyone making them, the rule-making function may be unnecessary.

The objection narrows the theorem but does not defeat it. The theorem requires a practice that selects collective decisions and replaces rules, not a legislator. Evolved custom changes through practices: some members deviate, others imitate or sanction the deviation, and social pressure settles on a new regularity. Ellickson’s ranchers are rule makers in this distributed sense. The norms change because particular neighbors propose, test, and enforce new expectations through gossip, reciprocity, and occasional self-help. The function is performed; the office is absent.

The objection still shows something important. Under customary rule change, the rule-making profile of Definition 2 is diffuse and difficult to observe, and control shares cannot be read from a written procedure. The power-index baseline does not apply, and concentration must be estimated from who in fact initiates and enforces changes. This is a measurement limitation of the paper’s framework, not a counterexample to the theorem.

3.5 Functional explanation and its limits

The theorem is a statement about the conditions under which a collective persists. It is not a functional explanation of why any particular rule-making institution exists. Hempel showed that an inference from the need for a function to the presence of a particular institution commits the fallacy of affirming the consequent, because functional equivalents can meet the same need (Hempel 1959). Elster argued that explaining an institution by its beneficial consequences requires a feedback mechanism through which the consequences maintain the institution (Elster 1983). The theorem respects both constraints. It claims the necessity of an activity, it allows any institution or none to perform it, and it does not explain the existence of the Parlement of Paris, the Politburo, or the Majles by their contribution to the persistence of their polities. Those institutions have histories, and the comparative section treats them as historical products whose relation to the function is a matter of evidence.

4 Literature on rule-making power

4.1 Elite, pluralist and structural theories

The oldest question about rule making is whether a minority always makes the rules. Mosca held that in every society an organized minority rules an unorganized majority, and that the minority’s advantage lies in its organization rather than in its numbers (Mosca 1939). Michels drew the same conclusion from the internal life of the German Social Democratic Party: organization requires delegation, delegation creates leaders with expertise and control of communication, and leaders entrench themselves even in organizations committed to democracy (Michels 1915). Mills extended the elite argument to the postwar United States, describing an interlocking set of corporate, military, and political leaders (Mills 1956).

Pluralists denied that the elite claim had been demonstrated. Dahl’s study of New Haven found that different actors prevailed in different issue areas and that no single group controlled decisions across them (Dahl 1961). His later account of polyarchy defined democracy by two dimensions, the inclusiveness of participation and the extent of public contestation (Dahl 1971). These two dimensions anticipate the separation in this paper between the distribution of rule-making shares and the contestability of rule making.

A third position holds that rule makers need not be controlled by a class to serve it. Lindblom argued that business occupies a privileged position in market societies because governments depend on business investment for economic performance, so that rule makers anticipate business reactions without being directed (Lindblom 1977). Winters defined oligarchy as the political use of concentrated wealth for wealth defense and showed that oligarchs can coexist with electoral democracy (Winters 2011). Structural arguments of this kind imply that integration between resource control and rule making, \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{P}}\), can be high even when no capture link in the source’s sense can be documented. They therefore pose a measurement problem for the framework that Part I examines in detail.

The empirical debate has moved to policy responsiveness. Gilens and Page estimated, on a dataset of 1,779 policy questions, that the preferences of economic elites and organized business groups have substantial independent associations with policy outcomes in the United States while the preferences of average citizens have little (Gilens and Page 2014). Bartels found that senators’ votes were more responsive to affluent constituents than to poor ones (Bartels 2008). Branham, Soroka, and Wlezien found that the preferences of the affluent and the middle class usually coincide and that, when they diverge, the affluent prevail only modestly more often (Branham et al. 2017). Elkjaer and Klitgaard review the comparative evidence on unequal responsiveness and its methods (Elkjaer and Klitgaard 2024). The disagreement concerns the size and the generality of a bias, not its direction in the American case.

4.2 Faces of power and agenda control

Bachrach and Baratz argued that the pluralist method of studying observable decisions misses the power exercised by keeping issues off the agenda (Bachrach and Baratz 1962). Lukes added a third dimension, the shaping of preferences so that conflicts of interest never surface as grievances (Lukes 2005). For rule making the second face is central and measurable in principle: a non-decision leaves traces in proposals that were drafted and never scheduled, in petitions that went unanswered, and in committee chairs who declined to act. The third face shades into the legitimation pillar and is left to Part IV.

Culpepper’s study of corporate control in Europe and Japan gives the agenda argument an empirical form. Business influence over the rules of corporate governance was greatest on issues of low public salience, where legislators relied on managers’ expertise and the press paid little attention (Culpepper 2010). Hall and Deardorff model lobbying as a subsidy of information and labor given to legislators who already agree with the lobbyist (Hall and Deardorff 2006). On this view, organized interests shape rules mainly by shaping what allied legislators can do with limited time, not by changing their votes.

4.3 Rule making in autocracies

Theories of autocracy explain the survival of rulers by how they share rule-making power with a narrow group of insiders. In selectorate theory, leaders must retain a winning coalition drawn from a larger selectorate, and when that coalition is small they survive by supplying private goods to its members rather than public goods to all (Bueno de Mesquita et al. 2003). Svolik locates the main threat to dictators among the elites who share power with them and shows how parties, legislatures, and rules of succession make that sharing credible (Svolik 2012). Gandhi and Przeworski and Magaloni reach compatible conclusions: nominally democratic institutions and ruling parties co-opt potential rivals and are associated with longer rule (Gandhi and Przeworski 2007; Magaloni 2008). Gerschewski adds legitimation and repression to co-optation as the supports of autocratic stability (Gerschewski 2013), which in the series’ vocabulary brings the legitimation and enforcement pillars to the aid of rule makers. The regime classification of Geddes, Wright, and Frantz makes these claims testable across party, personalist, military, and monarchic autocracies (Geddes et al. 2014).

Hale describes post-Soviet politics as patronal: power runs through networks of personal reward and punishment, organized either as a single pyramid around one patron or as competing pyramids (Hale 2014). Levitsky and Way identify competitive authoritarian regimes in which elections are real but the field is uneven (Levitsky and Way 2010). These categories matter for measurement because they describe regimes whose formal rule-making procedures are pluralistic and whose actual concentration is high.

4.4 Separation of powers and veto players

Montesquieu argued that liberty requires that the power to make law, the power to execute it, and the power to judge not be united in the same hands (Montesquieu 1989). Madison’s defense of the proposed American constitution relied on the same idea in a different form: “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition” (Madison 1788). The constitutional design he defended gave each branch the means and the motive to resist encroachment by the others.

Tsebelis generalized the idea. A veto player is an individual or collective actor whose agreement is required for a change of the status quo, and the set of policies that can replace the status quo shrinks as veto players are added or as their positions diverge (Tsebelis 2002). Lijphart contrasts majoritarian democracies, which concentrate rule making in the cabinet of a single-party majority, with consensus democracies that disperse it among coalition partners, two legislative chambers, federal units, and courts (Lijphart 1999). Linz argued that presidential systems, which give the executive and the legislature separate mandates, make conflicts between them harder to resolve (Linz 1990). Weingast showed that constitutional limits on rule makers are self-enforcing only when citizens can coordinate their reactions to transgressions (Weingast 1997).

4.5 Sources of rule-making authority

Weber classified legitimate domination as traditional, charismatic, or rational-legal according to the grounds on which subjects accept commands (Weber 1978). Each regime type in the comparison below claims rule-making authority on a different ground: divine right and dynastic tradition in the Catholic monarchies (Figgis 1914; Kantorowicz 1957), divine law administered by rulers and jurists in the Islamic states (Crone 2004; Lambton 1981), the historical mission of a vanguard party in the communist states (Kornai 1992), and popular sovereignty expressed in elections in the democracies (Dahl 1971). Bodin made the power to give law to all without the consent of others the first mark of sovereignty (Bodin 1992). Al-Mawardi set out the conditions under which an imam is validly appointed and the powers he may delegate to viziers, governors, and judges (al-Mawardi 1996). These are theories written from inside regimes, and they describe what rule makers claimed, not necessarily what they could do.

4.6 Relation to prior work

Three gaps in this literature motivate the present paper. The theories of democratic and autocratic rule making use different concepts, so comparisons across regime types tend to be either impressionistic or confined to a single dimension such as regime survival. The functional-prerequisite tradition in sociology asserted that every society must make collective decisions (Aberle et al. 1950; Parsons 1951) but did not separate the necessity of the function from the distribution of its control, and it was criticized for that reason. The source framework of the series (Largo 2026) supplies a common network vocabulary but merges rule making with adjudication and leaves the measurement of control shares open. This paper separates rule making from adjudication, gives control shares a de jure baseline in power indices, proves that necessity and concentration are logically independent for this pillar, and uses one set of questions for all six regime types.

5 Rule making across six regime types

5.1 Comparative method

Each regime type is examined for the same five features: the institutions that hold the rule-making function, the concentration of control in the sense of \(H^{\mathsf{P}}\) and its contestability in the components of Definition 7, the strength of its coupling to the other five pillars, the arrangements that preserve accountability, and the documented capture episodes and effective checks. The answers are qualitative. Terms such as “high” or “low” concentration refer to the ordering implied by Proposition 5 applied to the described procedure, adjusted for documented informal concentration, and they are not estimates of \(H^{\mathsf{P}}\) for any polity.

The six types are analytical lenses, not mutually exclusive categories. A single country can be a market-capitalist society and a secular democracy at once, and the two lenses ask different questions of it. The market-capitalist lens examines how private economic resources reach rule making. The secular-democratic lens examines how rule making is distributed among elected officials, courts, independent bodies, and private institutions under a constitution. Islamic states and Catholic monarchies are treated as polities in which religious law or religious institutions supplied a source of political legitimacy, in particular historical states with large internal differences. Neither religion is treated as an essence, and no regime type is ranked above another.

5.2 Market-capitalist societies

In market-capitalist societies with electoral institutions, general rules are made by elected legislatures, by executives through decrees and regulations, and by administrative agencies under delegated authority. The United States, the most studied case, divides rule making among two legislative chambers, a president with a veto, federal agencies that issue regulations, and fifty states. The formal procedure has several veto players, so the de jure concentration of rule making is moderate, and its formal contestability is high on all five components of Definition 7.

The question this lens asks is how private economic resources enter that procedure. The source framework describes a path from unequal private resources through advocacy and access to the specialized development of policy and the distribution of legal benefits (Largo 2026, Section 8). Several links in that path are documented. A randomized field experiment found that when a political organization revealed that attendees at requested meetings were campaign donors, congressional offices were more likely to arrange meetings with senior staff or the member (Kalla and Broockman 2016). Lobbyists who had worked for a senator saw their lobbying revenue fall when that senator left office, which indicates that part of what they sold was access to a particular rule maker (Blanes i Vidal et al. 2012). Lobbyists also follow the legislators to whom they are connected when those legislators change committee assignments (Bertrand et al. 2014). These findings concern access. They show privileged access, the first condition of a capture link, but they do not by themselves show that the access changed a rule.

Across ninety-eight lobbying issues, the status quo usually prevailed and the resources of a side predicted its policy success only weakly (Baumgartner et al. 2009). Evidence that access changes rules is therefore weaker than the evidence of access itself, and it is contested: Gilens and Page’s estimates (Gilens and Page 2014) are disputed by work that finds the preferences of affluent and middle-income citizens usually coincide (Branham et al. 2017). Ansolabehere, de Figueiredo, and Snyder observed that campaign contributions are small relative to the value of the policies at stake, which is hard to reconcile with a market in which rules are bought (Ansolabehere et al. 2003). The theory of economic regulation predicted that concentrated interests would capture the rules that govern them (Stigler 1971), and Peltzman generalized it to a regulator who balances the support of several groups (Peltzman 1976); Part I develops the capture literature (Long 2026b).

Rule making in this regime type is coupled most strongly to resource control. The coupling runs through the wealth-to-rules path described above and through the structural dependence of rule makers on investment that Lindblom described (Lindblom 1977). Hacker and Pierson argue that organized interests used this path to shift tax and regulatory rules toward the very wealthy over several decades (Hacker and Pierson 2010), and Culpepper’s comparison of Europe and Japan shows that business influence is greatest when issues are technical and receive little public attention (Culpepper 2010). Coupling to interpretation is weaker by design: federal judges are appointed by the president with Senate confirmation, so no single coalition controls the appointment channel unless it holds both bodies.

Elections, opposition rights, judicial review, disclosure rules, and competition among organized interests, which frequently lobby on opposite sides of the same question (Baumgartner et al. 2009), keep this rule making contestable. The regime type thus combines moderate de jure concentration and high formal contestability with unequal practical access, and the open question is how much that access changes rules.

5.3 Communist party-states

In communist party-states, the ruling party makes the rules that matter and the state legislature ratifies them. In the Soviet Union the Politburo of the Central Committee decided major questions, the Central Committee apparatus prepared and supervised policy, party congresses ratified the line, and the Supreme Soviet enacted laws whose content had been determined elsewhere (Fainsod 1953; Hough and Fainsod 1979). Kornai treats the undivided power of the communist party as the defining feature of the classical socialist system, from which its economic institutions follow (Kornai 1992). The de jure concentration of rule making is high, and every component of contestability except transparency of some rule texts is low for actors outside the party.

The appointment channel is the defining instrument. Party committees at each level held lists of posts, the nomenklatura, whose holders they appointed or approved, covering the leadership of ministries, enterprises, courts, newspapers, universities, and the armed forces (Hough and Fainsod 1979). In the notation of Definition 8, the party’s appointment share \(\alpha_{S,\ell}\) approached one in every pillar. Voslensky, a former Soviet official writing in emigration, described the nomenklatura as a ruling class (Voslensky 1984), and Djilas, a Yugoslav dissident, called the party bureaucracy a new class that held collective ownership through political control (Djilas 1957). Both accounts come from authors with strong political commitments and should be read alongside the institutional scholarship. Hough and Fainsod described a system of bureaucratic bargaining among ministries and regions, and Lane and Ross found significant divisions within the Soviet ruling elite of 1981 to 1991 (Lane and Ross 1995). These findings bear on the loyalty of appointees, a parameter formalized in Section 6 (Proposition 22): appointment by a single party does not guarantee that appointees act as one coalition.

The People’s Republic of China retains the Leninist structure with important changes. Shirk described the relation between the party’s top leaders and the Central Committee as reciprocal accountability: leaders appoint the members of the committee that formally selects them (Shirk 1993). Landry showed how the party’s control of the appointment and evaluation of local cadres allowed it to decentralize economic decisions without losing political control (Landry 2008). Minzner documented a turn after the early 2000s away from legal professionalization and toward mediation and political direction of the courts (Minzner 2011), which raises the coupling \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\).

Coupling to the other pillars is high by design in all four pairs treated here: party direction of courts, of propaganda and education, of the security services, and of official trade unions organized under party leadership (Kornai 1992). Accountability operates mainly within the party, through collective leadership, factional bargaining, and rules of succession, which Svolik identifies as the institutions that make power sharing among elites credible (Svolik 2012). The documented checks are intra-elite. In 1964 the Soviet party leadership removed Khrushchev as first secretary through the Presidium and the Central Committee, a replacement of the top rule maker carried out entirely inside the party (Hough and Fainsod 1979). Svolik’s cross-national evidence points the same way: most authoritarian leaders who lose office by nonconstitutional means are removed by regime insiders rather than by popular uprisings (Svolik 2012). The Soviet case also shows the limits of high integration: the party’s control of every pillar did not prevent the divisions that Lane and Ross document or the collapse of the system at the end of the period they study.

5.4 Social democracy and democratic socialism

In social democracies, rules are made by parliaments in which strong programmatic parties compete, and many economic and social rules are negotiated with organized labor and employers before they reach parliament. Sweden is the standard case. The Social Democratic Workers’ Party governed for most of the twentieth century, and its close tie to the national trade union confederation gave organized labor direct access to rule making (Esping-Andersen 1985). Korpi’s power-resources theory treats the strength of labor’s organization as the main determinant of the redistributive content of rules in capitalist democracies (Korpi 1983). De jure concentration of rule making is moderate, because rule making requires parliamentary majorities and coalition partners, and contestability is high on every component.

Corporatist bargaining is the first feature that distinguishes this regime type in the rule-making layer. Peak organizations of labor and capital take part in drafting rules about wages, working conditions, and social insurance, which raises the coupling \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{W}}\) by design. Within the domains it covers, a tripartite bargain among the government, the labor confederation, and the employers’ confederation is a unanimity procedure among three organs, so by Proposition 5(d) the organ-level concentration of rule making in those domains is \(1/3\). The same arrangement creates a risk of closure: interests that are not organized into the peak associations, such as the unemployed or new categories of workers, may be excluded from rule making that formally includes all producers.

The long tenure of a single party is the second feature. Prolonged dominance of rule making by one party in a competitive system is concentration over time, which the static index \(H^{\mathsf{P}}\) does not capture.

Przeworski described the strategic dilemma of electoral socialism: to win majorities, workers’ parties must appeal beyond the working class, and to govern a market economy they depend on the investment decisions of private owners (Przeworski 1985). Pontusson’s study of investment politics in Sweden showed that proposals to extend collective control over investment met strong resistance from business and were scaled back (Pontusson 1992). In the series’ vocabulary, the structural dependence of rule makers on capital constrained the rules that social-democratic majorities could make. Kitschelt traced how European social-democratic parties changed their programs as their electorates changed (Kitschelt 1994), and Berman argued that the social-democratic movement was defined by a belief in the primacy of political choice over market outcomes (Berman 2006).

Accountability rests on competitive elections, which did remove social-democratic governments, on an independent civil service, and on the formal openness of the corporatist channels. Rothstein’s study of Swedish labor-market and school policy argues that the bureaucratic design of implementation, not only legislative majorities, determined whether social-democratic reforms achieved their goals (Rothstein 1996). Huber and Stephens show that long periods of social-democratic government were associated with the expansion of welfare states and that the effects persisted after the parties lost office (Huber and Stephens 2001). The documented check on concentration in this regime type is alternation in government combined with corporatist channels that remain open to opposing organized interests, and the documented risk is closure of rule making to the unorganized.

5.5 Islamic states

In polities that derived political legitimacy from Islamic law, the question of who makes the rules has had an answer that differs from the European cases in one structural respect. Much of the general law, the fiqh developed from the Quran and the Sunna, was elaborated by jurists who were not officials of the ruler. Crone and Hinds argue that the early caliphs claimed religious authority in their own right and that this authority passed over the first centuries of Islam to the scholars, the ulama (Crone and Hinds 1986). Rulers continued to make rules in the domains of administration, taxation, and public order, but they did not control the content of the law in the way that Bodin attributed to the sovereign. Hallaq argues that the modern state, with its claim to a monopoly of legislation, cannot reproduce this moral and legal order (Hallaq 2013). The argument is contested; the difference in the distribution of rule-making shares that it identifies is not.

Classical theory placed the imam, or caliph, at the head of the community. Al-Mawardi’s treatise sets out the qualifications of the imam, the ways in which he is validly appointed, including selection by qualified electors, and the powers he may delegate (al-Mawardi 1996). Lambton and Crone describe a long gap between this theory and practice, in which military rulers held effective power while caliphs conferred legitimacy and jurists accommodated the arrangement (Lambton 1981; Crone 2004). The Ottoman Empire added a body of sultanic regulation, the qanun, alongside sharia. Imber’s study of the sixteenth-century chief mufti Ebussuud shows a senior jurist harmonizing sultanic law with Hanafi jurisprudence, which places a religious official inside the rule-making process of the empire (Imber 1997). Barkey describes Ottoman rule as the brokered management of diverse intermediaries rather than direct command (Barkey 2008).

Two modern states show how differently the legitimating source can be institutionalized. The constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran, adopted in 1979 and revised in 1989, vests leadership of the community in a just and pious jurist in the absence of the Twelfth Imam, following Khomeini’s doctrine of the guardianship of the jurist (Islamic Republic of Iran 1989; Khomeini 1981). The Leader is selected by an elected Assembly of Experts and sets the general policies of the state. Legislation passed by the elected Majles must be sent to the Guardian Council, whose jurists judge its compatibility with Islam, and disputes between the two bodies go to the Expediency Council (Islamic Republic of Iran 1989; Schirazi 1997). Rule making in Iran therefore combines electoral and appointed components, and its de jure concentration is moderate to high depending on how the Leader’s appointment power is weighted. Factions and parallel institutions compete within this structure (Brumberg 2001; Buchta 2000).

Saudi rule making is concentrated in form and fragmented in practice. The Basic Law of Governance of 1992 declares the system of government monarchical, states that the regime derives its power from the Quran and the Sunna, provides that laws are issued and amended by royal decree, and gives the King the power to dissolve and re-form the consultative council (Kingdom of Saudi Arabia 1992). The state rests on the alliance between the Al Saud and the religious establishment descended from the eighteenth-century Wahhabi movement (Commins 2006; Al-Rasheed 2002). Yet Hertog shows that rule making in the oil era was divided among princes, brokers, and bureaucratic fiefdoms rather than centralized (Hertog 2010).

In both modern cases rule making is closely coupled to legitimation and interpretation, and each case has one further strong coupling. In Iran the coupling to enforcement runs through the Revolutionary Guards, whose economic and political role is debated (Alfoneh 2013). In Saudi Arabia the coupling to resource control runs through the allocation of oil rents (Gause 1994); whether oil revenue itself weakens accountability is disputed (Ross 2001).

Whether religious jurists raised or lowered the contestability of rule making is the question on which the scholarship divides. Jurists with authority independent of rulers raised the oversight component, while jurists co-opted by rulers could lower the entry component by excluding rival law makers. Kuran attributes the economic divergence of the Middle East to particular institutions of Islamic law, and Kuru attributes authoritarianism and underdevelopment in Muslim-majority states to an alliance between the ulama and the state (Kuran 2011; Kuru 2019); Rubin explains the divergence instead by the terms on which rulers bargained with religious authorities for legitimacy (Rubin 2017). Doctrine permits both arrangements. Shia jurists have held positions ranging from political quietism to the activism of Khomeini’s doctrine (Arjomand 1988), and Sunni theory accommodated military rulers alongside autonomous jurists (Lambton 1981).

Checks on rule makers are documented throughout the type. In the classical period the jurists’ authority over much of the law limited what rulers could legislate, Ottoman and Saudi rule rested on negotiation with intermediaries, and Iranian elections remain contested within the limits set by candidate screening. Religious legitimation also constrains the rulers who depend on it: militant challenges to the Saudi state show that religious actors can turn the ruler’s own ground of legitimacy against the ruler (Hegghammer 2010).

5.6 Catholic monarchies

In the Catholic monarchies of early modern Europe, the king was in theory the source of law. Bodin made the power to legislate without the consent of others the mark of sovereignty (Bodin 1992), and Bossuet grounded royal authority in Scripture (Bossuet 1990). Kantorowicz traced the medieval idea that the king had a mortal natural body and an undying political body (Kantorowicz 1957), and Figgis described the theory of divine right as it developed in early modern Europe (Figgis 1914). In formal terms, rule making was concentrated in one person, close to the dictatorship case of Proposition 5(d).

In practice, Catholic monarchs shared rule making with councils, estates, and courts that could delay or block royal decrees. Habsburg Spain was a composite monarchy whose kingdoms kept their own laws and representative assemblies, and royal government worked through separate councils for kingdoms and functions (Elliott 1963). When Olivares tried in the 1620s and 1630s to make the kingdoms share the costs of the monarchy’s wars, the resistance contributed to the revolts of Catalonia and Portugal in 1640 (Elliott 1963; Elliott 1984), a documented check on what the crown could impose. In Bourbon France the Estates-General did not meet between 1614 and 1789, yet royal ordinances still had to be registered by the Parlements, which could remonstrate and delay unless the king overrode them in a formal session in person. Beik’s study of Languedoc shows absolutism working through collaboration with provincial elites who shared in its revenues (Beik 1985), and Collins presents the French state as a negotiated structure with real limits (Collins 1995). The crown’s reach was nonetheless real: Root shows royal rules on village finances reshaping relations between peasants and lords in Burgundy (Root 1987).

The Papal States combined spiritual and temporal rule making in one office. The pope was elected by the cardinals, so the Papal States were an elective monarchy, and Prodi describes the early modern papacy as a single body with two souls, the head of the universal Church and the prince of a territorial state (Prodi 1987). Rule making in the Papal States was concentrated in the pope and the curial congregations, and the coupling between rule making and legitimation was complete by construction.

Rule making in these monarchies was coupled to the other pillars through the Church and through office. Crowns controlled appointments to high Church office in their territories, which raised \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{I}}\); the Gallican tradition in France asserted royal and episcopal liberties against Rome, and Van Kley traces how disputes over Jansenism drew the Parlements into conflict with both crown and papacy (Van Kley 1996). Venality of judicial and financial offices coupled rule making to resource control and to interpretation, because offices were property that the crown sold and whose holders could resist the crown. Ertman classifies France and Spain as patrimonial absolutist states, in which office holding and fiscal administration were tied to private interests (Ertman 1997).

Accountability did not take the form of replacement of rule makers, which the dynastic principle excluded, but of the corporate privileges of estates, towns, Parlements, and the Church, of the Church’s autonomous canon law, and, in extreme cases, of revolt. The de jure concentration was high, and practical concentration was lower because of brokerage; contestability was low in replacement and entry and intermittently significant in oversight through the Parlements and estates.

5.7 Secular democracies

In secular democracies, the authority to make rules is derived from popular sovereignty and distributed by a constitution among elected legislatures, executives, courts with powers of review, independent agencies, subnational governments, and in some cases the voters directly. The distribution varies widely. Lijphart’s comparison of thirty-six democracies distinguishes majoritarian systems, in which a single-party cabinet controls rule making between elections, from consensus systems that disperse it (Lijphart 1999). Federal systems add a territorial division, giving subnational governments rule-making authority that the national legislature cannot revoke by ordinary statute. Presidential systems add a division between an executive and a legislature with separate electoral mandates and fixed terms, which Linz argued makes conflicts between them harder to resolve (Linz 1990). Where constitutions provide for referendums and popular initiatives, the electorate itself becomes a veto player or an agenda setter in the rule-making procedure (Lijphart 1999). In the vocabulary of this paper, majoritarian systems have higher de jure concentration and fewer veto players; consensus systems have lower concentration and more veto players, and the trade-off between them is the subject of Proposition 21 in Section 6.

Contestability is the defining property of this regime type. Dahl’s polyarchy requires both inclusive participation and public contestation (Dahl 1971). Przeworski characterized democracy as a system in which parties can lose elections and accept the loss, so that outcomes are uncertain in advance (Przeworski 1991). The replacement component \(q^{\mathrm{rep}}\) is therefore the component that distinguishes democracies from the other regime types most sharply. Data projects such as V-Dem measure it alongside legislative and judicial constraints on the executive and the freedom to form parties (Coppedge et al. 2011), and the Regimes of the World classification uses these measures to separate liberal and electoral democracies from electoral and closed autocracies (Lührmann et al. 2018).

Secular democracies have relocated part of rule making away from elected bodies. Constitutional courts in Europe shape legislation by reviewing it, and Stone Sweet shows that legislators anticipate review and draft accordingly (Stone Sweet 2000). Hirschl argues that the transfer of power to courts in several democracies served elites who expected to lose control of legislatures (Hirschl 2004). Independent agencies and central banks make rules under delegated authority. Each of these relocations lowers the concentration of rule making in elected bodies and raises its concentration in bodies that are not removable by voters, and whether the result is more or less accountable depends on the oversight component, not on the relocation itself.

The documented capture risk in this regime type is the erosion of contestability by elected rule makers. Levitsky and Way describe regimes that hold real elections while incumbents abuse state resources, media access, and legal harassment to tilt the field (Levitsky and Way 2010). In such regimes the formal rule-making procedure is unchanged while the replacement and entry components of contestability fall. The documented checks are the ones Madison anticipated, rival institutions with the means and motive to resist (Madison 1788), together with the coordination of citizens against transgressions that Weingast identifies as the condition for constitutional limits to hold (Weingast 1997). Pettit’s republican account treats the contestability of rule making by those subject to it as the core of freedom from domination (Pettit 1997), which supplies a normative ground for the measurement emphasis of Definition 7.

6 Mechanisms, failure modes and checks

The necessity theorem says that rule making occurs; Proposition B concerns the conditions under which it becomes concentrated, coupled to other pillars, and unaccountable. Each mechanism below is a comparative static under explicit assumptions, not a law of history, and each identifies the check that interrupts it, in line with the source’s proposition that checks can interrupt the transmission of concentrated power (Largo 2026, Proposition 2).

6.1 Delegation and oligarchy

Michels argued that the leaders of large organizations escape the control of members because members lack the time, expertise, and information to monitor them (Michels 1915). Olson’s analysis of collective action explains why: the benefit of monitoring is shared by all members, while its cost falls on the one who monitors (Olson 1965). A simple model makes the conditional character of the argument precise.

Assumption 18 (Monitoring game). An organization has \(n\ge2\) members and a leadership that extracts a rent \(\rho>0\) from the organization in each period, at a cost of \(\rho/n\) to each member. Each member chooses simultaneously whether to monitor the leadership at private cost \(c>0\). Each monitor independently detects the extraction with probability \(\delta\in(0,1]\), and detection leads to the replacement of the leadership. After replacement the rent is returned to the members, so each member recovers \(\rho/n\). A member’s payoff is the expected rent recovered by that member minus the member’s own monitoring cost.

Proposition 19 (Monitoring vanishes in large organizations). Under Assumption 18:

  1. if \(n>\rho\delta/c\), the unique Nash equilibrium is that no member monitors, and the leadership retains the rent with probability one;

  2. suppose instead that an organized opposition faction, distinct from the members, chooses simultaneously with them whether to monitor at cost \(c\), detects the extraction independently with probability \(\delta\) if it monitors, and takes office whenever the leadership is replaced, whoever detected the extraction. In office the faction keeps a share \(\beta\in(0,1]\) of the rent, so each member recovers only \((1-\beta)\rho/n\), and the faction’s payoff is \(\beta\rho\) times the probability of replacement minus \(c\) if it monitors. If \(n>(1-\beta)\rho\delta/c\) and \(\beta\rho\delta>c\), the unique Nash equilibrium is that no member monitors and the faction monitors, so the leadership is detected and replaced with probability \(\delta\) whatever the value of \(n\).

Proof. (a) If \(k\) other members monitor, the probability that none of them detects the extraction is \((1-\delta)^k\). A member who adds monitoring raises the detection probability by \(\delta(1-\delta)^k\) and so gains \((\rho/n)\delta(1-\delta)^k\) in expected recovered rent. This gain is at most \((\rho/n)\delta\), which is less than \(c\) when \(n>\rho\delta/c\). Monitoring is therefore strictly dominated for every member, whatever the others do, and the unique equilibrium has no monitoring.

(b) A member’s gain from monitoring is now at most \((1-\beta)(\rho/n)\delta\), which is less than \(c\) when \(n>(1-\beta)\rho\delta/c\), so monitoring is strictly dominated for every member as in (a). In any equilibrium no member monitors, and the faction’s gain from monitoring is then \(\beta\rho\delta\), which exceeds \(c\) and does not depend on \(n\). The faction therefore monitors, and the equilibrium is unique because the members’ choice is dominant. ◻

The threshold \(n^\ast=\rho\delta/c\) rises with the size of the rent and the effectiveness of monitoring and falls with its cost. Part (a) is Michels’s tendency stated as a conditional result. Part (b) identifies the check. An organized opposition with a private stake in winning office converts the public good of oversight into a private incentive, and it does so even though it lowers the members’ own incentive to monitor. Lipset, Trow, and Coleman’s study of the International Typographical Union found exactly such an arrangement, a durable two-party system inside a union, and it is the classic exception to Michels’s law (Lipset et al. 1956). Voss and Sherman documented later cases in which American union locals reversed oligarchic tendencies (Voss and Sherman 2000), and Cassinelli offered an early analytic critique of the law (Cassinelli 1953). Schumpeter’s competitive theory of democracy rests on the same mechanism at the scale of a polity: rival teams of leaders compete for the vote and monitor one another (Schumpeter 1942).

The model omits much. Leaders can raise the cost of monitoring by controlling information, which is Michels’s own emphasis, and members may value leaders for reasons other than rent. The proposition shows only that oligarchy follows from size under one set of conditions and that a specific institutional change reverses it. That is enough to treat oligarchization as a conditional tendency, which is the role the series assigns it in the argument for Proposition B (Part VII), rather than as a necessity.

6.2 Veto players and the status quo

Adding veto players is the standard constitutional remedy for concentrated rule making. It has a cost that the comparison of majoritarian and consensus democracies brings out, and a simple result shows why the cost is unavoidable.

Definition 20 (Winset). Let \(X\) be a set of alternatives, \(q\in X\) the status quo, and \(K\) a nonempty set of veto players, each with strict preferences \(\succ_i\) on \(X\). The winset of \(q\) is \(W_K(q)=\{x\in X: x\succ_i q\text{ for all }i\in K\}\), the set of alternatives that every veto player prefers to the status quo (Tsebelis 2002). A pure veto-player procedure enacts \(x\) against \(q\) exactly when \(x\in W_K(q)\); it is the unanimity game on \(K\) of Proposition 5(d). In a general simple game membership in \(W_K(q)\) is necessary for replacing \(q\) but not sufficient.

Proposition 21 (Veto players protect the status quo).

  1. If \(K\subseteq K'\), then \(W_{K'}(q)\subseteq W_K(q)\).

  2. If some \(i\in K\) prefers \(q\) to every other alternative, then \(W_K(q)=\emptyset\).

  3. Let \(q\) be a preferential rule, one that every member of a group \(B\) prefers to every other alternative, and let \(x\neq q\) be a reform that every member of \(K\setminus B\) prefers to \(q\). Under a pure veto-player procedure, \(x\) can replace \(q\) if and only if \(K\cap B=\emptyset\).

Proof. (a) An alternative in \(W_{K'}(q)\) is preferred to \(q\) by every member of \(K'\), and so by every member of \(K\). (b) If \(i\in K\) prefers \(q\) to every \(x\neq q\), no \(x\) is preferred to \(q\) by \(i\), so no \(x\) lies in \(W_K(q)\). (c) If \(K\cap B=\emptyset\), every member of \(K\) prefers \(x\) to \(q\), so \(x\in W_K(q)\). If some \(i\in K\cap B\), then \(i\) prefers \(q\) to \(x\), so \(x\notin W_K(q)\). ◻

Part (c) contains the trade-off. A preferential rule survives any reform whenever one of its beneficiaries holds a veto. Adding veto players protects the polity against new preferential rules, because a new rule must pass every veto player, and it protects existing preferential rules, because their repeal must also pass every veto player. Part (a) states the general form: the set of possible changes can only shrink as veto players are added. This is why the hypothesis HP7 in Section 7 predicts that the number of effective veto players is associated both with the persistence of preferential rules and with low responsiveness. The same logic explains the status-quo bias of consensus societies in Proposition 17, where every adult holds a veto.

The check implied by the proposition is not more veto players but the control of who holds them. A veto held by an independent body with no stake in a preferential rule, such as a court reviewing a statute against a general constitutional standard, constrains new preferential rules without protecting old ones, provided the body cannot itself be captured. That condition is the subject of Part III.

6.3 The appointment channel

The appointment channel acquires testable content under one explicit candidate, the coalition-overlap measure. Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a preregistered list of coherent coalitions, groups such as parties, factions, or dynasties that can issue joint directives. For \(S\in\mathcal{S}\) let \(s^{\mathsf{P}}(S)\) be the Shapley-Shubik share of the bloc player \(p_S\) in the quotient game on \(N_S=(N\setminus S)\cup\{p_S\}\), whose winning coalitions are defined for \(T\subseteq N\setminus S\) by \(T\in\mathcal{W}_S\) if and only if \(T\in\mathcal{W}\), and \(T\cup\{p_S\}\in\mathcal{W}_S\) if and only if \(T\cup S\in\mathcal{W}\). This bloc share is not in general the sum of the members’ separate shares; in the Iranian example of Table 2 the two councils hold \(1/6+1/6\) separately and \(1/2\) as a bloc. Let \(\kappa_\ell(S)\in[0,1]\) be the share of decision-relevant control in pillar \(\ell\) that is exercised in accordance with the directives of \(S\), and set \[\begin{equation} C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}=\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\min\{s^{\mathsf{P}}(S),\kappa_\ell(S)\}. \label{eq:overlap} \end{equation}\] The candidate lies in \([0,1]\), equals one when a single coalition holds all rule-making control and commands all of pillar \(\ell\), and equals zero when no listed coalition has a positive share in both.

Proposition 22 (Appointment bounds on integration). For a coalition \(S\) and an office \(o\) let \(f_{S,o}\in[0,1]\) be the share of the holder’s decisions that follow the directives of \(S\). For \(S\in\mathcal{S}\) with \(\alpha_{S,\ell}>0\) define the loyalty of its appointees as the conditional average \[\bar f_S=\frac{1}{\alpha_{S,\ell}}\sum_{o\in O_\ell}w_o f_{S,o}\,a_{S,o},\] and set \(\bar f_S=0\) when \(\alpha_{S,\ell}=0\). Suppose that command in pillar \(\ell\) decomposes as \(\kappa_\ell(S)=\bar f_S\alpha_{S,\ell}+\gamma_S\), where \(\gamma_S\in[0,1-\bar f_S\alpha_{S,\ell}]\) is command exercised through channels other than appointment, such as budgets, sanctions, and informal patronage. Then:

  1. for every \(S\in\mathcal{S}\), \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\ge\min\{s^{\mathsf{P}}(S),\bar f_S\alpha_{S,\ell}\}\);

  2. if command runs only through appointment (\(\gamma_S=0\) for all \(S\)) and loyalty is at most \(\bar f\) (\(\bar f_S\le\bar f\) for all \(S\)), then \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\le\bar f\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{S,\ell}\);

  3. if command runs only through appointment and no listed coalition can unilaterally appoint or remove the holder of any office in \(O_\ell\), for example because both appointment and removal require the consent of two bodies controlled by different coalitions, then \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}=0\).

Proof. (a) By Equation [eq:overlap], \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\ge\min\{s^{\mathsf{P}}(S),\kappa_\ell(S)\}\). Since \(\gamma_S\ge0\), \(\kappa_\ell(S)\ge\bar f_S\alpha_{S,\ell}\), and the minimum is nondecreasing in each argument. (b) For every \(S\), \(\min\{s^{\mathsf{P}}(S),\kappa_\ell(S)\}\le\kappa_\ell(S)=\bar f_S\alpha_{S,\ell}\le\bar f\max_{S'}\alpha_{S',\ell}\); take the maximum over \(S\). (c) The hypothesis gives \(\alpha_{S,\ell}=0\) for every \(S\in\mathcal{S}\) by Definition 8, and (b) gives \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\le0\). ◻

The proposition separates three quantities that comparative accounts often run together. The rule-making share \(s^{\mathsf{P}}(S)\) says how much of rule making the coalition controls. The appointment share \(\alpha_{S,\ell}\) says how many posts in another pillar it fills. The loyalty parameter \(\bar f_S\) says how far the people it appoints do what it wants. Nomenklatura systems maximize the first two, and part (a) then makes integration high unless loyalty is low. The Soviet evidence of elite divisions (Lane and Ross 1995) and of bureaucratic bargaining (Hough and Fainsod 1979) concerns loyalty, and so it limits the integration that appointment alone can produce. Parts (b) and (c) identify the check. Requiring the consent of a second body for both appointment and removal closes the appointment channel to any single coalition, and security of tenure lowers loyalty after appointment by removing the threat of dismissal. Under plural appointment any remaining integration must run through budgets, sanctions, or patronage (\(\gamma_S\)), which tells an investigator where to look.

6.4 Agenda control and quiet politics

The three propositions concern visible procedures. A fourth mechanism operates through what never reaches a procedure. An actor with agenda control can keep a reform off the agenda without casting a vote, and no power index computed from the formal procedure will register the effect (Definition 9). The evidence for this mechanism comes from studies of low-salience rule making, where business influence over the rules of corporate governance was greatest when issues were technical and public attention was low (Culpepper 2010), and from accounts of lobbying as a subsidy that extends the capacity of allied legislators (Hall and Deardorff 2006).

Agenda control fails as a mechanism of capture when the transparency and entry components of contestability are high. Publication of drafts and of the record of who proposed what makes non-decisions visible, and free entry into rule-making competition allows excluded interests to place their proposals on the agenda through other actors. The source framework’s redesign proposals include lobbying registers and the disclosure of contacts between rule makers and interested parties for this reason (Largo 2026, Section 11).

6.5 Checks

The four mechanisms share a structure. In each, rule making drifts toward capture when one component of contestability is weak: oversight in the monitoring game, the identity of veto holders in the veto game, independence of appointees in the appointment channel, and transparency in agenda control. The corresponding checks are organized opposition, vetoes held by actors without a stake in the rule, plural appointment and security of tenure, and publicity. None of these checks removes the rule-making function, and none requires that it be dispersed to the point of the consensus society, whose own status-quo bias Proposition 17 describes. The checks act on contestability, not on the existence of rule makers, which is the rule-making form of the series’ central distinction between Proposition A and Proposition B.

7.2 Hypotheses with falsifiers

Each hypothesis below refines one of three general hypotheses in the source framework (Largo 2026, Section 9): that benefits concentrated among connected actors go with weaker chances of challenge (H1), that groups controlling appointments, funds, and sanctions across several domains keep preferential rules after exposure (H2), and that appointment chains explain allocation in one-party sectors better than private capital does (H3). Each names its parent and the observation that would count against it.

In party-state regimes, the share of key economic, judicial, media, and security posts filled from party appointment lists predicts the integration \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\ell}\). In secular democracies, where appointment to such posts requires confirmation by a second body or is governed by civil-service rules, the same pairwise integrations are lower. Parent: H3. Falsifier: equal integration across regimes with different appointment rules, after controlling for the loyalty of appointees.

Autocracies with functioning ruling parties or legislatures that share rule making have longer-lived leaders (Gandhi and Przeworski 2007; Magaloni 2008). Parent: H2. Falsifier: no difference in leader survival once resource rents are controlled.

Leadership turnover in membership organizations is lower where monitoring is costly, and higher where an organized internal opposition exists, as Proposition 19 predicts. Parent: H1. Falsifier: turnover unrelated to measured monitoring costs or to the presence of an organized opposition.

Smaller winning coalitions relative to the selectorate predict a higher share of private goods in public spending (Bueno de Mesquita et al. 2003). Parent: H1. Falsifier: no association, or the reverse association.

Where rulers depend on religious legitimation, they grant religious authorities autonomy in law and education in return for support. Rubin and Kuru predict different consequences of this bargain (Rubin 2017; Kuru 2019), and both predictions should be tested. Parent: H2. Falsifier: religious autonomy that does not vary with rulers’ dependence on religious legitimation.

Royal rule making in the Catholic monarchies depended on negotiated intermediaries, so royal legislative success varied with the survival of provincial estates and corporate bodies (Beik 1985). Parent: H1. Falsifier: royal legislative success unrelated to the survival of intermediaries.

The number of effective veto players is positively associated with the persistence of preferential rules and negatively associated with responsiveness to changes in majority preference, as Proposition 21 predicts. Parent: H2. Falsifier: no association with the persistence of preferential rules.

HP1 and HP7 test the propositions of Section 6 directly, and HP3 tests the role of organized opposition. HP2 and HP4 import established theories of autocracy into the framework, and their value here lies in coding the same variables across all six regime types. HP5 and HP6 apply to particular historical regime types and require archival coding. Identification is difficult for all seven. Appointment rules, veto structures, and legitimation strategies are chosen by rule makers, so cross-sectional associations confound the effects of these institutions with the reasons they were adopted. The source framework’s event-study design, which compares outcomes before and after reforms of oversight in matched jurisdictions, is the appropriate design where such reforms occurred (Largo 2026, Section 9).

8 Revelation 13 and the rule-making pillar

The series reads Revelation typologically: the beast is a recurring pattern of power that claims ultimate loyalty, with the Roman Empire of the first century as its historical anchor (Bauckham 1993; Koester 2014). The reading identifies no present-day government, party, institution, or person with the beast, and it declines the futurist practice of doing so, whose long record of failed identifications is documented in the history of its reception (Fuller 1995; McGinn 1998). The mapping from the text to the six pillars is an interpretive claim of the series, not a result of exegesis.

Revelation 13 opens with a beast rising from the sea to whom “the dragon gave him his power, and his seat, and great authority” (Rev. 13:2, King James Version 1611). The rule-making authority of the beast is derived; it comes from a source outside itself and is then exercised over others. In Weber’s terms the passage poses the question of legitimacy, the ground on which a claim to rule is accepted (Weber 1978). The text then records the response of those ruled: “Who is like unto the beast? who is able to make war with him?” (Rev. 13:4). The question describes authority that admits no contest. In the vocabulary of this paper it is an image of contestability near zero and concentration near one, the condition under which the pillar’s term \(H^{\mathsf{P}}(1-Q^{\mathsf{P}})\) in unaccountable concentration is at its maximum. The beast’s reach extends “over all kindreds, and tongues, and nations” (Rev. 13:7), so a single authority governs a population whose plurality the necessity theorem takes as its starting point.

Two features of the text qualify this image. The authority is repeatedly described as given, and given for a limited time: “power was given unto him to continue forty and two months” (Rev. 13:5). Within the text the beast’s rule is bounded by a sovereignty it does not control. The second beast, from the earth, “exerciseth all the power of the first beast before him” and causes the inhabitants of the earth to worship the first (Rev. 13:12). Read typologically, it represents the legitimation of political authority by a second institution, a coupling of rule making with the ideological pillar that Part IV examines. Kraybill and Friesen locate the historical referent in the imperial cult of the cities of Asia Minor and its ties to commerce and civic life (Kraybill 1996; Friesen 2001), and Collins reads the book as a response to a crisis perceived by its audience (Collins 1984).

Revelation 17 gives the clearest image of the rule-making pillar itself. The ten horns “are ten kings, which have received no kingdom as yet; but receive power as kings one hour with the beast. These have one mind, and shall give their power and strength unto the beast” (Rev. 17:12 to 13). Ten rulers who could have acted as separate veto players surrender their authority to a single center. In the terms of Proposition 5, the image is a move from a dispersed set of rule makers toward the dictatorship case, accomplished by delegation rather than conquest. The text then has the same kings turn on the city they served (Rev. 17:16), an image of the instability of coalitions that rest on delegated power. Svolik’s account of authoritarian power sharing, in which the ruler’s allies are also the most likely source of the ruler’s removal, describes the same instability in the vocabulary of political science (Svolik 2012). The parallel is an interpretive analogy and makes no claim about the authorial intention of the text.

Bauckham reads Revelation as a critique of Rome’s claim to absolute loyalty, set against the alternative of faithful witness (Bauckham 1993). On this reading the object of the critique is not the existence of political authority. Paul’s letter to the Romans tells its readers, “Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers” (Rom. 13:1), and Christian political ethics has long read Romans 13 and Revelation 13 together (Yoder 1972). The typological reading adopted here locates the warning of Revelation 13 at the point where rule making becomes unanswerable, not at the existence of rule makers. This is the theological counterpart of the distinction between Proposition A and Proposition B. Every polity has rule makers. The image of the beast applies when the rule makers can no longer be contested.

9 Objections and scope conditions

The necessity theorem is not vacuous, because both of its substantive assumptions can fail. A group whose members always agreed would need no procedure for selecting among divergent preferences, and a group in an unchanging environment could persist without changing its rules. The predicate “implements rule making” is thin, but Section 3 states what it excludes: decisions and rule changes imposed from outside, and rules that never change through environments that no single rule system could survive. A thin predicate that can fail differs from one that holds by definition.

The boundary between rule making and interpretation is analytical, and it is drawn by act rather than by institution. Common-law courts make rules, constitutional courts shape legislation, and administrative agencies both make and apply regulations. Definition 3 classifies each such act, and the many-to-many role map of the source framework records an institution that does both in both pillars, with the coupling \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) measuring how far one coalition controls the two. Part III applies the same classification of mixed acts from the interpretive side.

Power indices measure formal power and nothing more. The Shapley-Shubik share assumes that all orderings of members are equally likely and ignores the content of proposals, the identity of agenda setters, and informal influence. The paper therefore uses the index only as a de jure baseline and pairs it with outcome-based indicators of de facto control in Table 5, recording the origin of proposals separately because initiation and enactment are different powers. A large gap between the two, as in brokered absolutism or patronal regimes, is itself evidence about where rule-making power lies.

The comparison of regime types from different centuries is valid because it asks the same questions of each, not because the answers are commensurable in every respect. A medieval caliphate and a twenty-first century democracy face different problems with different technologies of rule. The six types are lenses, as Section 5 states, and the comparative claims concern the distribution of a function that the necessity theorem shows all of them must perform.

The theological reading is separable from the analysis. Nothing in Sections 2, 3, 6, and 7 depends on it, and the reading is offered as an interpretation of a text that has shaped the political imagination of many societies, with its interpretive status stated.

The mechanisms are modeled in their simplest forms. Richer models of oligarchy, veto bargaining, and appointment would move the thresholds and could qualify some conclusions, so the propositions are offered as conditional mechanisms, not as complete theories.

The sources for some regime types are themselves contested. For the Islamic states and the Catholic monarchies in particular, the paper relies on specialists whose interpretations are debated, and it reports their positions without adjudicating among them.

10 Conclusion

Every persistent collective whose members disagree about binding choices, and whose environment changes, makes rules. The theorem of Section 3 establishes this from explicit assumptions, and the stateless and consensus societies that seem to refute it display the function in distributed form: talk, oratory without command, collective suppression of would-be dominants, and fission. The same function is performed by parliaments, party committees, kings in council, and assemblies of jurists.

The existence of the function places no constraint on its concentration. For a group of any size, procedures that satisfy the necessity theorem range from equal shares to dictatorship. Concentration, contestability, and coupling to other pillars therefore have to be measured, and the comparative evidence shows that they vary independently of the source of legitimacy that a regime claims. The conditional mechanisms of Section 6 describe how rule making can drift toward capture through diffuse monitoring incentives, protected vetoes, appointment chains, and agenda control. Each mechanism has a check that acts on contestability, and none of the checks removes the need for rule makers.

What remains uncertain is quantitative: how concentrated and how contestable rule making is in particular polities, and how strongly it is coupled to the other pillars. Answering these questions requires the preregistered coding that the framework specifies. The conclusion that the paper can defend is structural. The question of who makes the laws has always had an answer; whether the answer can be contested is the question on which accountability depends.
