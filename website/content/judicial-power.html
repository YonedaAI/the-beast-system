1 Introduction

Every governance system answers the question of who interprets its laws. The answer may be a supreme court, a council of jurists, a royal chancery, a party committee or an old man with a leopard skin who walks between two feuding lineages. This paper argues that some answer is unavoidable and that the variety of answers is where the political economy of judicial power lies.

The argument belongs to a seven-part series on the six functions that organized governance requires: control of resources (\(\mathsf{F}\)), rule-making (\(\mathsf{P}\)), interpretation (\(\mathsf{J}\)), legitimation (\(\mathsf{I}\)), coercion (\(\mathsf{C}\)) and production (\(\mathsf{W}\)) (Long 2026b; Long 2026e; Long 2026d; Long 2026a; Long 2026f; Long 2026c). The series adopts the vocabulary and the multiplex-network model of Largo (2026), which named the configuration the Hierarchy of the Beast System after the imagery of Revelation 13. The source paper used five functions and placed adjudication inside a single legal-administrative function. The series splits that function. Part II treats the making and changing of general rules; this Part treats their application to particular cases. The name “Beast System” refers throughout to the universal six-function structure, never to a religion, people, profession, regime or person.

The series keeps two propositions apart. Proposition A says that every functioning governance system contains the six functions and the dependencies among them; Part VII proves it as a theorem from six working axioms. Proposition B says that in some systems the functions become concentrated and coordinated so that independent accountability fails. Proposition B is contingent and measurable, and Part VII proves that A does not imply B.

The judicial piece of Proposition A is a theorem. A collective that persists with general rules, recurrent disputes, and costly self-help and exit must dispose of all but finitely many of its unresolved disputes through a procedure that neither party controls (Theorem 3.3). The judicial piece of Proposition B is a claim about measurement. A captured ruling persists with a probability that depends on the product of four review links, disclosure, access, independence and compliance, so an additive index of judicial independence can rate a judiciary well while one broken link leaves capture unchecked (Proposition 2.8).

The comparison is even-handed by construction. Since every regime type has the judicial function, the question is never whether a civilization has a Beast System. The question is how the function is distributed: which institutions hold it, how concentrated it is, how tightly it is coupled to the other five functions, and what keeps it accountable. Differentiation of judges, lawyers and courts is not evidence of capture (Largo 2026). Capture is a claim about particular links, and it carries a burden of evidence.

2 The judicial layer

2.1 Shared objects

The series inherits its formal objects from Largo (2026). A polity at time \(t\) is a directed multiplex network \(G_t=(V_t,E_t)\) whose nodes are organizations or coherent actor groups. Each pillar \(m\in\mathcal{M}=\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{J},\mathsf{I},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{W}\}\) has a layer with adjacency \(A^m_t\) and edge weights \(a^m_{ij,t}\in[0,1]\), the documented capacity of actor \(i\) to constrain a feasible action of actor \(j\) through mechanism \(m\). A role map assigns actors to pillars, many to many. Actor \(i\) holds a control share \(s^m_{i,t}\) in pillar \(m\), with \(\sum_i s^m_{i,t}=1\), and concentration is the Herfindahl index \(H_{m,t}=\sum_i (s^m_{i,t})^2\).

Accountability and integration are measured separately. Contestability \(Q_{m,t}\in[0,1]\) summarizes transparency, appeal rights, independence, competition and credible replacement, and the illustrative index of unaccountable concentration is \(U_t=\sum_m \omega_m H_{m,t}(1-Q_{m,t})\) with preregistered weights. Cross-pillar integration \(C_{mn,t}\in[0,1]\) is the extent to which one coalition commands appointments, budgets, sanctions or agenda in both \(m\) and \(n\), and the integration statistic is \(\mathcal{I}_t=\frac{2}{M(M-1)}\sum_{m<n}C_{mn,t}\) with \(M=6\). A capture link \((i\to j,m)\) requires privileged access or discretion, a weakening of applicable accountability, and material evidence tying the asymmetry to an institutional output. The source’s strong classification requires both high \(U_t\) and high \(\mathcal{I}_t\) (Largo 2026). Following the source, pillar indices are superscripts on quantities attached to actors or edges and subscripts on quantities attached to a whole pillar, and the time subscript is dropped when the period is clear from context.

The judicial layer \(A^{\mathsf{J}}_t\) records one kind of constraint: the capacity of one actor to fix what a general rule requires of another actor in a particular case. A court that orders a firm to pay damages, a qadi who assigns custody, an inquisitor who declares a proposition heretical and a party committee that tells a judge how to decide all hold edges in this layer. The weight of an edge does not depend on whether the actor is called a court. It depends on whether the actor’s determinations are acted upon.

The boundary with rule-making follows H. L. A. Hart’s distinction among secondary rules (Hart 1961). Hart argued that a regime of primary obligations alone suffers three defects: uncertainty about which rules exist, a static character, and the inefficiency of leaving disputes about violations to diffuse social pressure. The remedies are a rule of recognition, rules of change and rules of adjudication. The series assigns the rule of change to Part II (Long 2026e), the rule of recognition to Part IV (Long 2026d), and the rule of adjudication to the judicial pillar. The boundary classifies acts rather than institutions: making or changing a general rule is rule-making, and fixing what a rule requires in a particular case is interpretation. A body that performs both kinds of act, as a king in council does, appears in both layers, and the coupling \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J},t}\) records how far one coalition controls both.

2.2 Rules, cases and open texture

Definition 2.1Rule system with open texture

A rule system with open texture is a tuple \((X,\mathcal{V},R,\mathfrak{R})\) in which \(X\) is a set of cases, \(\mathcal{V}\) a set of verdicts with at least two elements, \(R\) a rule system in the sense of Long (2026e) (Definition 2.1), and \(\mathfrak{R}\) assigns to each rule \(r\in R\) a nonempty set \(\mathfrak{R}(r)\) of admissible readings \(\rho:X\to\mathcal{V}\). The open set of \(r\) is \[O(r)=\{x\in X : \rho(x)\neq\rho'(x)\text{ for some }\rho,\rho'\in\mathfrak{R}(r)\}.\] A rule has open texture when \(O(r)\neq\emptyset\).

An admissible reading is one that competent members of the collective would accept as a good-faith reading of the rule. The set is fixed by the collective’s own interpretive practice, not by the analyst. Hart used the phrase “open texture” for the fact that general terms have a core of settled application and a penumbra where reasonable users disagree (Hart 1961). The open set is that penumbra, expressed as the cases on which admissible readings diverge.

The series’ third working axiom, owned by Part VII, states that rules are general, cases are particular, and disputes arise. In the present vocabulary the pillar-level statement is that some rule has a nonempty open set and that cases in it occur repeatedly. The axiom is an empirical claim about rule-governed collectives. It would fail for a collective whose every rule was applied identically by every member to every case that arose.

Definition 2.2Dispute and residual dispute

A dispute is a tuple \((x,r,a,b)\) in which \(x\in O(r)\), members \(a\neq b\) each endorse an admissible reading of \(r\) and the two readings assign different verdicts to \(x\), and each member strictly prefers the verdict of its own reading. A dispute is residual if unassisted bargaining between \(a\) and \(b\) fails to produce a verdict that both act on.

The definition excludes disputes that bargaining resolves. Most disagreements end that way, and nothing in the argument below requires them to be rare. It requires only that some fail. Bargaining failure has familiar sources: each party believes its own reading is the right one, private information about the facts, the absence of a credible commitment to abide by a compromise, and indivisible stakes such as custody or honor.

2.3 Determination procedures

Definition 2.3Determination procedure and implemented adjudication

A disposition of a residual dispute takes one of four forms. In a determination, a verdict is produced by a procedure that neither party controls alone, and both parties act on it. In self-help, one party imposes its verdict by force, or the dispute escalates into feud. In exit, one party leaves the collective. Abandonment is every other disposition: the parties stop pursuing the dispute without a verdict that both act on, and the grievance remains. Adjudication is implemented in a collective when infinitely many residual disputes are disposed of by determination over an unbounded horizon.

The class of determination procedures is wide on purpose. It includes a judge’s ruling, a mediator’s proposal that both parties accept, the verdict of a council, an oath, an ordeal, a lot, and the output of a program to which both parties have submitted. Martin Shapiro argued that the triad of two disputants and a third party is the prototype of courts in every society, and that courts differ mainly in how far the third party departs from consent toward imposition (Shapiro 1981). The definition follows Shapiro by locating the function in the triadic structure rather than in an office. It departs from him only in counting impersonal procedures such as lots and programs as third parties, because they have the property that matters here: neither disputant controls the outcome.

Control shares in the judicial layer are assigned by the last word. For each final determination, the last word belongs to the actor that decided it, unless another actor directed the decision or reversed it, in which case it belongs to that actor. A lower court’s judgment that is never appealed, or that an apex court declines to review, is attributed to the lower court; the bare possibility of reversal does not count. Direction counts only when it is documented, for example by an instruction, an approval requirement or a review by a body outside the bench. A multimember court is a single node, since the source’s nodes are organizations, and a determination that requires the concurrence of two bodies is split equally between them. Actor \(i\)’s control share \(s^{\mathsf{J}}_{i,t}\) is its stake-weighted share of the last words in period \(t\). A party committee that instructs judges in politically salient cases therefore holds judicial control share in those cases even though it signs no judgment. Anticipatory deference, in which judges decide as a committee would wish without being told, does not enter \(s^{\mathsf{J}}_{i,t}\); it appears as a low value of the independence component defined below.

2.4 Judicial contestability and ruler-boundness

The source defines \(Q_{m,t}\) as a composite of transparency, appeal rights, institutional independence, effective competition and credible replacement (Largo 2026). For the judicial layer these ingredients form a sequence. A determination can be corrected only if its defect is visible, if an affected party can bring a challenge, if the forum that hears the challenge is independent of the party that benefited, and if the powerful party obeys the corrected determination.

Definition 2.4Judicial contestability components

The judicial contestability vector at time \(t\) is \(\mathbf{q}_t=(q^{\mathrm{dis}}_t,q^{\mathrm{acc}}_t,q^{\mathrm{ind}}_t,q^{\mathrm{cmp}}_t)\in[0,1]^4\). Its components are the probabilities of four successive stages in the correction of a defective determination, each conditional on the stages before it. Disclosure \(q^{\mathrm{dis}}_t\) is the probability that the defect and the facts bearing on the determination’s integrity are publicly recorded. Access \(q^{\mathrm{acc}}_t\) is the probability that, given disclosure, an adversely affected party brings a challenge, including an appeal, given the cost of counsel and the availability of a forum. Independence \(q^{\mathrm{ind}}_t\) is the probability that, given a challenge, the reviewing forum decides on the merits rather than at the direction of the beneficiary, which depends on tenure security and on appointment insulation, that is, on the appointment channel by which a coalition fills judicial offices (Long 2026e). Compliance \(q^{\mathrm{cmp}}_t\) is the probability that, given an adverse ruling on review, the beneficiary complies with it. Two aggregates are used: \[Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}=\sum_{k} w_k q^{k}_t,\quad \textstyle\sum_k w_k=1,\ w_k\ge 0, \qquad\qquad Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}=\prod_{k} q^{k}_t.\]

The additive aggregate is the form of most published indices. The multiplicative aggregate is the review-chain form, which Proposition 2.8 shows governs the persistence of captured outcomes. The two are ordered.

Lemma 2.5Ordering of the aggregates

For every \(\mathbf{q}_t\in[0,1]^4\) and every weight vector, \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}\le\min_k q^k_t\le Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}\). Consequently, the judicial term \(\omega_{\mathsf{J}}H_{\mathsf{J},t}(1-Q_{\mathsf{J},t})\) of \(U_t\) is at least as large when \(Q_{\mathsf{J},t}\) is measured by \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}\) as when it is measured by \(Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}\).

Proof. A product of numbers in \([0,1]\) is no larger than any of its factors, so \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}\le\min_k q^k_t\). A weighted average with nonnegative weights summing to one is no smaller than the smallest averaged value, so \(\min_k q^k_t\le Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}\). The second claim follows because \(\omega_{\mathsf{J}}H_{\mathsf{J},t}\ge 0\) and \(1-Q_{\mathsf{J},t}\) decreases in \(Q_{\mathsf{J},t}\). ◻

The bounds in the lemma are attained in opposite cases. When every component is equal, the minimum and the additive aggregate coincide; when one stage fails and the others work, the chain aggregate is zero while the additive aggregate stays high. Dispersion among the components is therefore what separates the two measures.

Definition 2.6Ruler-boundness and the two-tier judicial layer

Ruler-boundness \(\beta_t\) is the compliance component \(q^{\mathrm{cmp}}_t\) restricted to rulings against members of the governing coalition: the probability that the coalition complies with an adverse final determination. A judicial layer is two-tier when the set of cases is partitioned into an ordinary class and a politically salient class, and the contestability vector differs between them.

Ruler-boundness gives a working form to a distinction that Brian Tamanaha draws between the rule of law and rule by law (Tamanaha 2004). Under rule by law, law is an instrument that rulers use to govern others; under the rule of law, rulers are themselves bound. A regime can have an effective judicial function for ordinary disputes and low ruler-boundness at once. Randall Peerenboom’s account of Chinese legal reform describes a thin rule of law of this partial kind (Peerenboom 2002), and Kathryn Hendley’s study of everyday law in Russia documents courts that decide ordinary disputes routinely while politically salient cases follow a different logic (Hendley 2017).

Definition 2.7Judicial capture link

A judicial capture link is a capture link \((i\to j,\mathsf{J})\) in the sense of Largo (2026) whose three conditions take the following form in the judicial layer. Actor \(i\) has privileged access to or discretion over determinations affecting \(j\), for example through control of the appointment, promotion, remuneration or election funding of the deciding judges, through ex parte communication, or through control of which forum hears the case. The privilege weakens at least one component of the contestability vector \(\mathbf{q}_t\). Material evidence ties the asymmetry to determinations, for example a difference in outcomes between cases in which the privilege is present and comparable cases in which it is absent.

2.5 Review chains

The source’s second proposition holds that effective independent review and voice should reduce the probability that attempted capture yields persistent outcomes (Largo 2026). For the judicial layer the structure of review can be stated precisely.

Proposition 2.8Review chain

Suppose an attempted capture produces a favorable determination with probability \(q_0\in(0,1]\), and the determination is reversed only if the four stages of Definition 2.4 all succeed, with the conditional probabilities given there. Then the probability that the captured outcome persists is \[P_{\mathrm{persist}}=q_0\big(1-Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}\big),\qquad Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}=q^{\mathrm{dis}}_tq^{\mathrm{acc}}_tq^{\mathrm{ind}}_tq^{\mathrm{cmp}}_t.\] Three consequences follow. If any link is zero, then \(P_{\mathrm{persist}}=q_0\) whatever the other links. Raising link \(k\) by \(\Delta\in(0,1-q^k_t]\) reduces \(P_{\mathrm{persist}}\) by \(q_0\Delta\prod_{j\neq k}q^j_t\), so the marginal effect of link \(k\) is \(-\partial P_{\mathrm{persist}}/\partial q^k_t=q_0\prod_{j\neq k}q^j_t\); when every link is positive, this marginal effect is largest for the link with the smallest value. For any weights \(w_k>0\) there are contestability vectors with the same additive aggregate \(Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}\) and different persistence probabilities, including a vector with \(Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}=1-\min_k w_k\) and \(P_{\mathrm{persist}}=q_0\).

Proof. Reversal is the intersection of four events, and the components are the probabilities of each event conditional on the preceding ones, so by the chain rule of probability the probability of reversal given a captured determination is the product \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}\). Persistence therefore has probability \(q_0(1-Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t})\). If some \(q^k_t=0\), the product is zero. Raising \(q^k_t\) by \(\Delta\) raises the product by \(\Delta\prod_{j\neq k}q^j_t\), which reduces \(P_{\mathrm{persist}}\) by \(q_0\Delta\prod_{j\neq k}q^j_t\). The marginal effect is the derivative of this linear expression in \(\Delta\). When all links are positive, \(\prod_{j\neq k}q^j_t=Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}/q^k_t\), which is largest for the smallest \(q^k_t\). For the last claim, let \(k^{*}\) be a link with the smallest weight, set \(q^{k^{*}}_t=0\) and every other link to one. Then \(Q^{+}_{\mathsf{J},t}=1-w_{k^{*}}\) and \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}=0\), so \(P_{\mathrm{persist}}=q_0\). Setting every link to \(1-w_{k^{*}}\) gives the same additive aggregate and a strictly positive product, hence, because \(q_0>0\), a strictly smaller persistence probability. ◻

The proposition explains why judicial-independence indices built by addition can mislead. A judiciary with published reasons, open access and independent judges scores well on an additive index, but if the governing coalition ignores adverse rulings, the review chain is broken at its last link and captured outcomes persist. The multiplicative form also implies that reform yields the most where the chain is weakest. The comparison in Section 5 below suggests that the weakest link is often access in market-capitalist systems, compliance in the political tier of party-states, and independence in backsliding democracies.

The product is exact because each component is defined conditionally on the stages before it; no independence assumption is involved. Dependence matters for measurement. Published indicators usually record marginal frequencies, such as the share of decisions published or of rulings against the government, and if the stages tend to succeed or fail together, the product of marginal frequencies understates the true probability of reversal. Tests of the review-chain claim should therefore estimate the conditional components from case histories, as hypothesis HJ8 requires.

2.6 Two-tier judicial layers

Proposition 2.9Aggregation hides the political tier

For a two-tier judicial layer in which the politically salient tier carries a share \(\varphi\in(0,1)\) of stake-weighted cases and has chain contestability \(Q^{\times}_{\Pi}\), while the ordinary tier has \(Q^{\times}_{O}\), define the stake-weighted contestability \(\bar Q^{\times}=(1-\varphi)Q^{\times}_O+\varphi Q^{\times}_\Pi\). For every \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\) there is a two-tier layer with \(\varphi\in(0,\varepsilon]\), \(\bar Q^{\times}\ge 1-\varepsilon\) and \(Q^{\times}_\Pi=0\).

Proof. Given \(\varepsilon\), take \(\varphi=\varepsilon\), \(Q^{\times}_O=1\) and \(Q^{\times}_\Pi=0\). Then \(\bar Q^{\times}=1-\varepsilon\). ◻

The proof is elementary, and the point is about measurement. Surveys of business users, caseload statistics and appeal rates are dominated by the ordinary tier. They can report a well-functioning judiciary in a regime whose courts never bind the ruling coalition. Control shares and concentration should also be computed tier by tier, because the last word in the political tier may rest with an actor that holds none in the ordinary tier. Measurements intended to test Proposition B must therefore be tier-specific, and hypothesis HJ3 in Section 7 makes the tiers an object of test.

3 Necessity of the judicial function

3.1 The necessity theorem

The argument for necessity is a conditions-of-persistence argument. It does not explain why any particular court exists. It shows that a collective with general rules cannot persist indefinitely without performing the function that courts perform, provided three assumptions hold.

Assumption 3.1Recurrent residual disputes

On the event that the collective persists for all time, infinitely many residual disputes arise, and each reaches a disposition at some finite time.

Assumption 3.2Costly self-help and exit

There is \(\kappa\in(0,1]\) such that, conditional on the history up to the disposition of any residual dispute, a disposition by self-help, by exit or by abandonment ends the collective’s persistence with probability at least \(\kappa\).

Assumption 3.1 is the third axiom in operational form together with a positive rate of bargaining failure. Assumption 3.2 bundles three claims. Self-help between members tends toward feud, which in segmentary societies can split the group along lineage lines. Exit by one party removes a member and, when the parties are heads of kin groups, a segment of the collective. Abandonment leaves a grievance that tends to return as self-help or exit. The assumption does not say that every feud destroys the group. It says that each abandoned or forcibly settled dispute carries a risk to the collective’s continuation as a single decision-making unit, and that the risk is bounded below.

Theorem 3.3Necessity of adjudication

Under Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2, the event that the collective persists for all time is contained, up to a null set, in the event that adjudication is implemented. If the collective persists with positive probability, the fraction of residual disputes disposed of by determination converges to one almost surely on the persistence event.

Proof. Let \(\Pi\) be the event that the collective persists for all time and let \(N\) be the number of residual disputes disposed of by self-help, exit or abandonment before the collective ends, with \(N=\infty\) allowed. For \(n\ge 1\) let \(S_n\) be the event that at least \(n\) such dispositions occur and the collective survives each of the first \(n\). By Assumption 3.2, conditional on \(S_{n-1}\) and on the occurrence of an \(n\)-th such disposition, survival through it has probability at most \(1-\kappa\). Induction gives \(\Pr(S_n)\le(1-\kappa)^n\). Since \(\Pi\cap\{N\ge n\}\subseteq S_n\), we have \(\Pr(\Pi\cap\{N=\infty\})\le\inf_n (1-\kappa)^n=0\). Hence, almost surely on \(\Pi\), only finitely many residual disputes are disposed of other than by determination. By Assumption 3.1, infinitely many residual disputes arise on \(\Pi\), and each reaches exactly one of the four dispositions of Definition 2.3, so infinitely many are disposed of by determination and adjudication is implemented. Index residual disputes in order of disposition, breaking ties arbitrarily. If \(D_k\) and \(N_k\) count determinations and other dispositions among the first \(k\) residual disputes so indexed, then on \(\Pi\) the count \(N_k\) is eventually constant while \(D_k+N_k=k\), so \(D_k/k\to 1\). ◻

The theorem says nothing about who determines. A single mediator, a rotating council, a lottery and a hierarchy of appellate courts all satisfy it. It also says nothing about the quality of determinations. A collective may persist with determinations that are biased, slow or cruel, so long as the parties act on them. The necessity is of a function, and the function is minimal: a procedure that neither disputant controls, whose output both act on.

3.2 Minimal forms in stateless and consensus societies

The theorem predicts that collectives without states still adjudicate, and that their procedures need not resemble courts. The ethnographic record bears this out (Table 1), with a qualification that the next subsection makes precise.

Among the Nuer, whom E. E. Evans-Pritchard described as living in an ordered anarchy without government, a killing set off a feud between lineages unless compensation in cattle was arranged through the leopard-skin chief (Evans-Pritchard 1940). The chief could not compel either side. His role was to offer sanctuary to the killer, to negotiate the payment and to perform the rites that ended the feud. Evans-Pritchard’s own emphasis on the absence of government is the strongest counter-reading: the chief decided nothing that the parties had not accepted. Under Definition 2.3, however, consent does not disqualify a procedure. What matters is that the settlement was produced through a third party whom neither lineage controlled, and that both acted on it. The source of compliance was the parties’ preference for settlement over a continuing feud, which is the sanction-side dependency discussed in Section 3.4.

Max Gluckman’s study of the Barotse shows a fuller judicial form, with judges who measured litigants against the conduct expected of a reasonable person in their position (Gluckman 1955). The case is a contrast rather than a hard case, since the Lozi had a kingdom. P. H. Gulliver’s Arusha are closer to the stateless baseline (Gulliver 1963). Gulliver described dispute settlement through negotiation in moots and councils in which the relative strength of the parties’ supporters shaped the outcome. That description invites the reading that no adjudication took place, only bargaining. The reply is that a dispute that reaches a moot is residual in the sense of Definition 2.2: unassisted bargaining between the principals has failed, and the moot supplies a forum and participants who are not principals. A determination produced by a forum in which strength matters is a biased determination, not the absence of one.

Even the societies most often cited as lacking hierarchy have a judging procedure: the camp’s collective talk. Richard Lee’s ethnography of the Ju/’hoansi of the Kalahari records disputes handled by public talk among kin and camp members, together with frequent movement between camps (Lee 2013). Polly Wiessner’s study of norm enforcement among the Ju/’hoansi finds that criticism and talk were the main instruments by which norm violations were addressed (Wiessner 2005). Christopher Boehm’s comparative work on foragers describes the same pattern from the side of the group, in which a coalition of subordinates judges and sanctions anyone who tries to dominate (Boehm 1999). James Woodburn adds that the egalitarianism of immediate-return societies is produced by leveling institutions, not by the absence of institutions (Woodburn 1982). The same body judges and sanctions, so \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) is high in these societies, but neither disputant controls the procedure and the function is present.

Elinor Ostrom’s design principles for long-enduring commons institutions include rapid access to low-cost local arenas for resolving conflicts among users and between users and officials (Ostrom 1990). Michael Cox and coauthors’ review of studies testing the principles found broad empirical support for them as a set (Cox et al. 2010). Ostrom’s cases are designed institutions, so they show that successful self-governance includes adjudication rather than proving that it must. With the stateless cases, they show the judicial function wherever general rules govern repeated interaction.

3.3 Counterexample attempts

The strongest counterexample to Theorem 3.3 is a collective that resolves residual disputes by exit. Woodburn’s immediate-return societies and the Ju/’hoansi practice of moving between camps come close. When exit is costless the theorem’s forcing argument fails, and the failure is a genuine limit on the claim.

Proposition 3.4Exit substitution

Suppose Assumption 3.1 holds and a disposition by self-help or abandonment ends persistence with probability at least \(\kappa>0\), but a disposition by exit carries no risk to persistence and is always available. Then there are collectives that persist with probability one and in which no residual dispute is disposed of by determination.

Proof. Consider a regional population divided among bands. Let every residual dispute be disposed of by one party moving to another band, which by hypothesis does not affect the persistence of the regional collective. The collective never incurs the hazard attached to self-help or abandonment, so it persists with probability one, and no determination occurs. ◻

The proposition narrows the claim. Adjudication is forced where exit is costly. Where exit is cheap, the judicial function can be thin, and the ethnography of immediate-return foragers is consistent with that prediction: talk, ridicule and departure do much of the work that courts do elsewhere (Woodburn 1982; Lee 2013). Two qualifications keep the narrowing in proportion. The cheap-exit condition is rare: it requires mobile residence, no stored wealth, and access to other groups on the same terms, which Woodburn identified as distinctive of a small set of societies. The ethnographies of these societies also record talk-based mediation alongside exit (Lee 2013; Wiessner 2005), so the function is thin rather than absent.

The second counterexample attempt is a rule system that leaves no room for interpretation because a program executes it. If rules are code, the program decides, and no judge is needed.

Proposition 3.5Relocation of interpretation

Let a rule \(r\) over cases \(X\) be executed by a program \(c:Z\to\mathcal{V}\) applied to recorded inputs \(z=\zeta(x)\), where an encoding \(\zeta:X\to Z\) records a case for the program. Let \(\mathcal{E}(r)\) be the nonempty set of admissible encodings, those that competent members would accept as recording cases faithfully for the rule’s purpose, and let the code-level open set be \[O_c(r)=\{x\in X : c(\zeta(x))\neq c(\zeta'(x))\text{ for some }\zeta,\zeta'\in\mathcal{E}(r)\}.\] Suppose the parties have agreed in advance that the verdict \(c(\zeta_0(x))\) settles every case \(x\) in a set \(X_0\subseteq X\), for a designated \(\zeta_0\in\mathcal{E}(r)\), and that neither party alone controls \(c\) or \(\zeta_0\) on \(X_0\). Outside \(X_0\) both the rule and the program have standing, so the admissible positions there are \(\mathfrak{R}^{*}(r)=\mathfrak{R}(r)\cup\{c\circ\zeta:\zeta\in\mathcal{E}(r)\}\), with open set \(O^{*}(r)\) computed from \(\mathfrak{R}^{*}(r)\) as in Definition 2.1. Then \(O(r)\cup O_c(r)\subseteq O^{*}(r)\), and \(O^{*}(r)\) also contains every case at which some \(\rho\in\mathfrak{R}(r)\) and some \(c\circ\zeta\) with \(\zeta\in\mathcal{E}(r)\) disagree. The following hold.

  1. On \(X_0\) the program with encoding \(\zeta_0\) is a determination procedure in the sense of Definition 2.3.

  2. For every case \(x\in O^{*}(r)\setminus X_0\) there are admissible positions with different verdicts, so disputes can arise at \(x\), and those in which bargaining fails are residual disputes subject to Theorem 3.3.

  3. \(O_c(r)=\emptyset\) if and only if every admissible encoding yields the same verdict under \(c\) for every case.

Proof. For (i), on \(X_0\) the program produces the verdict, neither party alone controls the program or the encoding by hypothesis, and both parties have agreed in advance to act on the verdict. The inclusion \(O(r)\cup O_c(r)\subseteq O^{*}(r)\) holds because \(\mathfrak{R}^{*}(r)\) contains both \(\mathfrak{R}(r)\) and \(\{c\circ\zeta:\zeta\in\mathcal{E}(r)\}\), and a case at which some reading \(\rho\in\mathfrak{R}(r)\) and some program reading \(c\circ\zeta\) disagree lies in \(O^{*}(r)\) by definition. For (ii), the pair \((r,\mathfrak{R}^{*}(r))\) is a rule system with open texture in the sense of Definition 2.1, so a case in \(O^{*}(r)\setminus X_0\) admits two admissible positions with different verdicts, and two members whose interests favor different verdicts can each endorse one, which is a dispute under Definition 2.2. The parties have not agreed that the program settles such a case, so a dispute there in which bargaining fails is residual, and Theorem 3.3 applies. Claim (iii) restates the definition of \(O_c(r)\). ◻

Encoding moves the open set from the terms of the rule to the recording of cases and to the gap between rule and program: whether a delivery occurred, whether a price feed was accurate, whether a reported event matched the contract’s description. A deterministic program removes open texture only if every admissible encoding agrees, which is rare for rules about the world rather than about the program’s own state. Unforeseen cases also fall outside \(X_0\) almost by definition. Systems that execute agreements automatically therefore acquire arbiters, oracles, governance votes or forks, each of which is a determination procedure. Interpretation relocates; it does not vanish.

The third counterexample attempt is pure self-help, a society in which wronged parties retaliate and nothing more. Evans-Pritchard’s model of balanced opposition among Nuer lineages is the classic candidate. The reply is that the Nuer did not leave feuds to run indefinitely. Compensation through the leopard-skin chief ended them (Evans-Pritchard 1940). A society of pure self-help without any procedure for ending feuds would violate Assumption 3.2 at every dispute, and the theorem predicts that it would not persist as a single collective. The falsifier for the necessity claim follows: a persistent collective with costly exit in which residual disputes about rule application are always settled by force of the parties or left open, without the collective dissolving.

3.4 Dependencies of the judicial function

A determination settles a dispute only if the losing party acts on it. Alexander Hamilton made the point about the federal judiciary in 1788: the judiciary “may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment,” and depends on the executive arm for the efficacy of its judgments (Hamilton 1788). The dependency is weaker than Hamilton’s phrasing suggests.

Proposition 3.6Disjunctive dependency

Suppose the losing party in a determination gains \(g>0\) by defying it, and complies if and only if \(\ell+\sigma\ge g\), where \(\ell\ge 0\) is the value it places on the procedure’s legitimacy, including internalized obligation and reputation, and \(\sigma\ge 0\) is the expected sanction for defiance. Then an effective determination requires \(\ell>0\) or \(\sigma>0\). Neither \(\ell>0\) nor \(\sigma>0\) is required by itself.

Proof. If \(\ell=\sigma=0\), then \(\ell+\sigma=0<g\) and the loser defies, so the determination is not effective. For the second claim, a determination with \(\ell\ge g\) and \(\sigma=0\) is effective without sanction, and a determination with \(\sigma\ge g\) and \(\ell=0\) is effective without legitimacy. ◻

The judicial function therefore depends on the legitimation pillar or on the coercion pillar. In Proposition 3.6, \(\ell\) is the value a party places on complying because it regards the procedure as rightful, which Part IV calls normative legitimacy (Long 2026d), and \(\sigma\) is the expected sanction for defiance, which Part V analyzes (Long 2026a). The Nuer settlement and the Ju/’hoansi talking-out rest mainly on legitimacy and the value of continued relations, while an imposed verdict in a party-state’s political case rests mainly on sanction. Hamilton’s executive arm is one source of \(\sigma\). The reservoir of public support that James Gibson and coauthors measured for the United States Supreme Court is a source of \(\ell\) (Gibson et al. 2003).

Because the dependency is disjunctive, it cannot be represented as two separate arcs into the judicial node. In the dependency digraph among the six functions that Part VII studies (Long 2026c), the arcs from legitimation and coercion into interpretation form a single or-condition, a hyperedge, and a proof that the digraph is strongly connected must treat them that way.

The judicial function also feeds the other pillars. Interpretation changes rules through precedent, which enters rule-making through the rule of change. Reasoned judgments are public acts that shape common beliefs about what the rules are, which enters legitimation through the rule of recognition.

3.5 Functionalist objections

Carl Hempel warned that from the premise that a system needs some function one cannot infer that a particular institution exists, because functional equivalents may meet the need (Hempel 1959). Theorem 3.3 respects the warning. Its conclusion is the existence of some determination procedure, and the minimal forms in Table 1 are exactly the functional equivalents that Hempel had in mind. Jon Elster argued that a functional explanation of an institution by its beneficial effects is legitimate only when a feedback mechanism links the effects back to the institution’s persistence (Elster 1983). The theorem does not explain why any collective has a court. It states a condition that every persisting collective with costly exit satisfies. Robert Merton’s distinction between manifest and latent functions, and his insistence on functional alternatives (Merton 1968), apply in the same way: courts have many latent functions, including legitimation and social control, and the theorem concerns none of them.

3.6 Necessity without capture

Corollary 3.7Necessity does not imply capture

For every value \(c\in[0,1]\) there are collectives that satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 3.3, implement adjudication, and have chain contestability \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}=c\).

Proof. Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 constrain the arrival of residual disputes and the hazard attached to resolving them other than by determination. Neither refers to disclosure, access, independence or compliance. Take a collective satisfying both assumptions in which every residual dispute is resolved by determination, and assign its review links any values whose product is \(c\), for example \(q^{\mathrm{dis}}_t=q^{\mathrm{acc}}_t=q^{\mathrm{ind}}_t=1\) and \(q^{\mathrm{cmp}}_t=c\). The hypotheses of the theorem still hold, adjudication is implemented, and \(Q^{\times}_{\mathsf{J},t}=c\). ◻

The corollary is the judicial instance of the series claim that Proposition A does not imply Proposition B, which Part VII proves in general. The necessity theorem fixes that some determination procedure exists. It leaves the contestability of that procedure, and hence the persistence of captured rulings under Proposition 2.8, entirely open.

4 Literature on judicial power

4.1 Accounts of judicial independence

The literature on judicial power divides over one question: why would anyone who holds power allow a body to tell them what the law requires? The answers fall into four families, and the comparative analysis in Section 5 draws on all of them.

The first family treats independent courts as a device that serves the powerful. William Landes and Richard Posner argued that an independent judiciary makes legislative deals durable, because judges who enforce statutes as enacted protect the bargains that interest groups purchased from earlier legislatures (Landes and Posner 1975). J. Mark Ramseyer argued that politicians tolerate independent courts when they expect to lose office, because a court that binds the next government protects them (Ramseyer 1994). Matthew Stephenson formalized the insurance logic as a repeated game between parties that alternate in power (Stephenson 2003), and Keith Whittington showed that politically fragmented coalitions in the United States have repeatedly supported the exercise of judicial review against their own legislative partners (Whittington 2005). Ran Hirschl’s hegemonic preservation thesis is a sharper version: elites facing electoral decline transfer power to courts whose judges share their commitments, as he argued for Israel, Canada, New Zealand and South Africa (Hirschl 2001; Hirschl 2004). The insurance and preservation accounts agree that independence is chosen for strategic reasons. They differ on whom it protects.

The second family studies courts under authoritarian rule. Tom Ginsburg and Tamir Moustafa collected evidence that authoritarian rulers use courts for social control, for legitimation, to commit credibly to investors, to discipline their own administrators and to resolve disputes within the ruling coalition (Ginsburg and Moustafa 2008). Moustafa’s study of Egypt’s Supreme Constitutional Court shows a regime that empowered a court to reassure investors and then found the court supporting challenges to the regime (Moustafa 2007). Gretchen Helmke’s study of Argentina showed that judges without secure tenure rule against outgoing governments when they expect a change in power, a pattern she called strategic defection (Helmke 2005). These works establish that courts matter under authoritarianism and that their independence varies by case type and by the political calendar.

The third family asks what makes judges’ rulings stick. Georg Vanberg’s study of the German Federal Constitutional Court argues that public support protects a court against noncompliance when the public can observe whether the government complies (Vanberg 2005). Gibson and coauthors found that the United States Supreme Court’s legitimacy survived its controversial decision in the 2000 presidential election, which they attributed to a reservoir of goodwill (Gibson et al. 2003). Alec Stone Sweet showed how European constitutional courts became co-legislators whose anticipated rulings shape the drafting of statutes (Stone Sweet 2000). Mark Tushnet compared strong-form review, in which courts have the last word, with weak-form review, in which legislatures can respond, and argued that weak-form systems can protect social rights without judicial supremacy (Tushnet 2008).

The fourth family concerns who uses courts and who wins. Marc Galanter distinguished repeat players, who litigate many similar cases, from one-shotters, and argued that repeat players come out ahead because they can play for favorable rules as well as for immediate outcomes (Galanter 1974). Charles Epp showed that rights revolutions depended on a support structure of lawyers, organizations and funding rather than on constitutional text alone (Epp 1998).

Comparative legal history supplies evidence of a different kind, from religious, ecclesiastical and royal courts that the political science literature seldom examines. Wael Hallaq’s work on Islamic law describes a judicial order in which state-appointed qadis applied a law developed by jurists who were not state officials (Hallaq 2005; Hallaq 2009). Harold Berman argued that the papal reforms of the late eleventh century produced the first modern Western legal system and a plurality of jurisdictions that limited every authority, including the Church (Berman 1983). Richard Kagan’s study of litigation in Castile, David Bell’s study of barristers in Paris, and Irene Fosi’s study of papal justice show courts in early modern Catholic monarchies used by large numbers of ordinary litigants (Kagan 1981; Bell 1994; Fosi 2011). The socialist-legality literature, from Harold Berman’s interpretation of Soviet law to Stanley Lubman’s and Carl Minzner’s studies of China, traces courts embedded in party rule (Berman 1963; Lubman 1999; Minzner 2011).

The strategic accounts explain why independence varies but take the existence of courts for granted. The legal-historical accounts document courts in many regimes but rarely compare them on common dimensions. A shared vocabulary, the judicial layer and its contestability vector, lets both be read as answers to one question: how is the universal judicial function coupled to the other five?

4.2 The insurance model

Why would a governing coalition allow courts to bind it? The insurance account answers that independence is a hedge against losing power (Landes and Posner 1975; Ramseyer 1994; Stephenson 2003).

Proposition 4.1Insurance model

An incumbent chooses court independence \(\iota\in[0,1]\), which persists into the next period. In each period the office holder can take an abusive action that yields it \(g>0\) and costs the other side \(L>0\), and the court blocks the action with probability \(\iota\). The incumbent loses office before the next period with probability \(\pi\in[0,1]\) and discounts the future by \(\delta\in(0,1]\). Let \[\pi^{*}=\frac{g(1+\delta)}{\delta(g+L)}.\] The incumbent’s expected payoff is maximized at \(\iota^{*}=1\) if \(\pi>\pi^{*}\), at \(\iota^{*}=0\) if \(\pi<\pi^{*}\), and by every \(\iota\in[0,1]\) if \(\pi=\pi^{*}\). The threshold \(\pi^{*}\) decreases in \(L\) and \(\delta\) and increases in \(g\). If \(g\ge\delta L\), then \(\pi^{*}\ge 1\) and independence is never strictly optimal.

Proof. The incumbent’s payoff is \[\Phi(\iota)=(1-\iota)g+\delta\big[(1-\pi)(1-\iota)g-\pi(1-\iota)L\big]=(1-\iota)\big[g(1+\delta)-\delta\pi(g+L)\big].\] \(\Phi\) is linear in \(\iota\) with slope \(-\big[g(1+\delta)-\delta\pi(g+L)\big]\), which is positive when \(\pi>\pi^{*}\), negative when \(\pi<\pi^{*}\) and zero when \(\pi=\pi^{*}\). The comparative statics follow from differentiating \(\pi^{*}\): \(\partial\pi^{*}/\partial L=-g(1+\delta)/[\delta(g+L)^2]<0\), \(\partial\pi^{*}/\partial\delta=-g/[\delta^{2}(g+L)]<0\), and \(\partial\pi^{*}/\partial g=(1+\delta)L/[\delta(g+L)^2]>0\). Finally, \(\pi^{*}\ge 1\) if and only if \(g(1+\delta)\ge\delta(g+L)\), which simplifies to \(g\ge\delta L\). ◻

The model’s assumptions do the work, and they should be stated plainly. Independence chosen today must persist, which requires that institutional change be costly; Landes and Posner’s account relies on the same stickiness (Landes and Posner 1975). The court must bind whoever holds office, which excludes a court that binds only the opposition. The model is linear, which produces an all-or-nothing choice; a convex cost of independence would produce interior solutions with the same comparative statics. In the vocabulary of Section 2, \(\iota\) is the independence component \(q^{\mathrm{ind}}_t\), and when the incumbent appoints the judges through the appointment channel, keeping \(\iota\) low is the same as keeping \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) high.

The result says that independence is more likely where the incumbent expects to lose power, where abuse by the other side would be costly, and where the incumbent is patient. It fits the variation across party-states and secular democracies described in Section 5: a ruling party with a low \(\pi\) has little reason to grant independence, and a fragmented democracy with a high \(\pi\) has strong reason. Ramseyer used the same logic to explain why Japanese courts were less independent than American ones during a long period of single-party dominance (Ramseyer 1994).

Remark 4.2Strategic defection

Helmke’s account of Argentine judges adds a judge-side version of the same logic (Helmke 2005). Assume that a judge without secure tenure is sanctioned, with loss \(s>0\), only by whichever side holds power after the ruling takes effect and only if the ruling went against that side; that the case sets the incumbent against the side that would replace it; and that the merits are balanced. Ruling against the incumbent then risks \((1-\pi)s\), and ruling for it risks \(\pi s\), so the judge rules against the incumbent when \(\pi>1/2\). De facto independence from the incumbent rises as the incumbent’s hold weakens, even when de jure independence is unchanged. The Argentine court that had favored the Menem government began to rule against it as its hold weakened, which fits this prediction.

4.3 Repeat players and legal aid

Galanter’s argument about repeat players can be stated as a contest. A repeat player litigates many similar cases and values a favorable ruling both for the case at hand and for the precedent it sets. A one-shotter values only the case at hand.

Proposition 4.3Repeat-player contest

A repeat player \(R\) and a one-shotter \(O\) contest a case by spending \(e_R,e_O\ge 0\), and \(R\) wins with probability \(p_R=me_R/(me_R+e_O)\), where \(m>0\) is the relative merit of \(R\)’s position. \(O\) values winning at \(v>0\). \(R\) litigates \(n\ge 1\) similar cases, and a favorable ruling is worth \(v\) in the present case plus \(\theta v\) in each of the \(n-1\) future cases through precedent, with \(\theta\in[0,1]\). In the unique equilibrium with positive spending, \[p_R=\frac{m(1+\theta(n-1))}{m(1+\theta(n-1))+1}.\] With equal merits (\(m=1\)), \(p_R>1/2\) if and only if \(\theta(n-1)>0\). The probability \(p_R\) is nondecreasing in \(n\) and \(\theta\), strictly increasing in \(n\) when \(\theta>0\), and strictly increasing in \(\theta\) when \(n>1\). If legal aid reimburses a fraction \(\lambda\in[0,1)\) of \(O\)’s spending, the equilibrium becomes \[p_R=\frac{m(1+\theta(n-1))(1-\lambda)}{m(1+\theta(n-1))(1-\lambda)+1},\] and with equal merits the advantage is eliminated exactly when \(\lambda=\theta(n-1)/(1+\theta(n-1))\).

Proof. Let \(v_R=v(1+\theta(n-1))\). Consider first a lottery contest in which players \(1\) and \(2\) value the prize at \(A>0\) and \(B>0\), choose efforts \(x_1,x_2\ge 0\) at unit cost, and player \(1\) wins with probability \(x_1/(x_1+x_2)\). Player \(1\) maximizes \(Ax_1/(x_1+x_2)-x_1\) and player \(2\) maximizes \(Bx_2/(x_1+x_2)-x_2\). Each objective is strictly concave in the player’s own effort when the other’s effort is positive, and the first-order conditions are \[\frac{A\,x_2}{(x_1+x_2)^2}=1,\qquad \frac{B\,x_1}{(x_1+x_2)^2}=1.\] Dividing gives \(x_1/x_2=A/B\), so player \(1\) wins with probability \(A/(A+B)\), and substituting back gives the unique positive solution \(x_1=A^2B/(A+B)^2\), \(x_2=AB^2/(A+B)^2\).

In the litigation contest, write \(x_1=me_R\) and \(x_2=e_O\). Then \(R\)’s payoff is \(v_Rx_1/(x_1+x_2)-x_1/m\), which is \(1/m\) times \(mv_Rx_1/(x_1+x_2)-x_1\), so \(R\) behaves as player \(1\) with \(A=mv_R\), and \(O\) behaves as player \(2\) with \(B=v\). Hence \(p_R=mv_R/(mv_R+v)\), which is the first expression. With reimbursement at rate \(\lambda\), \(O\)’s payoff is \(vx_2/(x_1+x_2)-(1-\lambda)x_2\), which is \((1-\lambda)\) times the payoff of a player with valuation \(B=v/(1-\lambda)\) and unit cost; substituting gives the second expression. With \(m=1\) the second expression equals \(1/2\) exactly when \((1+\theta(n-1))(1-\lambda)=1\), which gives the stated \(\lambda\). Finally, \(p_R\) is strictly increasing in \(1+\theta(n-1)\), which is nondecreasing in \(n\) and \(\theta\), strictly increasing in \(n\) when \(\theta>0\), and strictly increasing in \(\theta\) when \(n>1\). ◻

Repeat litigants win more often than one-shot litigants with equally good cases whenever rulings set precedent, because a repeat litigant bids for future cases as well as the case at hand. Legal aid removes the advantage only when the subsidy to the one-shotter matches the share of the repeat litigant’s stake that comes from future cases, \(\lambda=\theta(n-1)/[1+\theta(n-1)]\). Since that share rises with the repeat litigant’s caseload and with the precedential weight of rulings, modest legal-aid budgets leave repeat players ahead in forums whose rulings bind later cases. Galanter (1974) identifies further advantages, including expertise, economies of scale and informal relations with court personnel, which add to the precedent effect modeled here and raise the subsidy needed to equalize access.

The model gives one reason why resource shares buy favorable rulings, a relation Part I measures for every pillar (Long 2026b), and it locates the remedy in the access component \(q^{\mathrm{acc}}_t\). It is also the core of \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{W}}\): workers, tenants and consumers are usually one-shotters facing repeat players, which is the judicial form of the source’s hypothesis about worker voice (Largo 2026).

5 Comparative analysis across six regime types

5.1 Coding conventions

The comparison codes each regime qualitatively from the historical record, and the series reports numerical values of \(H\), \(Q\), \(U_t\) and \(\mathcal{I}_t\) only under a preregistered coding protocol (Largo 2026). Each regime type is therefore compared on five questions: which institutions hold the judicial function; how concentrated control over final determinations is (\(H_{\mathsf{J},t}\)) and how contestable it is (\(\mathbf{q}_t\)); how strongly the judicial function is coupled to the other five pillars; what preserves accountability; and which capture episodes and checks are documented.

The six types are ideal types, and real polities mix them. The United States is both a market-capitalist society and a secular democracy; Sweden is both a social democracy and a secular democracy. The comparison assigns each case to the type whose distinctive pathway it best illustrates. The market-capitalist section concentrates on the channel from private resources to courts, and the secular-democracy section on the design of the relation between courts and elected bodies.

5.2 Market-capitalist societies

In market-capitalist societies the judicial function is held by courts of general jurisdiction staffed by appointed or elected judges, by a large private legal profession, by administrative tribunals and by private arbitration. The United States is the principal illustration. Federal judges are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, many state judges are elected, and since v. Madison (1803) courts have reviewed the constitutionality of statutes. Hamilton’s defense of life tenure in Federalist No. 78 rests on the claim that “the interpretation of the laws is the proper and peculiar province of the courts” (Hamilton 1788).

Concentration has two levels. Across ordinary litigation, control over final determinations is spread across thousands of judges, so \(H_{\mathsf{J},t}\) is low. For constitutional questions a single court holds the last word, and concentration at the apex is high. Disclosure is high, because opinions are published with reasons. Independence is protected by tenure and salary guarantees for federal judges and is weaker for elected state judges who must raise campaign funds. Compliance by government with adverse rulings is high, and Gibson and coauthors attribute part of it to diffuse public support (Gibson et al. 2003). Access is the weakest link. Litigation is costly, and the advantage of organized repeat players is the core of Galanter’s argument (Galanter 1974).

The distinctive coupling runs from resources to adjudication, the pair \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{J}}\) that Part I treats (Long 2026b). Three channels are documented. Repeat players who can invest in rule-shaping litigation obtain favorable precedents (Proposition 4.3). Rights litigation depends on a support structure of funded organizations (Epp 1998). Campaign spending in judicial elections ties litigants to judges directly.

Judicial doctrine also shapes the channel from resources to rule-making. In United v. FEC (2010) the Supreme Court held the federal ban on independent expenditures by corporations and unions unconstitutional. The ruling changed the rules governing the path from \(\mathsf{F}\) to \(\mathsf{P}\), so the judicial pillar acted as a gatekeeper between two others. It is coupling, not capture: the decision was reached through the ordinary procedure, and capture requires privileged access and a weakened check.

The clearest documented episode of capture risk is v. A. T. Massey Coal Co. (2009). While Massey’s appeal of a large jury verdict was pending, Massey’s chairman spent about three million dollars supporting Brent Benjamin’s 2004 campaign for the West Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals. Benjamin won, declined to recuse himself, and joined the majority that reversed the verdict. The United States Supreme Court held that “in all the circumstances of this case, due process requires recusal.” Under Definition 2.7 the facts establish privileged access and a threat to independence, but the Court did not find actual bias; it found a serious risk of it. The episode documents a capture risk and a check that operated through appeal, which is the pattern Proposition 2.8 predicts when the access and independence links of the review chain hold.

Outside the resource channel, market-capitalist adjudication is loosely coupled to the other pillars, with one exception in production. The coupling with legitimation runs through law schools, the bar and the public reputation of courts, professional institutions that no single coalition commands. The coupling with coercion is moderate: prosecutors decide which disputes reach the courts, and the enforcement of judgments depends on executive agencies, as Hamilton observed (Hamilton 1788). The exception is the arbitration clause in employment and consumer contracts, which moves disputes from public courts to private forums whose awards set no public precedent. Firms that draft such clauses are repeat players in Galanter’s sense (Galanter 1974), so the clauses raise \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{W}}\) on terms set by one side.

5.3 Communist party-states

In communist party-states the judicial function is held by courts, by the procuracy, by party bodies that oversee legal work, and by a bar organized under state discipline. Harold Berman’s interpretation of Soviet law described a procuracy that combined criminal prosecution with general supervision of the legality of administrative acts (Berman 1963). That combination puts prosecution and legality review in one organ, so \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) is high by design. Eugene Huskey’s history of the Soviet bar traces how advocates were organized into collegia subject to state control between 1917 and 1939 (Huskey 1986). In the People’s Republic of China, Lubman’s study of legal reform after Mao describes courts that operate inside a structure of party supervision, with judicial decisions in sensitive cases subject to review by bodies outside the bench (Lubman 1999).

Concentration depends on the type of case. For ordinary civil and commercial disputes, determinations are made by many courts with little party involvement, and concentration is low. For politically salient cases, the last word rests with party organs, and concentration at the level of the ruling coalition is high. This is the two-tier structure of Definition 2.6. The appointment of judges and procurators through the party’s personnel system makes \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) high, a coupling Part II codes through its appointment channel. Disclosure is limited for political cases. Access has grown in some areas: Peerenboom documents administrative litigation in which citizens sue government agencies (Peerenboom 2002). Compliance by the ruling coalition with adverse determinations is low in the political tier, so ruler-boundness is low.

Ginsburg and Moustafa’s list of authoritarian court functions fits the party-state well: courts discipline lower officials, resolve commercial disputes and give legal form to repression (Ginsburg and Moustafa 2008). Peter Solomon’s study of Soviet criminal justice under Stalin documents courts that operated under campaign directives and pressure from party and state bodies, so that the volume and severity of prosecution followed political priorities (Solomon 1996). That is the strongest documented case of judicial capture in the sense of Definition 2.7: privileged direction of determinations by the party, the elimination of independent review, and material evidence in the archival record linking directives to outcomes.

The party-state also contains checks, and they are documented. Kathryn Hendley’s study of labor law in the late Soviet period examines how far law mattered in employment relations as legal reform began (Hendley 1996). Peerenboom argues that Chinese reform produced a thin rule of law in which legal rules increasingly bind officials in ordinary matters (Peerenboom 2002). Minzner identifies a reversal after the mid-2000s, in which the party shifted emphasis from courts toward mediation, petitioning and the maintenance of stability, and retreated from legal professionalism (Minzner 2011). The trajectory shows that the coupling between rule-making and adjudication in a party-state is a variable that leaders adjust, not a fixed property of the regime. The insurance model of Proposition 4.1 predicts this: a ruling party that expects to stay in power gains little from independent courts.

The party-state also couples adjudication to legitimation and production, because it treats law as an instrument of education and of economic administration. Berman described Soviet law as parental, in the sense that courts were charged with educating citizens in the values of the regime as well as deciding their disputes (Berman 1963), which raises \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\). The same legal order handled labor disputes and disputes between state enterprises, so that the enforcement of plan discipline passed through adjudicative bodies (Berman 1963; Hendley 1996), which raises \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{W}}\).

5.4 Social democracy and democratic socialism

In social democracies the judicial function is spread across ordinary courts, specialized administrative and labor courts, ombudsmen, and parliamentary bodies that review the constitutionality of legislation. The Nordic countries illustrate a model of restrained judicial review. Sweden’s Instrument of Government directs that a court which finds that a provision conflicts with a rule of fundamental law shall not apply it (Sweden 1974). A reform in 2010 removed an earlier requirement that the conflict be manifest before a court could set a statute aside (Jonsson Cornell et al. 2016).

Finland’s constitution assigns primary review of legislative proposals to Parliament’s Constitutional Law Committee, and allows courts to give primacy to the Constitution only when applying an act would be in evident conflict with it (Finland 1999). Constitutional interpretation is thus shared between a parliamentary committee and the courts, which raises \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) by design. Tushnet’s weak-form review describes this arrangement (Tushnet 2008).

Germany shows a different social-market pattern. Its Federal Constitutional Court exercises strong-form review, and Vanberg’s study shows that its ability to obtain compliance depends on public support and on the visibility of the government’s response (Vanberg 2005). Stone Sweet’s account of constitutional courts as co-legislators draws heavily on the German and French experience (Stone Sweet 2000). The social-democratic family therefore contains both the weakest and some of the strongest forms of constitutional review among democracies.

Concentration of ordinary adjudication is low. Disclosure is high, and the ombudsman adds a channel of review outside the courts. Sweden’s Parliamentary Ombudsmen are elected by the Riksdag to supervise the application of laws in public activities (Sweden 1974). Access is supported by legal aid and by the low cost of administrative appeals. Independence is protected by constitutional prohibitions on interference: the Instrument of Government provides that neither the Riksdag nor any public authority may determine how a court shall adjudicate an individual case (Sweden 1974). Compliance by government is high.

The distinctive coupling is \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{W}}\), through corporatist labor adjudication. The Swedish Labour Court sits with neutral members and with members representing employer and employee interests, most of whom are appointed on the nomination of the principal labor-market organizations, and its judgment is final (Arbetsdomstolen 2026). This design gives the organized parties of production a place inside the judicial function. It also creates a recognizable risk: a forum whose members are nominated by organizations may favor those organizations over outsiders. The European Court of Human Rights addressed that risk for a related Swedish tribunal in v. Sweden (1989), finding a violation of the right to an independent and impartial tribunal when lay assessors in a housing and tenancy court were nominated by organizations whose interests ran against the applicant’s. The European Commission of Human Rights later declined to find a violation in a Labour Court case in which the nominating organizations had no common interest opposed to the applicant’s (v. Sweden 1990). The two rulings mark the line between coupling and capture with some precision: organizational nomination is compatible with impartiality unless the nominators share an interest in the outcome.

Bo Rothstein’s study of Swedish social reform emphasizes that the implementation of universal programs depends on bureaucratic organization and on citizens’ trust in impartial administration (Rothstein 1996). That is the legitimation coupling \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\) in its social-democratic form: the authority of courts and ombudsmen rests on a shared belief in impartial application. We found no documented case of systematic judicial capture by an economic coalition in the Nordic sources reviewed. The risks identified in the literature are of another kind: legislative supremacy weakens the counter-majoritarian check when a governing majority is durable, and corporatist adjudication can close the forum to actors outside the organized parties.

5.5 Islamic states

Islamic states derive political legitimacy from religious law, but the institutions that interpret it have varied widely across schools, dynasties and centuries. Hallaq describes the classical arrangement as a division of labor (Hallaq 2009). Rulers appointed qadis to decide cases. Jurists who were not state officials developed the law, and muftis issued nonbinding opinions, fatwas, that litigants and judges consulted (Masud et al. 1996). Rulers also held a jurisdiction for the redress of grievances, the mazalim, which al-Mawardi’s eleventh-century treatise on government discusses alongside the qadi’s office (al-Mawardi 1996). Hallaq’s further claim that the jurists’ independent authority confined rulers to administrative regulation is a contested thesis (Hallaq 2009; Hallaq 2013). Sami Zubaida emphasizes the long interplay of state power and religious law (Zubaida 2003), and Joseph Schacht’s older synthesis, which presents the classical law as largely detached from practice, is read critically by later scholarship (Schacht 1964).

Court records contradict the older image of Islamic adjudication as unpredictable. Max Weber used the term “kadi justice” for an ideal type of adjudication guided by substantive considerations of the individual case rather than by general rules (Weber 1978). Haim Gerber tested that characterization against Ottoman court records and rejected it for the courts he examined (Gerber 1994). Ronald Jennings’s study of the seventeenth-century court of Kayseri shows regular procedure, extensive records and wide use by ordinary inhabitants (Jennings 1978).

Ottoman law also shows a ruler’s legislation reconciled with jurists’ law through interpretation rather than by command. Colin Imber’s study of Ebu’s-su’ud, the Ottoman chief mufti in the mid-sixteenth century, shows sultanic law, the kanun, brought into harmony with jurists’ law through juristic opinion (Imber 1997).

Concentration in the classical and Ottoman pattern was moderate. Qadis were numerous and appointed by the ruler, which made \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) substantial, but the law they applied was developed by a scholarly class with its own institutions of training, the madrasas that George Makdisi studied (Makdisi 1981). The same scholars taught, issued opinions and judged, so the legitimation coupling \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\) was high through the ulama. Plurality of schools and the availability of independent muftis supplied contestability: a litigant could seek an opinion from a jurist outside the court.

Saudi Arabia couples uncodified juristic interpretation to royal appointment and royal enforcement. Its Basic Law of Governance declares the judiciary an independent authority subject to no authority other than the Islamic Shari’ah and requires courts to apply the Shari’ah as drawn from the Qur’an and the Sunna. The same law provides that judges are appointed and their service terminated by royal order on the proposal of the supreme judicial council, assigns the implementation of judgments to the King or his deputy, and declares the King the ultimate source of the state’s authorities (Saudi Arabia 1992). Frank Vogel’s study describes a judiciary that applies largely uncodified Islamic law through judges’ own interpretation, alongside royal regulations and the Board of Grievances as an administrative jurisdiction (Vogel 2000). In this design \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) runs through royal appointment, and \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) through royal enforcement of judgments.

Iran’s constitution builds judicial review into rule-making by assigning the interpretation of all legislation to a council of jurists. The jurists of the Guardian Council decide whether all laws and regulations conform to Islamic criteria (Iran 1989). The Council has twelve members: six jurists in Islamic law selected by the Leader, and six jurists elected by the parliament from among nominees of the head of the judiciary. Every bill passed by parliament goes to the Council, which must review it within ten days. Islamic compatibility is decided by a majority of the six religious jurists, constitutional compatibility by a majority of all members. The Council also supervises elections. Hirschl treats Iran as a leading case of constitutional theocracy, in which religious interpretation is constitutionalized and assigned to a council (Hirschl 2010).

The ordinary judiciary is tied to the same apex. The Leader appoints the head of the judiciary for five years. The constitution also provides that judges cannot be removed except after trial and proof of guilt, and that citizens may seek annulment of unlawful regulations in the Court of Administrative Justice (Iran 1989).

The Iranian design concentrates interpretation at the apex and couples it to rule-making and legitimation. The Leader’s appointments reach both the Guardian Council and the judiciary, and the Council’s religious jurists decide whether legislation is valid. Asghar Schirazi documents repeated conflict between parliament and the Guardian Council in the 1980s, which led to the creation of the Expediency Council to resolve deadlocks between them (Schirazi 1997). Wilfried Buchta describes the Council’s use of its electoral supervision to screen candidates (Buchta 2000). The vetting of bills and the screening of candidates are rule-making acts, a veto over legislation and over entry, and Part II codes them that way (Long 2026e). The judgments on which they rest, whether a bill conforms to Islamic criteria and to the Constitution, are interpretive determinations. One body performing both kinds of act is fusion, with high \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\).

Fusion is not yet capture. A capture finding under Definition 2.7 would require evidence that particular screening decisions followed privileged access and had material effect, and no such coding is attempted here. The system also contains checks: senior clerical authorities outside the state, whose plurality Muhammad Qasim Zaman documents across Muslim societies (Zaman 2002), and the constitutional provisions on judicial tenure and administrative review.

5.6 Catholic monarchies in early modern Europe

In the Catholic monarchies of early modern Europe the judicial function was divided among royal courts, the courts of the Church, the Inquisition, municipal and seigneurial courts, and, in the Papal State, tribunals under a ruler who was also head of the Church. Berman’s account of the papal reforms of the late eleventh century places the origin of this plurality in the separation of an ecclesiastical legal order from the secular ones (Berman 1983). James Brundage and Richard Helmholz describe the canon law and the church courts that applied it to marriage, testaments, clerical discipline and sin (Brundage 1995; Helmholz 1996). Overlapping jurisdictions produced constant conflict over which court could hear a case, and that conflict was itself a check: litigants could sometimes choose their forum, and each jurisdiction watched the others.

In Habsburg Castile the royal chanceries at Valladolid and Granada heard appeals from across the kingdom, and Kagan’s study documents a large volume of litigation by ordinary subjects in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (Kagan 1981). Access was therefore substantial for those who could pay. The Spanish Inquisition, a tribunal under royal control that judged offenses against the faith, coupled adjudication with the legitimation pillar as tightly as any institution in this comparison. Henry Kamen’s revisionist history argues that its scale and severity were smaller than its later reputation suggested (Kamen 1997), and the quantitative studies collected by Gustav Henningsen and John Tedeschi make the trial records available for testing such claims (Henningsen and Tedeschi 1986). James Given’s study of the medieval inquisition in Languedoc shows inquisitors using records and interrogation as techniques of power (Given 1997). Inquisitorial courts handed those they condemned to death to the secular authorities for execution, so the coercion coupling \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) ran through the royal arm (Kamen 1997).

In the Crown of Aragon the Justicia was an officer charged with defending the kingdom’s laws against royal encroachment. J. H. Elliott’s history of imperial Spain recounts how Philip II’s pursuit of his former secretary Antonio Pérez, who had taken refuge under Aragonese jurisdiction, led to unrest in 1591 and to the execution of the Justicia (Elliott 1963). The episode is a documented case of a ruler overriding a jurisdictional check by force, in which the coercion pillar removed a judicial constraint on the political one.

In Bourbon France, venal officeholding gave the parlements independence from the crown and tied adjudication to private wealth. Judicial offices were purchased and heritable property, so the crown could not remove judges without compensating them (Doyle 1996). That security allowed the Parlement of Paris to remonstrate against royal edicts. The crown answered with the formal session called the lit de justice, which compelled registration, and in 1771 with Chancellor Maupeou’s suppression and reconstruction of the parlements, which was reversed in 1774 (Swann 1995). Registration and remonstrance are rule-making acts in the series’ classification (Long 2026e); the parlements’ judicial power lay in their appellate jurisdiction.

In that capacity the parlements contested the Church’s authority. Dale Van Kley shows them intervening in the 1750s against clergy who refused sacraments to Jansenists (Van Kley 1996), a case in which the judicial pillar checked the legitimation pillar. The property right that insulated judges from the monarch also entrenched a corporate interest in the offices themselves (Doyle 1996), a coupling of adjudication to resources (\(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{J}}\)). David Bell shows the Paris barristers acquiring a political voice in the same period (Bell 1994).

The Papal State fused functions at its apex. Paolo Prodi described the pope as both spiritual head and temporal prince, “one body and two souls” in his subtitle (Prodi 1987). Fosi’s study of papal justice between 1500 and 1750 shows a system of tribunals and a practice of petitioning the pope for justice that gave subjects a route around local courts (Fosi 2011). Petitioning raised access while concentrating final authority in the ruler. Across the Catholic monarchies, concentration was moderate, \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\) was high in ecclesiastical and inquisitorial courts, \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) varied with the crown’s success in subordinating rival jurisdictions, and contestability depended heavily on the competition among jurisdictions.

5.7 Secular democracies

In secular democracies the judicial function is held by constitutional or supreme courts, ordinary and administrative courts, judicial councils, and international courts. The defining design question is the relation between courts and elected bodies. Constitutional courts in France and Germany became co-legislators whose anticipated rulings shape legislation (Stone Sweet 2000). Hirschl’s hegemonic preservation thesis holds that constitutionalization in Israel, Canada, New Zealand and South Africa transferred power to courts at moments when established elites faced electoral threats (Hirschl 2001; Hirschl 2004). The thesis is contested on timing and motive, and it is stated below as a hypothesis with a falsifier.

Rafael La Porta and coauthors coded constitutional provisions on judicial independence and constitutional review across countries and related them to economic and political freedom (La Porta et al. 2004). Lars Feld and Stefan Voigt distinguished de jure from de facto independence and found that the de facto measure, not the de jure measure, was associated with economic growth in their sample (Feld and Voigt 2003). The distinction is the central measurement problem of this Part: constitutional guarantees are not the same as observed independence.

Concentration of ordinary adjudication is low in secular democracies, and concentration at the constitutional apex is high. Disclosure and access are generally high. Independence and compliance depend on the political environment. Vanberg’s public-support mechanism explains why governments comply with courts whose rulings voters observe (Vanberg 2005), and Whittington’s account explains why fragmented coalitions sustain judicial review (Whittington 2005). When a governing coalition becomes dominant, both mechanisms weaken.

The documented capture episodes in this regime type are court-packing and court-curbing by elected governments. Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way identify the judiciary as one of the arenas in which competitive authoritarian incumbents tilt the field against opponents (Levitsky and Way 2010). Argentina provides a documented case. In 1990 the government of Carlos Menem enlarged the Supreme Court from five to nine members, and the resulting court became known for an “automatic majority” that favored the government’s interests (Hevia 2008). Helmke’s study of the same court shows the reverse movement: as the government’s hold weakened, justices began to rule against it (Helmke 2005). The two observations together illustrate both halves of the series’ thesis. The judicial function persisted throughout; its coupling to the political pillar rose and fell with the political calendar.

The couplings \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\), \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) and \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{W}}\) are generally lower in secular democracies, because law schools, prosecutors and labor organizations are governed separately from the courts. Party-dominated judicial councils, prosecutors who answer to the executive and underfunded legal aid each raise one of them.

Every regime type has the judicial function, as Theorem 3.3 requires. The types differ little in the concentration of ordinary adjudication, which is low almost everywhere. 6 Mechanisms, failure modes and checks

6.1 Judicial capture

Definition 2.7 separates three things that public debate often merges. A judge appointed by a party is not thereby captured, because appointment is the ordinary channel through which every regime staffs its courts. A ruling that favors a powerful litigant is not thereby captured, because the powerful litigant may have had the better case. Capture requires a privilege, a weakened check and an outcome linked to the privilege. Caperton establishes the first two conditions for one case and leaves the third unresolved. Stalin-era criminal justice establishes all three for a whole system.

6.2 Failure modes

Interpretation fails as a check in four ways, one for each link of the review chain, and the formal results of Sections 2 and 4 identify the conditions under which each failure occurs. Disclosure fails when proceedings are sealed, reasons go unpublished or financial ties between litigants and judges stay hidden; the chain then never starts. Access fails when one side can outspend the other, and Proposition 4.3 shows that repeat players win more often for that reason even when merits are equal. Independence fails when the incumbent expects to stay in power, and Proposition 4.1 shows that such an incumbent gains nothing from granting it. Compliance fails when the governing coalition ignores adverse rulings, and Proposition 2.8 shows that no strength elsewhere in the chain repairs that failure. A regime can also confine all four failures to the politically salient tier, where caseload statistics do not see them (Proposition 2.9).

Each failure has a characteristic check: publication duties and recusal rules for disclosure; legal aid, collective litigation and limits on forced arbitration for access; tenure security, insulated appointment and plural forums for independence; and, for compliance, public support that makes defiance costly (Vanberg 2005) together with enforcement agencies that answer to courts. Section 5 documents checks of each kind in every regime type.

6.3 Coupling pathways and checks

The legitimation pathway runs through the body that produces authoritative readings of the law and the beliefs that support them. When the same coalition trains the jurists, licenses the lawyers, appoints the judges and controls the doctrine that justifies the regime, \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\) is high. The ulama in classical Islamic societies, the canonists and inquisitors of Catholic Europe, and the party’s legal theorists in socialist states each occupied this position in different degrees. High \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\) is not capture. It becomes capture when interpretive dissent loses its institutional footing, so that no independent jurist, school or faculty can contest the authoritative reading. The historical check was plurality: of schools in Islamic law, of jurisdictions in Catholic Europe, of faculties and bar associations in modern democracies.

The coercion pathway runs through the organs that bring cases and enforce judgments. When the same organ prosecutes, supervises legality and directs judges, as the Soviet procuracy and party legal organs could, \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) is high and the judicial pillar loses the ability to restrain coercion. The check is separation of prosecution from adjudication and of both from the police, with judicial control over detention. The sanctioning apparatus belongs to Part V, which shows that sanctions can deter rule violations equally well whether or not they also fall more heavily on a coalition’s opponents (Long 2026a). Independent adjudication is the check that ties sanctions to conduct rather than allegiance, and by Proposition 3.6 a judiciary with strong public legitimacy needs less coercive backing to do so.

The production pathway runs through access. When workers, tenants and consumers cannot afford to litigate while employers, landlords and lenders litigate routinely, courts apply rules that the organized side has shaped. Beyond the access checks listed above, the Swedish Labour Court shows that corporatist representation of producers inside the court is a second kind of check, and the Langborger line of cases shows its limit (v. Sweden 1989; v. Sweden 1990).

Each archive carries selection problems. Records survive unevenly, and the cases that reached a court were already selected by the cost of bringing them.

Measurement invariance is the main threat to comparison. An expert’s judgment that a court is independent may mean different things for a constitutional court in a democracy and for a qadi court in a sixteenth-century city. The source recommends testing invariance and using regime-specific models when it fails (Largo 2026). For the judicial layer the safest comparisons are within regime types over time, and across regime types on narrowly defined events, such as the execution of adverse judgments against state bodies.

7.2 Hypotheses

The following hypotheses refine the source’s general hypotheses for the judicial pillar. Each states a falsifier. None is tested in this paper.

Judicial independence, measured de facto, is greater where control of government is more fragmented or electoral turnover is more likely (Proposition 4.1). Falsifier: no association between de facto independence and measures of electoral uncertainty in a country panel, after controlling for income and legal origin.

The empowerment of constitutional courts follows declines in the electoral prospects of established elites (Hirschl 2004). Falsifier: the timing of constitutional empowerment is unrelated to prior electoral decline across cases of new constitutionalism.

In party-states and competitive authoritarian regimes, contestability is higher for commercial and ordinary civil cases than for politically salient cases (Proposition 2.9). Falsifier: rates of rulings against state bodies and of compliance with them are equal across case types once case merits are controlled.

In comparable cases repeat litigants win more often than one-shotters, and the gap narrows when legal aid expands (Proposition 4.3). Falsifier: no gap after controlling for case merit, or no narrowing after an expansion of legal aid.

Where religious jurists hold interpretive authority independent of state salaries and appointments, the share of law produced by ruler’s decree is smaller than where jurists are state employees (Hallaq 2009). Falsifier: no difference in the share of decree-made law between periods or states that differ in jurists’ independence.

Venal judicial offices protect judges against removal by the crown and also entrench corporate privilege (Doyle 1996). Falsifier: the frequency of remonstrance and of successful resistance to royal legislation does not differ between venal and non-venal courts or periods.

The integration \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\) and the unaccountable concentration of the judicial layer rise together when judges are appointed and removable by the executive or the ruling party, and judges’ reputational concerns limit the rise (Helmke 2005). Falsifier: changes in appointment and removal rules leave rates of rulings against the government unchanged.

The probability that a documented capture link persists after disclosure is predicted better by the weakest component of the contestability vector than by an additive index (Proposition 2.8). Falsifier: an additive index predicts persistence as well as or better than the minimum component in out-of-sample tests.

HJ1, HJ3 and HJ7 can be tested with existing cross-national data and HJ4 with case-level data on merits; HJ2, HJ5 and HJ6 await archival coding on comparable terms.

8 Judgment in the Book of Revelation

The series reads Revelation typologically. The beast is a recurring pattern of power that claims ultimate allegiance, and Babylon is the idolatrous city of every age. The reading does not identify any present-day government, party, institution, technology or person with the beast or its mark. Futurist identifications have a long record of failure, which Robert Fuller and Bernard McGinn document (Fuller 1995; McGinn 1994), and historicist identifications of the papacy or of any other institution with the beast belong to the history of polemic, not to analysis. The theological interpretation is the project’s own and is offered as an analogy that aids comparison, not as an exegetical result. Part VII owns the shared reading; this section applies it to the judicial pillar.

Revelation 13 describes political authority, war-making, worship and economic exclusion, but no court. The dragon gives the first beast “his power, and his seat, and great authority” (Rev 13:2), and the second beast “causeth all, both small and great, rich and poor, free and bond, to receive a mark in their right hand, or in their foreheads: And that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark” (Rev 13:16 and 13:17) (King James Bible 1611). Those who would not worship the image are to be killed (Rev 13:15). In the vocabulary of this paper, the passage describes an order in which a person’s standing is fixed by a mark of allegiance rather than by the application of a general rule to a particular case. There is no procedure by which the excluded can contest their exclusion. Typologically, the judicial function has been absorbed into the legitimation and coercion functions, so that \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{I}}\) and \(C_{\mathsf{J}\mathsf{C}}\) are at their maximum and contestability is zero. It is the limiting case of capture in Proposition 2.8, with every link broken: sanctions follow allegiance alone.

The rest of the book does not imagine a world without judgment. It relocates judgment. The souls under the altar cry out, “How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?” (Rev 6:10). Their complaint is the absence of a just forum. The response comes in a refrain: “true and righteous are thy judgments” (Rev 16:7), and “true and righteous are his judgments: for he hath judged the great whore” (Rev 19:2). After the fall of Babylon, John sees thrones, and “judgment was given unto them,” to those who had not worshipped the beast or received its mark (Rev 20:4) (King James Bible 1611). The judicial function passes from the imperial order to a court that the text presents as just.

The commentators agree that the beasts had a Roman referent, which is the historical anchor that the typological reading generalizes. Richard Bauckham reads Rome as the paradigm of the beast and frames the book’s conflict as one between worship of the beast and faithful witness (Bauckham 1993). Adela Yarbro Collins reads the book as a response to a crisis that its audience perceived, whose rhetoric works through catharsis (Collins 1984). Craig Koester’s commentary situates the beasts in the imperial politics and civic cults of Roman Asia (Koester 2014). J. Nelson Kraybill shows how participation in commerce in the province was bound up with the imperial cult (Kraybill 1996), which gives the mark’s economic exclusion its historical referent. Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza’s reading of the book as a work about justice and judgment puts the judicial theme at its center (Schüssler Fiorenza 1985).

The typology maps onto the series’ two propositions. The text presupposes Proposition A: judgment is a permanent function, and the question is who exercises it. The warning attaches to Proposition B: an order in which allegiance replaces adjudication, and in which those excluded have no forum. The typology does not tell us which regimes approach that limit. Only the measurement program of Section 7 can do that, and its results will not track the boundaries of religions or ideologies.

9 Objections and scope conditions

The first objection is that the necessity result is empty, because a function defined so broadly that a lottery counts as a court must exist everywhere. The reply is that the definitions exclude real possibilities. A collective in which every rule application is uncontested, or in which every residual dispute ends in force, departure or abandonment, has no judicial function under them, and Proposition 3.4 shows that such a collective can persist when exit is free. The theorem’s content lies in Assumption 3.2, an empirical claim about the cost of feud and exit, and societies in which exit is costless are the test cases. The result predicts thin adjudication in them.

The second objection comes from legal realism and its descendants in political science: courts are political actors whose decisions reflect judges’ preferences, so the distinction between making rules and interpreting them is a fiction. Shapiro’s own account of courts as political actors is the strongest version (Shapiro 1981). The reply is that the split between rule-making and interpretation in this series is functional, not motivational. Whatever moves judges, the activity of fixing what a general rule requires in a particular case is distinct from the activity of making the general rule. When the two are performed by the same actor, the series records fusion through \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{J}}\). Realism about judicial motives strengthens the case for measuring the couplings; it does not dissolve the function.

The third objection is that comparing qadi courts, inquisitorial tribunals and constitutional courts imposes modern categories on institutions that understood themselves differently. The reply has two parts. The comparison concerns the function each performed, which the institutions’ own records allow us to observe: who brought cases, who decided them, how the decisions were enforced and whether they could be challenged. And the comparison does not import modern evaluative standards into the coding. Weber’s ideal type of “kadi justice” is a cautionary example of what happens when a comparative category is applied without testing it against court records, and Gerber’s test of that type against Ottoman records shows how the error is corrected (Weber 1978; Gerber 1994).

Four scope conditions limit the results. The necessity theorem applies to collectives with recurrent residual disputes and costly self-help and exit, and it says nothing about the quality of adjudication. The formal models are stylized, and their assumptions can fail, especially the persistence of chosen independence in the insurance model and the contest form of the repeat-player model. The comparative codings are qualitative and rest on secondary scholarship, so they illustrate pathways rather than measure them. The hypotheses are stated and not tested.

10 Conclusion

Every governance system that persists with general rules and costly exit has a procedure for deciding particular disputes that neither party controls. That is the judicial pillar in its minimal form, and it is present among the Nuer and in the Parlement of Paris, in the qadi court of Kayseri and in the Swedish Labour Court. The function is universal; its holders, concentration and couplings are not.

Judicial power becomes capture when privileged access to determinations weakens a link in the chain of disclosure, access, independence and compliance, and the outcomes follow the privilege. Because the chain is multiplicative, a single broken link suffices, and additive indices can hide it. Each of the six regime types contains documented episodes of capture and documented checks against it. The ideology that legitimates a regime does not settle whether its courts bind its rulers. What settles it is the strength of the weakest link, which is a measurable property of particular institutions at particular times.
