### Summary Assessment

This paper develops the rule-making layer ($\pP$) of a six-pillar model of governance. Its core analytical contribution is a clean separation between the functional necessity of rule making (Theorem 3.4, proved from axioms of preference plurality, environmental change, and persistence) and the concentration of rule-making power (Proposition 2.5 and Corollary 2.6, showing that necessity is compatible with any Herfindahl-Hirschman concentration $H^{\pP} \in \{1/n, \dots, 1\}$). The paper pairs this separation with three comparative-static models of capture and its checks (Section 6), a qualitative comparison across six regime types (Section 5), falsifiable hypotheses (Section 7), and a typological reading of Revelation 13 and 17 (Section 8).

While the central separation argument is sound and the proofs of Proposition 2.5 and Lemma 3.2 are clean, the manuscript has substantive mathematical and definitional gaps in Section 2 and Section 6, structural forward-referencing between the empirical comparisons and the formal mechanisms, heavy reliance on an unpublished companion manuscript ("in preparation") for foundational definitions, and recurring prose and LaTeX defects.

### Evaluation by Criterion

#### 1. Mathematical Correctness
* **Proofs of Proposition 2.5 and Lemmas 3.1–3.2 (Lines 149–171, 284–300):** The proofs of the Shapley-Shubik properties—including the transposition bijection establishing that a veto player's share weakly dominates every other player's share in Proposition 2.5(e) (Line 170)—and the disjoint-viability argument in Lemma 3.2 (Line 299) are mathematically correct. The worked computation for the Iranian legislative game in Table 2 (Lines 190, 204–205) is also exact ($\varphi = (2/3, 1/6, 1/6)$ and $H^{\pP} = 4/9 + 1/36 + 1/36 = 1/2$).
* **False inference from Definition 2.8 to Proposition 6.3(c) (Lines 229, 605, 610):** Definition 2.8 (Line 229) defines the appointment share $\alpha_{S,\ell}$ using the disjunction:
  $$\alpha_{S,\ell}=\sum_{o\in O_\ell}w_o\,\mathbf{1}[S \text{ can unilaterally appoint or remove the holder of } o].$$
  Proposition 6.3(c) (Line 605), however, states: *"if command runs only through appointment and no listed coalition can fill any office in $O_\ell$ unilaterally, for example because every appointment requires the consent of two bodies controlled by different coalitions, then $C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\pP\ell}=0$."* The proof (Line 610) claims that this hypothesis implies $\alpha_{S,\ell}=0$ by Definition 2.8. That inference fails: a coalition (such as a president) that cannot unilaterally *fill* an office because appointment requires legislative confirmation may still have the constitutional power to unilaterally *remove* the officeholder. Under Definition 2.8, unilateral removal power alone sets the indicator to $1$ and yields $\alpha_{S,\ell} > 0$.
* **Inconsistency between non-additive coalition shares in Section 2.4 and additive coalition shares in Equation (2) (Lines 190, 204–205, 594):** Equation (2) (Line 594) defines the rule-making share of a coherent coalition $S \in \mathcal{S}$ ("groups such as parties, factions, or dynasties that can issue joint directives," Line 592) as the simple sum $s^{\pP}(S) = \sum_{i \in S} s^{\pP}_i$. In cooperative game theory, coalescing players into a bloc that acts jointly changes the simple game and is not additive in Shapley-Shubik shares. Indeed, Section 2.4 (Line 190 and Table 2, Lines 204–205) explicitly demonstrates that merging the Guardian Council and Expediency Council into a single coalition raises their combined Shapley-Shubik share from $1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3$ to $1/2$.
* **Ambiguous normalization of $\lambda_S$ in Proposition 6.3 (Line 601):** In $\kappa_\ell(S) = \lambda_S \alpha_{S,\ell} + \gamma_S$, $\lambda_S \in [0,1]$ is defined as *"the weighted share of decisions in which the appointees of $S$ follow its directives."* For the product $\lambda_S \alpha_{S,\ell}$ to represent the share of total control in pillar $\ell$ exercised according to $S$'s directives via appointment, $\lambda_S$ must be the *conditional* share of decisions among $S$'s appointees (weighted by $w_o / \alpha_{S,\ell}$ for $\alpha_{S,\ell} > 0$), not a share weighted across all $o \in O_\ell$ (which would double-count $\alpha_{S,\ell}$). Furthermore, $\kappa_\ell(S) \in [0,1]$ (Line 592) requires the domain constraint $\lambda_S \alpha_{S,\ell} + \gamma_S \le 1$.
* **Implicit completeness and voting-behavior assumptions in Propositions 3.5(b) and 6.2(b) (Lines 99, 344–354, 569–584):** Definitions 2.1 and 6.1 and Proposition 3.5 require "strict preferences $\succ_i$" without specifying whether $\succ_i$ is a *strict total order* (complete on distinct alternatives, ruling out indifference) or the strict part of a weak preference order $\succeq_i$. If indifference between distinct alternatives $x \neq q$ is permitted, $\{x : x \succ_i q \text{ for all } i \in N_a\}$ is empty when $q$ is *weakly* Pareto optimal rather than merely strongly Pareto optimal (Proposition 3.5(b), Line 347), and Proposition 3.5 requires an explicit behavioral assumption stating whether an indifferent voter assents to $x$ or defaults to $q$.
* **Incomplete specification of the opposition faction's move in Proposition 6.1(b) (Lines 549–557):** Assumption 6.1 (Lines 539–542) specifies simultaneous choices and independent detection probability $\delta$ only for the $n$ members. Proposition 6.1(b) introduces an external opposition faction that can monitor at cost $c$, without stating in the proposition hypothesis that the faction chooses simultaneously with the members, detects extraction independently with probability $\delta$, and collects $\beta\rho$ upon replacement regardless of who detected the rent extraction.

#### 2. Clarity and Completeness
* **Mismatch between multi-alternative collective decisions and binary simple games (Lines 99, 144, 181):** Definition 2.1 (Line 99) defines a collective decision over a set $X$ of at least two alternatives ($|X| \ge 2$). A simple game $\Win$ (Definition 2.4, Line 144), however, is a binary aggregation rule (approving or rejecting a single proposal against a status quo $q$). When $|X| \ge 3$, properness of $\Win$ ($S \in \Win \implies N \setminus S \notin \Win$) only prevents two disjoint coalitions from passing contradictory binary motions simultaneously; it does not specify how an alternative is selected from $X$ without an agenda protocol, nor does it prevent Condorcet cycles when $\Win$ is not a collegial/veto game.
* **Reliance on unpublished companion papers for core definitions (Lines 69, 86–93, 254–256, 592, 894):** Foundational definitions—most notably the formal definition of cross-pillar integration $C_{mn,t}$ (which Line 592 notes is deferred to Part VII, `long2026formalized`, listed on Line 894 as *"Working paper, in preparation"*), the formal proof that Proposition A does not imply Proposition B (Lines 69, 184), and the formal definition of a capture link (Lines 91–93, deferred to Part I)—are not self-contained in this manuscript.

#### 3. Logical Structure
* **Inverted order of empirical regime comparisons (Section 5) and formal mechanisms (Section 6) (Lines 77, 413–624):** The Introduction (Line 77) describes the paper's logic as moving from necessity (Section 3) and concentration (Section 2) to the formal mechanisms of capture (Section 6) and then to the comparative regime analysis (Section 5) that illustrates those mechanisms. In the body of the paper, however, Section 5 precedes Section 6. As a result, Section 5 repeatedly forward-references formal propositions that have not yet been defined or proved—e.g., Line 436 relies on the loyalty parameter $\lambda_S$ from Proposition 6.3 (`\ref{prop:political-power:appointment-bound}`), and Line 480 relies on the veto trade-off from Proposition 6.2 (`\ref{prop:political-power:veto}`). Moving Section 6 before Section 5 resolves these forward dependencies.
* **Redundant and fragmented Section 9 (Lines 684–702):** Section 9 ("Objections and scope conditions") largely repeats points already made in Subsections 2.2, 2.3, 3.2, 5.1, and Section 8. The disclaimer that the paper codes no polity numerically is repeated six times across the manuscript (Lines 59, 79, 195, 418, 495, 697). Moreover, the final four paragraphs of Section 9 (Lines 695, 697, 699, 701) are single-sentence or two-sentence fragments rather than developed paragraphs.

#### 4. LaTeX Quality

* **Residual variable typo in Definition 2.8 (Line 227):** Line 227 warns that *"the letter $n$ is reserved for the size of a collective; see Remark 2.10,"* and then in the very next clause writes *"the appointment share of $S$ in $n$ is"* instead of *"in $\ell$ is."*

Line 680 writes `(Rev.\ 17:12 to 13)` instead of `(Rev.\ 17:12--13)`.

#### 5. Prose Quality Against the House Style
Much of the analytical prose in Sections 1–3 and 6 is crisp and direct. However, the literature review (Section 4), comparative survey (Section 5), and hypothesis framing (Section 7.2) frequently violate the house style in four ways:
1. **Multi-claim "annotated bibliography" paragraphs:** Several paragraphs (e.g., Lines 360, 385, 395, 430, 462, 470) string together five or six disparate author summaries without a single governing claim.
2. **Signposting and meta-prose openings:** Paragraphs open by announcing what a section or literature does rather than stating the substantive claim directly (e.g., Line 75: *"This paper offers such a vocabulary and uses it"*; Line 86: *"The objects in this subsection are defined in the source framework"*; Line 395: *"Autocratic rule making has its own theory"*; Line 470: *"The historical scholarship shows a different distribution of practical control"*; Line 657: *"The hypotheses below refine three of the source framework's general hypotheses"*).
3. **Qualification or literature synthesis before the finding:** In Paragraphs 428 and 430, the main comparative claim appears at the end of the paragraph after a list of mixed citations, rather than opening the paragraph before qualification.
4. **Nominalization and passive chains:** Paragraph 657 relies on dense nominalizations (*"concentration of discretionary benefits among connected actors,"* *"opportunities for effective challenge,"* *"adverse disclosure,"* *"organizational survival"*), and Paragraph 75 chains four passive verbs in five sentences.

### Weakest Three Paragraphs and Model Rewrite

#### Weakest Paragraph 1 (Line 395)
> Autocratic rule making has its own theory. Bueno de Mesquita and coauthors model leaders who must retain a winning coalition drawn from a larger selectorate; when the coalition is small relative to the selectorate, leaders survive by providing private goods to coalition members rather than public goods to all \citep{bdm2003}. Svolik identifies two problems that every dictator faces, sharing power with the elites who could remove him and controlling the masses who could rise against the regime, and shows how parties, legislatures, and rules of succession help solve the first \citep{svolik2012}. Gandhi and Przeworski argue that nominally democratic institutions under autocracy co-opt potential opponents and are associated with longer rule \citep{gandhi2007}, and Magaloni argues that ruling parties make power-sharing promises credible \citep{magaloni2008}. Geddes, Wright, and Frantz provide a dataset that classifies autocracies as party, personalist, military, or monarchic and records how they end \citep{geddes2014}. Gerschewski describes stability in autocracies as resting on legitimation, repression, and co-optation together \citep{gerschewski2013}, which in the series' vocabulary correspond to the legitimation, enforcement, and resource-allocation pillars acting on behalf of rule makers.

*Why it fails the house style:* The opening sentence is empty signposting rather than a substantive claim, and the body concatenates six distinct book and dataset summaries (private goods, intra-elite power sharing, legislative co-optation, party credibility, regime-type datasets, and stability taxonomies) without a single unifying argument.

#### Weakest Paragraph 2 (Line 470)
> The historical scholarship shows a different distribution of practical control. Habsburg Spain was a composite monarchy in which each kingdom kept its own laws and representative institutions, and royal government operated through a system of councils for the separate kingdoms and functions \citep{elliott1963}. Olivares's attempt in the 1620s and 1630s to make the kingdoms share the costs of the monarchy's wars met resistance and contributed to the revolts of Catalonia and Portugal in 1640 \citep{elliott1963,elliott1984}. The episode is a documented check: the constitutional distinctness of the kingdoms limited what the crown could impose. In Bourbon France, royal ordinances had to be registered by the Parlements, which could remonstrate and delay, and the crown could override them only by a formal session in the king's presence. The Estates-General did not meet between 1614 and 1789. Beik's study of Languedoc showed that absolutism worked through collaboration with provincial elites who shared in its revenues \citep{beik1985}, and Collins presents the early modern French state as a negotiated structure with real limits \citep{collins1995}. Root's study of Burgundy shows the crown's rules about village finances reshaping relations between peasants and lords \citep{root1987}.

*Why it fails the house style:* The topic sentence is meta-commentary about scholarship rather than a direct historical claim. The paragraph then mixes Spanish councils, French judicial registration, an unintegrated sentence that points toward absolutist concentration rather than dispersion (*"The Estates-General did not meet between 1614 and 1789"*), provincial fiscal bargaining, and a final sentence on Burgundian village finances that illustrates royal reach rather than limits on royal rule making.

#### Weakest Paragraph 3 (Line 430)
> Coupling to the other pillars is strongest with resource control, through the wealth-to-rules path, and through the structural dependence of rule makers on investment that Lindblom described \citep{lindblom1977}. Coupling to interpretation runs through the appointment of federal judges by the president with Senate confirmation, so no single coalition controls the appointment channel unless it holds both bodies. Accountability is preserved by elections, by the rights of opposition parties, by judicial review, by disclosure rules, and by competition among organized interests, which frequently lobby on opposite sides of the same question \citep{baumgartner2009}. Hacker and Pierson argue that organized interests used this system to shift tax and regulatory rules toward the very wealthy over several decades \citep{hackerpierson2010}; Culpepper's comparison of Europe and Japan shows that business influence is greatest when issues are technical and receive little public attention \citep{culpepper2010}. The regime type thus combines moderate de jure concentration and high formal contestability with an unequal distribution of practical access, and the main open question is how much that access changes rules.

*Why it fails the house style:* This paragraph attempts to cover four distinct analytical topics at once—coupling to $\pF$, coupling to $\pJ$, checks preserving accountability, and empirical studies of policy capture—before ending with a summary of the entire subsection. It relies on passive constructions (*"is strongest,"* *"is preserved"*) and nominalizations (*"structural dependence,"* *"unequal distribution of practical access"*) and buries its synthesizing claim at the end.

#### Model Rewrite (of Line 470)
> In practice, early modern Catholic monarchs shared rule-making power with provincial estates, councils, and courts that could delay or block royal decrees. Habsburg Spain governed through separate councils for kingdoms that kept their own laws and representative assemblies \citep{elliott1963}; when Olivares tried in the 1620s and 1630s to force those kingdoms to share war costs, Catalonia and Portugal revolted \citep{elliott1963,elliott1984}. ### Structured Feedback by Severity

#### Critical Issues
1. **Invalid deduction in Proposition 6.3(c) from Definition 2.8 (Lines 229, 605, 610):**
   * *Issue:* Definition 2.8 (Line 229) sets the indicator in $\alpha_{S,\ell}$ to $1$ whenever coalition $S$ *"can unilaterally appoint **or remove** the holder of $o$."* Proposition 6.3(c) (Line 605) assumes only that *"no listed coalition can **fill** any office in $O_\ell$ unilaterally"* and deduces $\alpha_{S,\ell}=0$ (Line 610). That deduction is invalid whenever a coalition can unilaterally remove an official whose appointment required confirmation by a second body.
   * *Fix:* Either change the hypothesis of Proposition 6.3(c) on Line 605 to *"no listed coalition can unilaterally appoint or remove the holder of any office in $O_\ell$,"* or separate appointment power and removal power in Definition 2.8.
2. **Contradiction between non-additive coalition Shapley-Shubik shares in Section 2.4 and additive coalition shares in Equation (2) (Lines 190, 204–205, 594):**
   * *Issue:* Equation (2) (Line 594) defines the rule-making share of a coherent coalition $S \in \mathcal{S}$ as $s^{\pP}(S) = \sum_{i \in S} s^{\pP}_i$, whereas Section 2.4 (Line 190 and Table 2, Lines 204–205) demonstrates that when actors form a single coalition in a simple game, the coalition's Shapley-Shubik share is not the sum of its members' standalone shares ($1/2 \neq 1/6 + 1/6$).
   * *Fix:* Define $s^{\pP}(S)$ either as the Shapley-Shubik share $\varphi_S(\Win_S)$ in the quotient game where members of $S$ vote as a single bloc, or clarify that nodes $i \in V_t$ already represent the coherent actors and specify under what indicator $s^{\pP}(S)$ is additive.

#### Major Issues
1. **Ambiguous normalization and domain bound in Proposition 6.3 (Lines 592, 601):**
   * *Issue:* On Line 601, $\lambda_S \in [0,1]$ is defined as *"the weighted share of decisions in which the appointees of $S$ follow its directives."* If the weights are $w_o$ summing to $1$ over all $o \in O_\ell$, multiplying $\lambda_S$ by $\alpha_{S,\ell}$ double-weights the appointment share. In addition, $\kappa_\ell(S) = \lambda_S \alpha_{S,\ell} + \gamma_S$ is required to lie in $[0,1]$ (Line 592), which restricts $\gamma_S \in [0, 1 - \lambda_S \alpha_{S,\ell}]$.
   * *Fix:* Define $\lambda_S \in [0,1]$ explicitly as the conditional loyalty rate among $S$'s appointees (weighted by $w_o / \alpha_{S,\ell}$ when $\alpha_{S,\ell} > 0$, and $0$ otherwise), and state $\gamma_S \in [0, 1 - \lambda_S \alpha_{S,\ell}]$.
2. **Incomplete game specification in Proposition 6.1(b) (Lines 549–557):**
   * *Issue:* Proposition 6.1(b) adds an external opposition faction to the monitoring game of Assumption 6.1 without stating the faction's detection probability, move timing relative to members, or whether the faction captures $\beta\rho$ when a member detects the rent extraction.
   * *Fix:* State explicitly in Proposition 6.1(b) (Line 549) that the faction chooses simultaneously with the members, detects extraction independently with probability $\delta$ at cost $c$, and receives $\beta\rho$ whenever the leadership is replaced.
3. **Unstated completeness and voting assumptions in Propositions 3.5(b) and 6.2(b) (Lines 99, 344–354, 569–584):**
   * *Issue:* The text refers to "strict preferences $\succ_i$" without stating whether $\succ_i$ is a strict linear order (excluding indifference between distinct alternatives $x \neq y$) or the strict part of a weak order $\succeq_i$, and Proposition 3.5 leaves implicit that an adult assents to $x$ over $q$ if and only if $x \succ_i q$.
   * *Fix:* Specify in Definition 2.1 (Line 99) that each $\succ_i$ is a strict total order on $X$ (or else distinguish weak and strong Pareto optimality in Proposition 3.5(b)), and state explicitly in Proposition 3.5 (Line 344) that each adult $i \in N_a$ assents to $x$ over $q$ if and only if $x \succ_i q$.
4. **Gap between multi-alternative collective decisions ($|X| \ge 2$) and binary simple games (Lines 99, 144, 181):**
   * *Issue:* A proper simple game $\Win$ (Definition 2.4, Line 144) models binary approval of a proposal against a status quo $q$, whereas Definition 2.1 (Line 99) defines collective choice over arbitrary sets $X$ with $|X| \ge 2$. When $|X| \ge 3$, properness does not prevent Condorcet cycles or determine which alternative in $X$ is pitted against $q$.
   * *Fix:* Clarify in Subsection 2.3 (Lines 140–145) and Corollary 2.6 (Line 181) that the simple-game baseline models binary choice between a proposed replacement and the status quo $q$ (or is paired with an agenda-setting rule when $|X| \ge 3$).
5. **Structural inversion of Sections 5 and 6 (Lines 77, 413–624):**
   * *Issue:* Section 5 (Lines 413–528) relies on formal concepts and comparative statics proved later in Section 6 (Lines 530–624), requiring forward references on Lines 436 and 480 and contradicting the narrative sequence outlined in the Introduction (Line 77).
   * *Fix:* Move Section 6 ("Mechanisms, failure modes and checks") ahead of Section 5 ("Rule making across six regime types") so the formal mechanisms precede the historical comparisons that apply them.
6. **House-style violations in multi-claim paragraphs and signposting (Lines 75, 360, 395, 430, 470, 657, 687–702):**
   * *Issue:* Paragraphs 360, 395, 430, and 470 aggregate multiple unrelated claims or citations without a single governing claim; Paragraphs 75 and 657 consist largely of signposting and nominalizations; and Section 9 (Lines 687–702) repeats earlier sections and ends with four one- or two-sentence stub paragraphs (Lines 695–701).
   * *Fix:* Rewrite Paragraphs 360, 395, 430, and 470 so each defends a single claim stated in its opening sentence; cut the roadmap paragraph on Line 75 and the redundant summary of H1–H3 on Line 657; and consolidate or cut the repetitive disclaimers in Section 9.
