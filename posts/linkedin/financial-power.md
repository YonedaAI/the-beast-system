Who controls the resources? Every governance system must settle conflicting claims on finite goods, and Part I of the Beast System series treats that settlement as the financial pillar of a six-function model of governance.

The approach starts from a scarcity axiom and an explicit assumption that unsettled conflicts carry a positive hazard of dissolution. From these we prove that a collective with no recognized allocation regularity cannot persist, except with probability bounded away from zero. In any collective that does persist, the share of conflicts settled by such a regularity tends to one. The result concerns a function rather than an institution: in foraging bands the regularity is a sharing norm, and control is dispersed close to the minimum concentration the index allows.

Two further results matter for measurement. Necessity places no restriction on how concentrated or contestable the pillar is, so showing that allocation must exist says nothing about whether it is captured. And aggregating subdomains understates concentration by exactly the cross-domain variance of control shares, which means averaging across sectors can hide real concentration. We also derive conditional comparative statics under which resource holders convert wealth into influence over rules.

A comparison of market-capitalist societies, communist party-states, social democracies, Islamic states, Catholic monarchies and secular democracies finds the same function held by different institutions, with documented capture episodes and documented checks in every type. Seven hypotheses state what would count against the capture claim. Revelation 13 and 18 are read typologically, as images of economic participation conditioned on allegiance, and the reading is applied to a structure, never to a group or a state.

The ideology changes. The institutional functions remain.
