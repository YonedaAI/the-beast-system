1 Introduction

Every polity must settle who enforces its rules, and polities differ more in how they distribute that task than in whether they perform it. A Ju/’hoansi camp answers it with teasing, gossip and avoidance (Lee 2013; Wiessner 2005). A Habsburg monarchy of the late sixteenth century answered it with contracted soldiers, garrisons that mutinied when unpaid, and the lay familiars of the Inquisition (Parker 1972; Thompson 1976; Kamen 1997). The German Democratic Republic answered it with a security ministry that ran a dense network of unofficial informants (Gieseke 2014). Sweden answers it with a police force under parliamentary control and a Parliamentary Ombudsman whose office was established with the Instrument of Government of 1809 (Riksdagens ombudsmän 2026). These are different institutions. They discharge the same function.

This paper is Part V of a series that treats governance as six functions: allocation of resources (\(\mathsf{F}\)), rule-making (\(\mathsf{P}\)), interpretation (\(\mathsf{J}\)), legitimation (\(\mathsf{I}\)), coercion (\(\mathsf{C}\)) and production (\(\mathsf{W}\)). The series takes its vocabulary and its formal objects from the working paper by Largo (2026), which describes a “Hierarchy of the Beast System” and borrows its name from the typological imagery of Revelation 13 and 17 to 18. The series thesis has two parts that must be kept apart. Proposition A holds that the six functions and their dependencies are present in every functioning governance system; Part VII proves it in general from six axioms. Proposition B holds that the functions can become concentrated and integrated so that independent accountability fails. B is contingent and measurable, and A does not imply B. In this series “Beast System” names the universal six-function structure. It never labels a religion, a people, a profession, a regime or a person.

The coercive pillar is the place where the distinction between A and B is easiest to blur. Coercion looks like domination even when it is accountable, and the existence of police, soldiers and prisons is often treated as evidence of capture. The source paper warns against this inference and proposes that coercive power be measured by discretionary and asymmetric deployment rather than by spending (Largo 2026, Section 4). The present paper gives that warning a formal basis.

The argument has four steps. 3 derives the coercive function from the axiom of defection (Ax5). A deterrence theorem shows that rule compliance above the rate set by agents’ temptations requires a nonzero sanction regime, and a second result shows that, when sanctioning is costly, sanctioners themselves need provisioning, a norm of punishment, a higher-order sanction or a direct stake in deterrence. Both results are stated at the level of the role, not of any institution, and the ethnographic record of stateless societies shows the minimal forms they predict. 5 compares the distribution of the function across six regime types with equal depth. 6 introduces the distinction that carries the paper’s argument about capture: the conduct margin of an enforcement apparatus, which measures how much it responds to what people do, and its allegiance margin, which measures how much it responds to whose side they are on. The two margins are separable, so a strong coercive function can coexist with zero capture. When capacity is limited, allegiance targeting weakens deterrence for at least one group; when opponents are targeted regardless of conduct, it also raises the volume of coercion against them. 7 turns these results into indicators and falsifiable hypotheses, and 8 reads Revelation 13 against Romans 13 as two typological images of the sword: one conditioned on conduct, the other on worship.

The paper makes four contributions. It gives a necessity lemma for coercion that is explicit about the axiom it needs and about the counterexamples that would refute it. It derives the dependence of coercion on resources (\(\mathsf{F}\to \mathsf{C}\)) and on legitimation (\(\mathsf{I}\to \mathsf{C}\)) as a disjunction rather than an assertion, and states the condition on sanctioning technology under which the disjunction binds at scale. It defines the conduct and allegiance margins, proves their separability, which gives the pillar-level form of the claim that A does not imply B, and proves that they trade off under a capacity constraint. It supplies comparative static results for coup-proofing and for the political trajectory of dependent coercive corps, each with a stated falsifier.

2 The coercive pillar as a layer of the multiplex network

2.1 Shared objects

The series represents a polity at time \(t\) as a directed multiplex network \(G_t = (V_t, E_t)\) whose nodes are organizations or coherent actor groups and whose layers \(A^m_t\) are indexed by the pillar set \(\mathcal{M}= \{\mathsf{F}, \mathsf{P}, \mathsf{J}, \mathsf{I}, \mathsf{C}, \mathsf{W}\}\), \(M = |\mathcal{M}| = 6\) (Largo 2026). An edge weight \(a^m_{ij,t} \in [0,1]\) records the documented capacity of actor \(i\) to constrain a feasible action of actor \(j\) through mechanism \(m\). A many-to-many role map assigns each actor the pillars in which it acts. Each actor holds a normalized control share \(s^m_{i,t}\) in pillar \(m\), with \(\sum_i s^m_{i,t} = 1\), and the concentration of the pillar is the Herfindahl-Hirschman index \(H_{m,t} = \sum_i (s^m_{i,t})^2\). Contestability \(Q_{m,t} \in [0,1]\) summarizes transparency, appeal rights, institutional independence, competition and the credible replacement of decision makers. The source combines these into an illustrative index of unaccountable concentration, \[\begin{equation} U_t = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \omega_m H_{m,t}(1 - Q_{m,t}), \qquad \omega_m \ge 0, \quad \sum_m \omega_m = 1, \label{eq:U} \end{equation}\] whose weights must be preregistered. Cross-pillar integration \(C_{mn,t} \in [0,1]\) measures the extent to which one coalition commands appointments, budgets, sanctions or agenda in both \(m\) and \(n\), and the integration statistic is \(\mathcal{I}_t = \frac{2}{M(M-1)} \sum_{m<n} C_{mn,t}\), the mean over fifteen pairs. A capture link \((i \to j, m)\) requires privileged access or discretion, a resulting weakening of applicable accountability, and material evidence tying the asymmetry to an institutional output. A strong Beast System classification in the source’s sense requires both high \(U_t\) and high \(\mathcal{I}_t\). All of these objects belong to the source paper; this Part uses them and does not redefine them. Part VII formalizes them for the series.

2.2 Pillar-specific definitions

The coercive layer \(A^{\mathsf{C}}_t\) records capacities to impose costs on actors in response to their conduct or their status. The following definitions are owned by this Part.

Definition 1 (Coercive act, sanction and sanction function). A coercive act is a cost imposed on an agent by one or more other agents in response to the agent’s conduct or status. The cost may be material (fines, seizure, detention, injury, death), relational (withdrawal of cooperation, exclusion from exchange, exile) or reputational (ridicule, gossip, loss of standing). A sanction is a coercive act that responds to an observed or alleged violation of a rule. The sanction function \(\mathsf{C}\) of a collective is the set of practices by which violations are detected and sanctions are decided and delivered. It is implemented if, when violations occur and are detected, they are met with sanctions with non-trivial regularity, whether through designated offices, through coalitions formed for the occasion, or through dispersed individual responses.

The definition is broad on the form of the cost and narrow on what makes a cost a sanction. Costs that fall on an agent regardless of what the agent does or is (a drought, a tax schedule applied equally to all) are not coercive acts. Costs that respond to status rather than to a rule violation, such as surveillance of an opposition party whose members have broken no rule, are coercive acts but not sanctions. The coercive layer records both, because the distinction between them is what 5 measures.

Definition 2 (Coercive control share). The coercive control share \(s^{\mathsf{C}}_{i,t}\) of actor \(i\) is its share of decision-relevant control over the deployment of coercive acts: the authority to order detection, detention, force or exclusion. Control over a deployment decision is attributed along the chain of command as follows. If actor \(i\) can countermand a decision or remove the actor \(j\) who takes it, and \(j\) follows \(i\)’s directives in a share \(f_{ij} \in [0,1]\) of such decisions, then \(i\) receives the share \(f_{ij}\) of the control attached to the decision and \(j\) retains \(1 - f_{ij}\); the rule is applied recursively up the chain, so shares sum to one. Spending, headcount and equipment enter only insofar as they are subject to deployment decisions. We write \(H^{\mathsf{C}}_t := H_{\mathsf{C},t}\), and \(H^{\mathsf{C}}_{(1),t}\) for the concentration computed among the organizations immediately below the apex, each treated as a unit, before attribution to the apex.

Two consequences of this definition matter later. A national army under one effective chain of command has its control attributed largely to the top of that chain, so its contribution to \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) is large regardless of its size. A federation in which many local police forces answer to separately elected authorities has low \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) even when total coercive capacity is high. The two concentration measures can diverge: a ruler who divides coercion among several organizations that all answer to the ruler lowers \(H^{\mathsf{C}}_{(1)}\) while \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) stays high. Concentration in either sense is distinct from Weber’s monopoly of legitimate violence, which Part II (Section 2) uses to describe the relation between rule-making and enforcement. Weber’s monopoly concerns the claim that only authorized agents may use force. It is a statement about the dependency of coercion on legitimation, not about who commands. A polity can make that claim successfully while dispersing command across many agencies, and a polity can concentrate command while failing to make the claim credible.

Definition 3 (Coercive contestability). Coercive contestability is \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}_t := Q_{\mathsf{C},t} = \sum_{k=1}^{6} \omega^{\mathsf{C}}_k q_{k,t}\), with preregistered weights \(\omega^{\mathsf{C}}_k \ge 0\), \(\sum_k \omega^{\mathsf{C}}_k = 1\), and component scores \(q_{k,t} \in [0,1]\) for: (1) appointment and removal of coercive commanders by an accountable authority distinct from the force itself; (2) judicial authorization of intrusive measures such as searches, interception and detention; (3) independent complaint and inspection bodies with access to records; (4) individual legal liability of agents for unlawful force; (5) protected refusal of manifestly unlawful orders; and (6) disclosure of coercive budgets and intelligence activities to a legislature or equivalent body.

Component (1) is a contestability score, and it should not be confused with the integration coefficient \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{C}}\), although the two are related. Part II’s appointment channel (Definition 2.8) assigns each coalition \(S\) an appointment share \(\alpha_{S,\mathsf{C}}\), the weighted share of coercive offices whose holders \(S\) can unilaterally appoint or remove. Proposition 6.5 of Part II bounds the coalition-overlap measure of integration by this share. Let \(\bar f_S\) be the average share of decisions in which \(S\)’s appointees follow its directives. Then \(C^{\mathrm{ov}}_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{C}} \ge \min\{s^{\mathsf{P}}(S), \bar f_S\alpha_{S,\mathsf{C}}\}\) for every coalition. If command runs only through appointment, the overlap is at most \(\bar f \max_S \alpha_{S,\mathsf{C}}\) when loyalty is bounded by \(\bar f\), and it is zero when no coalition can unilaterally appoint or remove any coercive commander. The hypothesis that command runs only through appointment is restrictive for coercion. Budgets, business holdings and patronage give coalitions command over coercive organizations whose heads they do not formally appoint, as the Iranian case in 5.4 shows, so a low appointment share does not by itself imply low integration. A high score on component (1) lowers the appointment share of any single coalition, and Part II’s result then limits the integration that appointment alone can produce. Component (2) depends on the judicial contestability \(Q^{\mathsf{J}}\) whose components Part III defines (Definition 2.4).

Definition 4 (Sanction regime and deterrence margin). Consider agents \(i \in N = \{1,\dots,n\}\) who in each period \(t\) choose \(v_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}\), where \(v_{i,t} = 1\) means violating a given rule. Let \(\sigma\) be a strategy profile and \(h_t\) a history. For \(v \in \{0,1\}\) let \(W_i(h_t, v)\) be \(i\)’s expected discounted payoff from period \(t\) onward, excluding the current temptation term, when \(i\) chooses \(v\) at \((t,h_t)\), follows \(\sigma_i\) thereafter, and the other agents follow \(\sigma_{-i}\) throughout. The deterrence margin faced by \(i\) at \((t,h_t)\) is \[\begin{equation} d_{i,t} = W_i(h_t, 0) - W_i(h_t, 1), \label{eq:margin} \end{equation}\] with discount factor \(\delta \in [0,1)\). The sanction regime of \(\sigma\) is the map \((i, h_t) \mapsto d_{i,t}\). The regime is trivial if \(d_{i,t} \le 0\) at every reachable node.

The margin compares two unilateral continuations, so it is defined at every node, including nodes at which \(\sigma_i\) prescribes compliance and violation is off the path of play. It includes \(i\)’s own optimal future behavior, which may itself change after a violation; an agent excluded from cooperation, for example, faces different future margins from one in good standing. Under the payoff assumption of 3, a violation affects \(i\)’s future only through other agents’ responses, so the margin is zero whenever \(\sigma_{-i}\) does not condition on \(i\)’s violations. A positive margin therefore requires that others respond to \(i\)’s violation in a way that lowers \(i\)’s payoff. The response may be a beating, a lawsuit, a boycott or a refusal to share meat. The definition does not ask who responds or whether they hold an office.

Definition 5 (Conduct and allegiance margins). Fix a reference rule set \(R^0\): a preregistered set of rules in force whose content does not refer to political alignment, such as prohibitions of theft, assault and fraud. Let \(v \in \{0,1\}\) indicate whether an agent violated a rule in \(R^0\) in the period, and let each agent carry an alignment \(y \in \{\mathrm{al}, \mathrm{op}\}\), aligned with or opposed to the coalition that directs the coercive apparatus. Let \(\psi(v,y) \in [0,1]\) be the per-period probability that an agent with violation status \(v\) and alignment \(y\) is the target of a coercive act of magnitude \(\mu > 0\). The conduct margin for alignment \(y\) is \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y) = \psi(1,y) - \psi(0,y)\). The allegiance margin at violation status \(v\) is \(\Delta^{\mathrm{a}}(v) = \psi(v,\mathrm{op}) - \psi(v,\mathrm{al})\).

The conduct margin is the enforcement of rules. The allegiance margin is the enforcement of loyalty. Rule-of-law ideals ask for \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y) > 0\) and \(\Delta^{\mathrm{a}}(v) = 0\): violators are targeted more than compliers, and opponents are treated like supporters with the same conduct. The reference rule set is what keeps the two margins distinct. If loyalty were itself a rule in \(R^0\), violation status and alignment would coincide for that rule and the allegiance margin would collapse into the conduct margin. Rules whose content is allegiance, such as an obligation to worship an image or a prohibition of opposition, are therefore excluded from \(R^0\). Coercive acts that enforce them appear in this framework as an allegiance margin, and their enactment is rule-making capture, which Part II analyzes. A third pattern is distinct from both margins: arbitrariness, in which \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\) falls toward zero for every alignment, so that coercion responds neither to conduct nor to allegiance. 6 shows that the conduct and allegiance margins can be chosen independently and analyzes the conditions under which they trade off.

2.3 Dependencies and integration

Two different relations connect the coercive pillar to the other five, and they must be kept apart. A dependency \(m \to \mathsf{C}\) holds when the coercive function needs an output of pillar \(m\) in order to operate. The integration coefficient \(C_{m\mathsf{C}}\) of the source measures something else: the extent to which one coalition commands appointments, budgets, sanctions or agenda in both pillars. Dependencies are present in every polity, as Part VII argues for the whole dependency structure; high integration is contingent. Dependency is evidence of Proposition A, and integration is an ingredient of Proposition B.

The dependencies of coercion run as follows. Coercion must be provisioned (\(\mathsf{F}\to \mathsf{C}\)); Part I owns the financing side of the fiscal-military state and the pair \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{C}}\), including the fusion of financial and coercive control in one organization (Part I, Section 6), and this Part treats the coercive side. Commanders are appointed (\(\mathsf{P}\to \mathsf{C}\)), and Part II’s appointment channel measures how far appointment produces integration. Sanctions follow determinations of wrongdoing (\(\mathsf{J}\to \mathsf{C}\)); Part III treats that pair, and its Proposition 3.6 shows that an effective judicial determination requires either legitimacy or an expected sanction for defiance, a dependency in the opposite direction. Sanctions must be recognized as legitimate to be distinguished from predation (\(\mathsf{I}\to \mathsf{C}\)); Part IV defines the legitimation function (Definition 2.1) and treats the pair \(C_{\mathsf{I}\mathsf{C}}\). The pair of coercion and production is the primary responsibility of this Part. Conscription is a dependency \(\mathsf{W}\to \mathsf{C}\): it converts the labor time of the producing population into coercive capacity. Coerced labor is a dependency \(\mathsf{C}\to \mathsf{W}\): it converts coercive capacity into production. The integration coefficient \(C_{\mathsf{C}\mathsf{W}}\) is high only when one coalition commands both the coercive apparatus and the allocation of labor. 6.5 treats the pair.

3 Necessity of the coercive pillar

3.1 The axiom of defection

Part VII states six working axioms about collective life and derives one pillar from each. The axiom behind the coercive pillar is Ax5 (defection): some members sometimes break rules. Hobbes put the consequence in a sentence that has organized the subject since: “covenants, without the sword, are but words” (Hobbes 1651, ch. 17). Hobbes drew from it the conclusion that a sovereign must hold the sword. The argument below accepts the premise and rejects that conclusion. Defection makes sanctioning necessary; it does not make a sovereign necessary.

To give Ax5 content we state it as a claim about temptation rather than about observed violations. In each period agent \(i\) privately observes a net temptation \(\tau_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}\), the gain from violating the rule minus every cost of violation that does not pass through other agents’ responses. Internalized norms, guilt and shame lower \(\tau_{i,t}\); so do any direct material costs of violation to the violator. Temptations are drawn independently across agents and periods from a distribution with cumulative distribution function \(G\), and \(p := \operatorname{Pr}(\tau_{i,t} > 0) = 1 - G(0)\) is the probability that an agent would gain from violation if nobody responded.

A rule is useful to a collective only if violations stay below some rate. Call that rate the tolerance \(\theta \in [0,1)\): if the expected frequency of violations exceeds \(\theta\) in the long run, the rule no longer coordinates behavior and the collective either abandons it or fails to persist. Formally, the rule is in force under \(\sigma\) if \[\limsup_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{nT} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Pr}(v_{i,t} = 1) \le \theta .\]

Assumption 6 (Ax5, defection, formal version). For the rule under consideration, \(p > \theta\): the probability that an agent gains from violation in the absence of any response by others exceeds the violation rate the collective can tolerate.

The assumption is empirical, not logical, and it is stronger than the informal statement of Ax5. Observed violations are compatible with \(p \le \theta\), since a collective may tolerate occasional violations of a rule that few are tempted to break. A population of saints, or one so thoroughly socialized that internal costs exceed every temptation, would have \(p \le \theta\) for every rule. To connect the theorem below to Proposition A, which concerns every functioning governance system, we therefore need a bridge.

Assumption 7 (Bridge). Every functioning governance system has at least one rule in force for which 6 holds.

Proposition A for the coercive pillar asserts the implemented function of 1: violations, when they occur and are detected, are met with sanctions with non-trivial regularity. It follows from 9 under the bridge assumption together with two further conditions, that members best-respond to their temptations and that \(G\) has unbounded support, so that violations occur on the path of play and the threatened sanctions are actually delivered. The bridge carries the empirical content of the coercive pillar’s place in Proposition A. It is not true by definition: it would fail for a collective whose every rule is one that almost nobody is tempted to break, and 3.5 states how to test it with a measurement of \(p\) that is independent of sanction practices. We also assume that the agent’s payoff depends on its own violation only through \(\tau_{i,t}\) and through other agents’ responses. Any direct effect of \(i\)’s violation on \(i\)’s own future (for example, overgrazing that also harms the violator’s herd) is folded into \(\tau_{i,t}\).

3.2 The deterrence lemma

Lemma 8 (Deterrence). Let \(\sigma\) be a strategy profile in which every agent best-responds at every node. At \((t, h_t)\), agent \(i\) violates if \(\tau_{i,t} > d_{i,t}\) and complies if \(\tau_{i,t} < d_{i,t}\). The probability that \(i\) violates at that node is at least \(1 - G(d_{i,t})\), with equality when \(G\) has no atom at \(d_{i,t}\) or ties are resolved toward compliance.

Proof. By 4, \(i\)’s expected payoff from period \(t\) onward is \(\tau_{i,t} + W_i(h_t,1)\) if it violates and \(W_i(h_t,0)\) if it complies, where both continuations include \(i\)’s own optimal future play. The difference between violating and complying is \(\tau_{i,t} - d_{i,t}\). A best-responding agent violates when this difference is positive and complies when it is negative. Because \(\tau_{i,t}\) is drawn from \(G\) independently of \(h_t\), the probability of violation is at least \(\operatorname{Pr}(\tau_{i,t} > d_{i,t}) = 1 - G(d_{i,t})\); ties at \(\tau_{i,t} = d_{i,t}\) can only add violations. ◻

The lemma is a dynamic version of the deterrence condition in the economics of crime: a rational offender is deterred when the expected cost of punishment exceeds the gain (Becker 1968). In the simplest regimes \(d_{i,t} = \pi\mu\), the product of the detection probability and the sanction magnitude. The general form [eq:margin] also covers sanctions that are not punishments in the legal sense: the loss of future cooperation in a repeated game, a damaged reputation in a trading network, or exclusion from the next meat distribution.

Theorem 9 (Necessity of the sanction function). Suppose 6 holds for a rule that is in force under a best-response profile \(\sigma\). Let \(\bar{\mathbb{E}}_T[x] = \frac{1}{nT}\sum_{t\le T}\sum_{i} \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[x_{i,t}]\) denote the expected average over agents and the first \(T\) periods. Then \[\begin{equation} \liminf_{T\to\infty} \bar{\mathbb{E}}_T\bigl[G(d) - G(0)\bigr] \ge p - \theta > 0, \label{eq:necessity} \end{equation}\] and the long-run expected share of agent-periods with a strictly positive deterrence margin is at least \((p - \theta)/p\). In particular, the sanction regime of \(\sigma\) is non-trivial.

Proof. By 8 and the law of iterated expectations, \(\operatorname{Pr}(v_{i,t} = 1) \ge \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[1 - G(d_{i,t})]\), so the expected violation frequency up to \(T\) is at least \(\bar{\mathbb{E}}_T[1 - G(d)]\). Because the rule is in force, \(\limsup_T \bar{\mathbb{E}}_T[1 - G(d)] \le \theta\), which gives \(\liminf_T \bar{\mathbb{E}}_T[G(d)] \ge 1 - \theta\). Since \(G(0) = 1 - p\), subtracting gives [eq:necessity]. For the second claim, each term satisfies \(G(d_{i,t}) - G(0) \le p \cdot \mathbf{1}[d_{i,t} > 0]\), because the difference is zero when \(d_{i,t} \le 0\) and at most \(1 - G(0) = p\) otherwise. Hence \(\liminf_T \bar{\mathbb{E}}_T[\mathbf{1}[d > 0]] \ge (p - \theta)/p\). A trivial regime would make the left side of [eq:necessity] non-positive, contradicting \(p > \theta\). ◻

The theorem is stated in expectation. Realized frequencies converge to the same limits: the differences \(v_{i,t} - \operatorname{Pr}(v_{i,t} = 1 \mid h_t)\) are bounded martingale differences, so by the strong law of large numbers for such differences their average tends to zero almost surely. The theorem is a necessity statement about a role, in the sense required by Part VII: some agents must respond to violations in a way that costs the violator. It is silent on who those agents are, on whether they specialize, and on whether the cost is physical. It also gives two quantitative floors. The sanction regime must shift at least \(p - \theta\) of probability mass from violation to compliance, and at least a fraction \((p-\theta)/p\) of agent-periods must face a positive margin. Sanctions that are too weak or too rarely threatened fail these bounds even when they exist on paper.

A positive margin is a threat. 1 counts the function as implemented only if violations, when they occur, are actually met with sanctions. The two notions coincide whenever violations occur on the path of play. If \(G\) has unbounded support, so that \(\operatorname{Pr}(\tau > x) > 0\) for every \(x\), then by 8 violations occur with positive probability at every node, and a positive margin can be sustained only if they are in fact sanctioned with positive probability. A collective in which temptations are bounded and every agent is perfectly deterred would have the threat without observed sanctions; we know of no such collective, and 1 records frequent sanctions in the societies closest to that ideal.

Corollary 10 (Magnitude). If every agent faces the same margin \(d = \pi\mu\), compliance at rate at least \(1 - \theta\) requires \(\pi\mu \ge G^{-1}(1 - \theta)\), where \(G^{-1}(u) := \inf\{x : G(x) \ge u\}\) is the generalized inverse. A rise in temptation (a rightward shift of \(G\)) or a fall in detection \(\pi\) must be offset by a larger sanction magnitude \(\mu\) or a larger detection probability \(\pi\); otherwise the collective must accept a higher tolerance \(\theta\).

Proof. With a common margin the violation rate is \(1 - G(\pi\mu)\) when ties are resolved toward compliance, and \(1 - G(\pi\mu) \le \theta\) holds if and only if \(G(\pi\mu) \ge 1 - \theta\), which is \(\pi\mu \ge G^{-1}(1-\theta)\) for the generalized inverse. ◻

3.3 The second-order problem

A sanction is an act, and acts have costs. A Ju/’hoansi who criticizes a boastful hunter risks a quarrel; a Nuer kinsman who joins a feud risks his life; a police officer who arrests a powerful man risks a career. If nobody is willing to bear these costs, the deterrence margin collapses to zero and 9 fails. The following result states what must be true of sanctioners when sanctioning is costly.

Proposition 11 (Second-order enforcement). Suppose that delivering a sanction against \(i\) requires an act by some agent \(j \neq i\) at private cost \(c_j > 0\), and that \(d_{i,t} > 0\) in a best-response profile. Decompose the private return to \(j\) of sanctioning into four terms: (a) a material transfer to \(j\) conditional on sanctioning, such as pay, provisioning or a share of seized goods; (b) an intrinsic or social reward, such as esteem, a sense of duty or satisfaction from punishing; (c) avoidance of a sanction that \(j\) would itself suffer for failing to sanction; and (d) \(j\)’s own share of the benefit of deterring \(i\) and, through the example, third parties, weighted by the probability that \(j\)’s act is decisive. Then for some such \(j\) the sum of (a) to (d) is at least \(c_j\).

Proof. The four terms are a decomposition of \(j\)’s payoff from its own sanctioning act: transfers conditioned on the act, \(j\)’s preferences over the act itself, others’ responses to the act or its omission, and the act’s effect on the behavior of \(i\) and of others. We take this decomposition as the definition of the private return. If, for every \(j\) whose act is required, the sum were below \(c_j\), then not sanctioning would be a strict best response for each such \(j\). No sanction would be delivered against \(i\) for a violation at \((t,h_t)\), so \(\sigma_{-i}\) would not respond to \(v_{i,t}\) and \(d_{i,t} = 0\), a contradiction. ◻

The proposition is a disjunction, and its interpretation needs care. Term (a) is the dependency \(\mathsf{F}\to \mathsf{C}\): paid soldiers and police are provisioned out of resources that someone allocates. Term (b) is the dependency \(\mathsf{I}\to \mathsf{C}\): a norm that makes punishing violators honorable, or a belief that the sanctioning authority is legitimate, supplies the reward. Term (c) is coercion applied to the coercers. In repeated games it can close the regress in equilibrium without transfers or intrinsic rewards: community-enforcement equilibria, in which those who fail to punish are themselves punished, need only that agents coordinate on the same punishment norm. Whether that coordination counts as legitimation is a question for Part IV, and this paper does not settle it by definition. Term (d) does not route the dependency through \(\mathsf{F}\) or \(\mathsf{I}\). The structure parallels Proposition 3.6 of Part III, where an effective judicial determination needs legitimacy or sanction: in both cases the incoming dependency is a disjunction, which Part VII must treat as a hyperedge of the dependency digraph rather than as separate arcs.

The scale argument is conditional on the technology of sanctioning.

Corollary 12 (Costly sanctions at scale). Suppose sanctions are costly to the sanctioner (\(c_j \ge \underline{c} > 0\)), the benefit of deterrence is shared across the collective so that the probability that any one sanctioner’s act is decisive tends to zero as \(n\) grows, and higher-order sanctions (term (c)) are absent. Then for \(n\) large enough, a non-trivial sanction regime requires that term (a) or term (b) be positive for some sanctioner.

Proof. Term (d) is bounded by the benefit of deterrence to \(j\) times the probability that \(j\)’s act is decisive, and the second factor tends to zero, so for \(n\) large enough term (d) is below \(\underline{c}\). Term (c) is zero by hypothesis. 11 then requires (a) or (b) to make up the difference. ◻

The hypotheses matter. When sanctions take the form of bilateral exclusion, as in the trading coalition discussed in 3.5, each excluder’s act is decisive for its own relationship whatever the size of the collective, and the cost may be zero or negative: refusing to rehire a cheat can save money. Then the premise \(c_j > 0\) fails, and coercion of this kind does not need the \(\mathsf{F}\to \mathsf{C}\) or \(\mathsf{I}\to \mathsf{C}\) arc of 12; it still depends on the other pillars through the arcs of 2.3, for example \(\mathsf{W}\to \mathsf{C}\). The corollary applies to sanctions that are costly public goods, such as detaining, fighting or punishing strangers, which is the form that coercion against serious violations takes in large polities. Under that condition the sanction function at scale must be provisioned or legitimated. This is a micro-foundation for Tilly’s coupling of war-making to extraction (Tilly 1990) and for the military-fiscal bargains studied in 5.5. Wherever term (a) dominates, the sanctioner’s loyalty follows the paymaster, which is the root of the guardian problem treated in 6. Experimental evidence supports term (b): subjects in public-goods experiments pay to punish free riders even when they cannot expect future gains from doing so, and cooperation is sustained when punishment is available (Fehr and Gächter 2002). In a band of thirty, by contrast, a person harmed by a thief has a direct interest in stopping the theft and a real chance that the intervention matters, so term (d) can carry the load. This is the collective-action logic of Olson (1965) applied to enforcement.

3.4 Minimal forms in stateless and consensus societies

9 predicts that every persistent collective satisfying Ax5 has some sanction practice. It does not predict police. The next result shows that the deterrence margins the theorem requires can be produced with no specialized role at all. Whether the sanctioners are willing to produce them is the separate second-order question.

Proposition 13 (Minimal form). Consider \(n\) agents who play the rule-compliance game in every period and also cooperate in a joint activity that yields each participant a per-period surplus \(b > 0\). Let the profile be: every agent cooperates with every other agent who is not excluded; an agent who violates is excluded from cooperation for the next \(L\) periods, and a violation by an excluded agent restarts the exclusion. Detection is perfect within the group, each agent best-responds in its compliance decision (as in 8), and \(\operatorname{Pr}(\tau < 0) > 0\). Then an agent with \(r \in \{0, \dots, L-1\}\) future periods of exclusion remaining, which are the reachable states, faces a deterrence margin \(d(r)\) that satisfies \[b\,\delta^{r+1}\operatorname{Pr}(\tau < 0)^{r} \;\le\; d(r) \;\le\; b \sum_{k=r+1}^{L} \delta^k ,\] so \(d(r) > 0\) in every reachable state. The upper bound holds with equality when the agent complies at every later node of both continuations; for an agent in good standing (\(r = 0\)) it equals \(b\,\delta(1-\delta^L)/(1-\delta)\). No agent holds a specialized sanctioning role, and with equal participation each agent’s coercive control share is \(1/n\), so \(H^{\mathsf{C}} = 1/n\).

Proof. Compare the two continuations of 4 by coupling them on the same future temptations. After complying at \(t\) the agent is excluded in periods \(t+1, \dots, t+r\); after violating it is excluded in periods \(t+1, \dots, t+L\). If the agent uses the same future actions in both continuations, the paths coincide from the first later violation at some date \(s\) onward, since each violation restarts the same exclusion. Before that, they differ only in periods \(t+r+1, \dots, \min(t+L, s)\), in which the complier receives \(b\) and the violator does not.

For the upper bound, give the violator the complier’s optimal future actions. This choice yields at most \(W_i(h_t,1)\), so \(d(r) \le \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[b\sum_{k=r+1}^{\min(L, s-t)}\delta^k\bigr] \le b\sum_{k=r+1}^{L}\delta^k\). If the agent complies at every later node of both continuations, both are deterministic exclusion paths and the bound is attained. For the lower bound, give the complier the violator’s optimal future actions. This yields at most \(W_i(h_t,0)\), so \(d(r) \ge \operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[b\sum_{k=r+1}^{\min(L, s-t)}\delta^k\bigr] \ge 0\), where \(s\) is now the first later violation under the violator’s optimal play. The same argument at every later node shows that all margins are non-negative, so an agent with \(\tau < 0\) strictly prefers to comply. With probability at least \(\operatorname{Pr}(\tau<0)^{r}\) the agent draws negative temptations in periods \(t+1, \dots, t+r\) and does not violate in them, so \(s \ge t+r+1\) and the sum contains at least the term \(b\delta^{r+1}\), since an agent who is not excluded in period \(t+r+1\) receives \(b\) in that period even if it violates then; this gives the lower bound, which is positive because \(r + 1 \le L\). For an agent in good standing it gives \(d(0) \ge b\delta\). Exclusion is delivered by each agent withholding its own cooperation, so no agent holds authority over another’s response, and under equal participation each holds an equal share \(1/n\) of the decision-relevant control over coercive acts. Then \(H^{\mathsf{C}} = n \cdot (1/n)^2 = 1/n\). ◻

Both bounds fall as \(r\) rises: an agent at the start of a long exclusion has less to lose from a further violation, which is why such profiles are usually combined with graduated sanctions or with longer exclusion for repeat violators. The proposition computes margins; it does not show that the profile is an equilibrium for the excluders. Each excluder forgoes its own surplus from cooperating with the violator, which is the second-order cost of 11. Extending the profile so that anyone who cooperates with an excluded agent is also excluded supplies term (c). Whether the extended profile is an equilibrium depends on whether the surplus an agent would lose by being excluded from the whole group exceeds the surplus it forgoes by withholding cooperation from one violator, and we do not claim that it is an equilibrium in general. The ethnographic record suggests that terms (b) and (d) also operate: people are angered by violations, and in small groups each person’s response matters.

The ethnographic record of societies described as stateless or radically egalitarian supplies sanction practices of exactly this distributed kind. 1 summarizes them.

Three features of the table deserve emphasis. The sanctions are distributed, so \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) is low. They are rarely specialized: no person makes a living from them. And they are not mild. Boehm’s reverse dominance hierarchy includes execution of persistent would-be dominants (Boehm 1999), and Lee recorded homicides among the Ju/’hoansi (Lee 1979). The egalitarian character of these societies does not come from the absence of coercion. It comes from the fact that coercion is held by the collective and directed against attempts to concentrate it. In the vocabulary of this series, the societies of 1 have the coercive function (Proposition A) and low concentration with high contestability (the negation of Proposition B). Woodburn’s phrase for the mechanism, institutions of leveling, describes a coercive pillar whose output is the prevention of capture (Woodburn 1982).

The ethnographic evidence has known limits. Most of it was gathered after contact with states and markets, from groups living in marginal environments, and foraging societies vary more than the classic accounts suggest (Kelly 1995). Graeber and Wengrow (2021) argue that some societies moved seasonally between egalitarian and hierarchical arrangements, including temporary policing authority during collective hunts. If correct, this strengthens the present argument: the sanction function persists while its holders change with the season.

3.5 Counterexample attempts

The strongest attempt to refute the necessity of coercion appeals to self-enforcing cooperation. Axelrod showed that reciprocal strategies can sustain cooperation without a central authority (Axelrod 1984). Greif showed that the eleventh-century Maghribi traders enforced agency contracts through a coalition in which cheated merchants were refused future employment by all members (Greif 1993). Edwards and Ogilvie dispute this reading and argue that the traders also relied on formal legal institutions (Edwards and Ogilvie 2012); on either reading the case contains sanctions. Ellickson found that cattle ranchers in Shasta County settled disputes by neighborly norms rather than by law (Ellickson 1991). None of these is a counterexample. In each case an agent’s continuation payoff depends on the agent’s own past conduct: the reciprocator retaliates, the coalition refuses to deal, and the neighbor gossips or engages in self-help. Each mechanism is a sanction regime in the sense of 4. What these cases refute is the Hobbesian inference from the need for sanctions to the need for a sovereign.

A second attempt appeals to internalization. If socialization makes norm violation intrinsically costly, temptations may never be positive, and sanctions are unnecessary. The theorem accepts this as a logical possibility: internalization lowers \(p\), and if \(p \le \theta\) Ax5 fails and the coercive pillar is not derived for that rule. The bridge assumption claims that no functioning governance system achieves \(p \le \theta\) for every rule it keeps in force. The societies that come closest, those of 1, are also the ones in which ethnographers record continual sanctioning talk (Wiessner 2005). Internalization and sanction are complements, not substitutes: shared norms reduce temptation and also supply the motive for punishing those who remain tempted (term (b) of 11).

A third attempt appeals to exit. Where dissatisfied members can simply leave, perhaps the collective needs no sanctions. Exit is a sanction when those who leave take with them something the violator valued. When exit costs the violator nothing, 8 predicts violations at the unsanctioned rate \(p\). A collective then persists only by relying on fewer rules that matter, which is what immediate-return foragers appear to do: with little storage there is less to steal and less to hoard (Woodburn 1982). Exit narrows the set of rules that require enforcement; it does not remove the function for the rules that remain.

These replies define the falsifier, and the falsifier must not be immunized. The replies to the second and third attempts could be used to reclassify any compliant, unsanctioned collective as one with \(p \le \theta\), which would make the necessity claim true by definition. Two safeguards prevent this. The first is that the temptation share \(p\) must be measured independently of the sanction practices it is used to explain, and before they are observed. Designs that offer members an unobserved opportunity to break a rule, so that no response by others is possible, measure the share who would violate in the absence of sanctions. The second is that the necessity falsifier does not use the tolerance \(\theta\) at all. By 8, a zero margin implies a violation rate of at least \(p\). Proposition A for the coercive pillar is therefore refuted by a persistent collective in which the measured \(p\) for a rule exceeds the observed violation frequency by more than sampling error, while violators’ subsequent treatment (cooperation, exchange, standing and material loss) is no worse than that of matched compliers. The bridge assumption has its own falsifier, which does need \(\theta\): the tolerance must then be preregistered for each rule, for example as the violation rate at which comparable collectives have abandoned the rule, and the bridge is refuted by a collective in which the measured \(p\) is below the preregistered \(\theta\) for every rule in force. We found no documented case of either kind in the sources reviewed, and we know of no study that has carried out these measurements in a stateless society.

3.6 Functional necessity without functionalist explanation

The theorem claims the necessity of a function, not of an institution. That distinction answers the classic objection of Hempel (1959): from the premise that a system needs some sanction practice, it does not follow that it needs a police force, because many functional equivalents can meet the need. 1 lists several. The theorem also makes no causal claim about why any particular polity has the coercive institutions it has. Elster (1979), Elster (1983) argued that explaining an institution by its beneficial effects requires a feedback mechanism from effects to institution. 9 is a condition of persistence, not a functional explanation: collectives without a sanction regime do not keep rules in force, but the theorem says nothing about which sanction regime a collective adopts or why. That question belongs to the comparative analysis of 5, where design varies with threats, finances and legitimating beliefs.

4 Literature on coercive power

4.1 Coercion and the making of states

Modern discussion of coercion begins with Weber’s definition of the state as the human community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory (Weber 1946; Weber 1978). Part II uses it where it treats the coupling of rule-making to enforcement. For the coercive pillar, the important word is “claims”. Weber’s definition concerns legitimacy, and 2 has already separated it from command concentration.

Tilly made coercion the engine of European state formation. Rulers who made war had to extract resources to pay for it, and the bargains they struck with those who held capital produced different state forms: coercion-intensive paths where rulers squeezed agrarian populations, capital-intensive paths where they borrowed from merchant cities, and capitalized-coercion paths in between (Tilly 1990). In an earlier essay Tilly compared war making and state making to organized crime, since the state sells protection against threats that it partly produces itself (Tilly 1985). The analogy is a heuristic, not an equivalence, and this paper treats it as a hypothesis about when coercion becomes capture. Karaman and Pamuk extended the fiscal side of the argument to show that the effect of warfare on state capacity depended on economic structure and regime type across Europe and the Ottoman Empire (Karaman and Pamuk 2013). The financing side of this literature belongs to Part I; here the question is how the coercive apparatus that resulted was controlled.

Mann distinguished despotic power, the range of actions rulers can take without negotiation with civil society, from infrastructural power, the capacity to penetrate society and implement decisions (Mann 1984). The distinction maps onto the two margins of 5. A state with high infrastructural power can enforce rules widely (a large conduct margin is feasible); a state with high despotic power can direct enforcement as it pleases (a large allegiance margin is feasible). North, Wallis and Weingast described most of recorded history as limited-access orders in which specialists in violence agree not to fight because they share rents (North et al. 2009). In the vocabulary of this series, such orders have high \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{C}}\) by construction.

4.2 The guardian problem

If coercion is provisioned (term (a) of 11), the agents who hold it can turn it against those who provision them. Civil-military relations theory is the study of this problem. Huntington proposed objective civilian control: a professional officer corps, autonomous in military matters and politically neutral (Huntington 1957). Janowitz replied that professionalism alone does not secure neutrality and that the military should be integrated into civilian society as a constabulary force (Janowitz 1960). Finer catalogued the forms of military intervention in politics (Finer 1962), and Huntington and Perlmutter described praetorian societies in which armed forces become ordinary political contestants (Huntington 1968; Perlmutter 1969). Feaver recast the relationship as a principal-agent problem in which civilians choose monitoring mechanisms and soldiers choose whether to work or shirk (Feaver 1999; Feaver 2003). Kohn emphasized that civilian control is a practice that must be exercised, not merely a constitutional provision (Kohn 1997).

Authoritarian rulers face the guardian problem in a sharper form. Svolik showed that dictators face two separate problems: sharing power with the allied elites who help them rule, and controlling the masses they rule (Svolik 2012). Coercive agents are useful against the second problem and dangerous in the first. Quinlivan described the coup-proofing practices of Middle Eastern regimes: reliance on family, ethnic and religious loyalties, the creation of parallel military forces, and multiple overlapping internal security agencies (Quinlivan 1999). Pilster and Böhmelt found that coup-proofing reduced battlefield effectiveness in interstate wars between 1967 and 1999 (Pilster and Böhmelt 2011). Greitens argued that the design of coercive institutions depends on whether rulers perceive their main threat as coming from elites or from the masses: elite threats favor fragmented and socially exclusive organizations, mass threats favor unified and inclusive ones, and these designs in turn shape patterns of state violence (Greitens 2016). Slater showed that elites who fear contentious mass politics may accept stronger coercive and extractive institutions as protection pacts (Slater 2010).

4.3 Repression and its limits

Repression is the use of coercion against political opponents, and it is the most direct observable form of the allegiance margin. Davenport’s review defines repression broadly, from surveillance and bans to arrests, torture and mass killing, and organizes the field around the problem of how authorities use coercion against domestic challengers (Davenport 2007). Poe and Tate found in a 153-country analysis of the 1980s that democracy and participation in civil or international war had substantively important effects on repression of personal integrity rights (Poe and Tate 1994). Hill and Jones, using cross-validation rather than significance tests, found that few proposed explanations improve out-of-sample prediction much; democratic institutions predict some kinds of repression far better than others, and features of domestic legal systems perform well (Hill and Jones 2014). Wintrobe modeled dictators as trading off repression and the purchase of loyalty (Wintrobe 1998). Rejali documented a boundary case: democracies under public monitoring developed and exported torture techniques that leave few marks (Rejali 2007). Democracy constrains what coercers can be seen to do; it does not by itself eliminate the allegiance margin.

Whether coercive agents carry out repression is not guaranteed. Bellin attributed the durability of Middle Eastern authoritarianism to the will and capacity of coercive apparatuses to repress, and after the Arab uprisings of 2011 she examined why some militaries defected and others did not (Bellin 2004; Bellin 2012). Albrecht and Ohl applied Hirschman’s categories of exit, voice and loyalty to military behavior during unrest (Albrecht and Ohl 2016), and Chenoweth and Stephan argued that nonviolent campaigns succeed in part by inducing defections among security forces (Chenoweth and Stephan 2011). Gerschewski placed repression beside legitimation and co-optation as the three pillars of autocratic stability (Gerschewski 2013).

4.4 Policing, punishment and legitimacy

Within consolidated states, the main question about ordinary enforcement is why people comply. Tyler found that people obey the law more because they judge the authorities legitimate and the procedures fair than because they fear sanctions (Tyler 1990), and Jackson and colleagues found the same pattern in survey data on policing (Jackson et al. 2012). These findings do not contradict 9. They show that legitimation lowers the temptation distribution and supplies the motive for cooperation with enforcement, which in the present model shifts \(G\) and strengthens term (b). Foucault traced the shift from spectacular punishment to the disciplinary prison and the surveillance it made possible (Foucault 1977). Pratt documented the comparatively low imprisonment rates and humane prison conditions of the Nordic countries and traced them to egalitarian welfare states, while warning that populist pressures could erode them (Pratt 2008a; Pratt 2008b).

4.5 Contribution

The literatures above mostly study either the necessity of order (from Hobbes to the economics of deterrence) or the dangers of coercive power (from civil-military relations to repression), and they rarely connect the two formally. The deterrence literature from Becker (1968) onward models the conduct margin and assumes enforcement is impartial. The repression literature studies the allegiance margin and largely takes the conduct margin for granted. This Part treats both as properties of a single sanction-probability function, which shows that the necessity of coercion and its capture are logically separate and empirically distinguishable. It also places the evidence of six regime types on a common scale, so that the comparison asks how each type distributes a function that all of them share.

5 Comparative analysis across six regime types

Each regime type below is asked the same five questions: which institutions hold the coercive function, how concentrated and contestable it is, how it depends on and is integrated with the other five pillars, what preserves accountability, and which capture episodes and checks are documented. Every claimed capture episode is held to the same standard, the three-part capture link of the source paper as applied to coercion in 6, and each subsection reports at least one documented episode or standing risk and one documented check. The types are ideal types rather than mutually exclusive classes. The United States, for instance, is both a market-capitalist society and a secular democracy; each illustration is assigned to the type whose defining feature it shows. The coding is qualitative; a preregistered scoring scheme converts it into numerical values of \(U_t\) and \(\mathcal{I}_t\). Statements about concentration and integration are qualitative orderings that a coding exercise could confirm or overturn.

5.1 Market-capitalist societies

In market-capitalist societies the coercive function is held by public police forces, militaries and intelligence agencies under elected civilian authority, together with a private sector that manufactures weapons, supplies services and provides security for property. The defining feature for this pillar is the participation of private capital in producing coercive capacity and its interest in how that capacity is used.

The link between private enterprise and coercion is older than industrial capitalism. Parrott showed that the armies of early modern Europe were raised and supplied to a large extent by military entrepreneurs who treated war as a business, and that this system was more effective than older accounts of state-led military modernization allowed (Parrott 2012). The modern form is the defense industry. In his farewell address of January 1961, President Eisenhower warned the American public against the acquisition of unwarranted influence by what he called the military-industrial complex (Eisenhower 1961). In the vocabulary of this series, the warning describes a possible capture link in which producers of coercive capacity gain privileged access to the decisions that set budgets, procurement and deployment, raising \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{C}}\).

Concentration differs sharply between the military and the police. Command of the armed forces is concentrated in a national executive, so the military contributes a large share to \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\), while legislative control of budgets divides authority over provisioning. Policing in the United States is dispersed across federal, state and local agencies, each answering to a different elected authority, which lowers \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) for ordinary enforcement. The theory of civilian control was developed largely on the American case: Huntington’s objective control, Janowitz’s constabulary force and Feaver’s agency model all address how elected civilians can command a professional military that they cannot fully monitor (Huntington 1957; Janowitz 1960; Feaver 2003). Accountability rests on component (1) of \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\), appointment and removal by elected civilians; on components (2) and (4), judicial authorization of searches and the civil and criminal liability of agents; and on component (6), legislative scrutiny of budgets. Kohn argued that these provisions secure control only when civilians actually exercise them (Kohn 1997).

The capture hypothesis specific to this regime type is that concentrated private interests shape the use of coercion. It meets the source paper’s standard for a capture link only if three things are shown: privileged access by a private party, a weakening of the accountability that would otherwise apply, and material evidence that the access changed an enforcement outcome (Largo 2026). Eisenhower’s warning is evidence that a senior insider regarded the risk as serious; it is not evidence that capture occurred in any particular decision. A better documented episode comes from industrial conflict. The federal Commission on Industrial Relations reported in 1915 that “practically every industrial State has at some relatively recent time permitted its institutions to be used by one party or the other to an industrial dispute (almost without exception the employers)”. It found that sheriffs in many counties deputized guards “in the employment and pay of corporations” and that militia were at times recruited from corporate guards and employees (Commission on Industrial Relations 1915, pp. 147 to 148). Each element of the capture link is present in that finding: privileged access through deputization, the displacement of publicly accountable police by employer-paid agents, and enforcement outcomes against strikers. In the notation of 5, the allegiance margin was indexed by the side a person took in an industrial dispute. The report was itself the check. A public commission with investigative powers documented the practice and recommended rules for the conduct of the militia (Commission on Industrial Relations 1915).

For the present, following the source’s proposal for narrow projects, the test of the military-industrial hypothesis is a preregistered sample of procurement decisions, not a selection of scandals. The rival explanation is that defense spending responds to external threats and that contractors compete for contracts under rules they do not control. Tilly’s protection-racket analogy suggests a related hypothesis about ordinary policing: if public coercion protects some forms of property more than others, it transfers value to their owners (Tilly 1985). This predicts a positive allegiance margin indexed by economic position, which is an empirical question about arrest and prosecution rates conditional on conduct.

5.2 Communist party-states

In communist party-states the ruling party holds the coercive function through security organs that answer to its leadership, through political officers embedded in the armed forces, and through the police and militia. In the Soviet case the line of security organs ran from the Cheka, founded as Lenin’s political police (Leggett 1981), through its successors to the KGB, whose own successors after 1991 Knight traced (Knight 1996). In the German Democratic Republic the Ministry for State Security built an apparatus of full-time officers and unofficial informants that reached into local society (Gieseke 2014; Bruce 2010; Koehler 1999). In the People’s Republic of China the party maintains command of the People’s Liberation Army (Shambaugh 2002), and the police carry heavy burdens of stability maintenance that Scoggins and O’Brien describe from the officers’ own perspective (Scoggins and O’Brien 2016).

Concentration is high at the apex: the party leadership appoints and removes the heads of every coercive organization through the appointment channel analyzed in Part II, so \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) is high and the integration \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{C}}\) is close to its maximum. Below the apex, however, the apparatus is divided, so \(H^{\mathsf{C}}_{(1)}\) is lower. Army, security service and police are separate organizations with separate chains of command, and party organs inside the army provide a further check. Colton’s study of Soviet military politics found that the relationship between commissars and commanders was less a struggle between party and army than a structured form of military participation in politics, with the military’s role largely confined to its own institutional concerns (Colton 1979). The division of the coercive apparatus is a form of counterbalancing, and the comparative static of 17 applies to it.

Integration with the ideological pillar is direct, since the same party commands both: political officers and party organizations inside the coercive organs make doctrinal loyalty part of the job, so \(C_{\mathsf{I}\mathsf{C}}\) is high. Integration with production is also high. The Soviet camp system used forced labor on a large scale under the direction of the same party-state that set production plans (Applebaum 2003; Gregory and Lazarev 2003), so the dependency \(\mathsf{C}\to \mathsf{W}\) and the integration \(C_{\mathsf{C}\mathsf{W}}\) were both at the center of the Soviet economy for several decades. 6.5 returns to it. Contestability from outside the party is low: there is no independent judiciary able to review security operations, no competitive legislature able to audit them, and no free press able to expose them. The internal checks are party discipline and periodic purges. Purges can be used against security chiefs, but they are themselves exercises of the coercive function, so they check particular holders of power without raising \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\).

The terror of the late 1930s is the episode with the largest consequences, and it shows why arbitrariness and allegiance must be distinguished. The security organs were turned against party members, officers and ordinary citizens (Conquest 1990). Coercion fell on people who had broken no rule in any allegiance-neutral reference set, so the conduct margin collapsed across alignments, which is the arbitrariness of 15. Whether the episode was also capture in the source’s sense turns on the material-evidence step: how far the targeting was directed from the center against categories defined by political suspicion. That is exactly the question on which Conquest and revisionist historians disagree. Getty argued that the purges grew in part out of conflicts and confusion within the party apparatus rather than from a single central plan (Getty 1985). On Conquest’s reading the episode meets all three conditions of a capture link; on Getty’s it is closer to arbitrariness produced by an apparatus that the center did not fully control. Either way it is a failure of the coercive pillar, and the numbers on either side should be cited with that debate in view. The documented checks are of two kinds. Party supremacy over the military meant that the Soviet army did not seize power (Colton 1979). And the deterrent power of the apparatus rested on beliefs: when those beliefs shifted in 1989, regimes that had seemed secure fell with little warning (Kuran 1991).

Reform socialism, which introduced markets inside party-states, did not change who held coercion. Kornai’s account of the socialist system treats the reforms as changes in coordination mechanisms under continued party rule (Kornai 1992). For this reason the coercive pillar of market socialism as practiced is analyzed here rather than in the next subsection.

5.3 Social democracy and democratic socialism

In the social democracies of Northern Europe the coercive function is held by national police and armed forces under parliamentary control, with independent oversight bodies and a long tradition of citizen armies. The distinctive feature of this regime type for the coercive pillar is the combination of a centralized apparatus with high contestability and low reliance on punishment.

Sweden illustrates the oversight structure. Its Parliamentary Ombudsman was established with the Instrument of Government of 1809 to supervise how judges and civil servants apply the law, and its supervision was later extended to local government (Riksdagens ombudsmän 2026). Ombudsman supervision of public authorities contributes to components (3) and (4) of \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\): complaints can reach an independent office with access to records, and officials can be held to account for unlawful conduct. Civilian control of the armed forces has varied with the perceived external threat. Hedlund’s study of Swedish civil-military relations from 1984 to 2011 found that civilian control over the officer rank system and over professional officer education changed as perceptions of threat changed (Hedlund 2011).

The Nordic countries are best known in this domain for penal moderation. Pratt described comparatively low imprisonment rates and humane prison conditions, rooted in egalitarian welfare states with high social trust, and warned that the pattern was under pressure from more punitive politics (Pratt 2008a; Pratt 2008b). Penal moderation is not the absence of the coercive function. 9 requires a positive deterrence margin, not a large sanction magnitude, and 10 shows that a high detection probability or a temptation distribution shifted toward compliance permits smaller sanctions. Legitimacy research suggests the second mechanism. Where people regard the police and courts as fair, they comply for reasons other than fear of punishment (Tyler 1990; Jackson et al. 2012), which lowers the temptation distribution and strengthens the motive to cooperate with enforcement.

The Nordic security services also supply documented episodes of the allegiance margin, and documented checks on it. In Norway the parliament appointed a commission to examine allegations of illegal surveillance. Its report of 1996, known by the name of its chair, found that the police security service had carried out widespread illegal political surveillance, particularly in the 1960s and 1970s, of individuals and organizations on the political left (EOS Committee 2026). A parallel response to the revelations that led to the inquiry was a parliamentary oversight committee for all the secret services, established under a separate law of 1995 and elected for the first time in 1996 (EOS Committee 2026). In Sweden the government appointed a commission in 1999 to examine the security services’ surveillance of domestic political groups since 1945. Its directive records that the security police were barred in 1969 from registering a person solely on account of political views, that a general constitutional ban on such registration followed in 1977, and that military intelligence had registered communists from 1955 (Swedish Government 1999). The commission reported in 2002 (Säkerhetstjänstkommissionen 2002). These episodes meet the capture-link test in the form the source requires: discretion over surveillance, use of it against political opponents who had broken no rule, and documentary evidence assembled by official inquiries. They also show the check working, though decades late: legislatures with investigative powers exposed the practices and created standing oversight.

Dependencies and integration take an unusual form in this regime type. Conscript armies draw on the labor time of the whole population, so the dependency \(\mathsf{W}\to \mathsf{C}\) runs through a universal obligation rather than through a professional caste, and it involves no integration of command over coercion and production. Appointment runs through parliament rather than through a party, and provisioning runs through the general budget of a high-tax state. Concentration \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) is moderate to high because national police and military command are centralized, but contestability \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\) is high on most components, so the contribution of the coercive pillar to \(U_t\) in [eq:U] is small. This is the case that the source paper’s Proposition 4 anticipates: concentration combined with high-quality review can coexist with competent services and low arbitrary sanctioning (Largo 2026).

Democratic socialism as a program, as distinct from the social-democratic welfare states that partly realized it, has no separate record of coercive institutions in the sources reviewed. Its claims about coercion are claims about how the institutions of the previous paragraphs should be governed, and they are evaluated by the same measures.

5.4 Islamic states

Islamic states are polities in which political legitimacy has been derived in significant part from religious law, and they differ widely: Sunni and Shi’i, dynastic and revolutionary, imperial and national. The coercive function has been held by caliphal armies paid through state registers, by corps of slave soldiers, by tribal levies, by dynastic guards and by revolutionary ideological forces. The common thread is not a single institution but a recurring problem: how a ruler whose legitimacy rests on religious law commands armed men whose loyalty may run to tribe, faction or their own corps.

In the early caliphates soldiers were paid from the revenues of conquest through state registers, which made coercion directly dependent on the fiscal system (Kennedy 2001). From the ninth century, rulers came to rely on soldiers of slave origin, recruited from outside the societies they governed. Crone argued that military slavery offered rulers a source of armed force independent of the tribal and local elites whose loyalty they could not trust (Crone 1980), and Pipes traced the genesis of the system (Pipes 1981). The term slave soldier is the scholars’ term for a legal status of military servitude that could lead to command, wealth and rule. It differs from chattel slavery in the household or the field, and this paper uses it only in that technical sense. The Mamluk sultanate that ruled Egypt and Syria from 1250 was governed by such soldiers and their successors (Irwin 1986).

The Ottoman Empire built the most elaborate version. Its kul system recruited the sultan’s servants, including the Janissary infantry, as personal dependents of the ruler, and Kunt traced how the households of these servants transformed provincial government between 1550 and 1650 (Kunt 1983). Agoston showed that the Ottoman state sustained a large military and a domestic weapons industry over centuries, adapting to European military change more successfully than older accounts of decline allowed (Ágoston 2005; Ágoston 2014). Tezcan dates a transformation of the Ottoman polity toward a more limited monarchy to the period from about 1580 to 1826 and treats the deposition and killing of Sultan Osman II in 1622, in which the sultan’s own soldiers took part, as its turning point (Tezcan 2010). The pattern, a dependent corps recruited for loyalty that later acquires interests and power of its own, is the subject of 18.

In the Islamic Republic of Iran the coercive function is divided between the regular army and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, created after the revolution as an ideological guardian of the new order (Katzman 1993; Arjomand 1988). Buchta mapped the parallel institutions through which the Leader, the Guardian Council and the Guard share power (Buchta 2000). Wehrey and colleagues documented the Guard’s expanding domestic political, economic and security roles (Wehrey et al. 2009). Alfoneh argued that the Guard was transforming the state from theocracy into military dictatorship (Alfoneh 2013); his account comes from an advocacy institute and is best read alongside the more cautious RAND assessment. Either way, one organization commanding both coercive forces and large economic holdings raises the integration \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{C}}\), and its ideological mandate under the same leadership raises \(C_{\mathsf{I}\mathsf{C}}\).

Saudi Arabia shows a dynastic version of counterbalancing. The kingdom maintains a regular army and a separate National Guard, a duality that Cordesman described and that Quinlivan treated as an instance of coup-proofing (Cordesman 2003; Quinlivan 1999). Gause placed it within the dynastic security structures of the Gulf monarchies (Gause 1994). The alliance between the Al Saud and the Wahhabi religious establishment supplies the legitimating side (Al-Rasheed 2002). The storming of the Grand Mosque in Mecca in November 1979 by Juhayman al-’Utaybi and his followers, whose origins and legacy Hegghammer and Lacroix reconstruct from fieldwork, challenged both arrangements at once: it contested the rulers’ religious legitimacy and their coercive control of the holy places (Hegghammer and Lacroix 2007).

Accountability in these polities has rested on different mechanisms from those of constitutional democracies. Where scholars of religious law held moral authority independent of the ruler, coercion depended on legitimation (\(\mathsf{I}\to \mathsf{C}\)) supplied by a group the ruler did not command, which is evidence of low rather than high \(C_{\mathsf{I}\mathsf{C}}\) and a potential constraint on the ruler. Its strength varied across these polities and has not been measured on a common scale. Counterbalancing among forces is an internal check that protects rulers more than subjects. The capture-link test can be applied to the dependent-corps episodes. In the Mamluk case the corps used its control of deployment to take rule itself, and the change of rulers is documented (Irwin 1986). In the Ottoman case of 1622 the sultan’s soldiers used their arms against the sultan, and Tezcan documents the lasting change in the distribution of power that followed (Tezcan 2010). Both are capture of the ruler by the coercive corps rather than capture of the coercive pillar by an outside coalition, which is the form 18 predicts. The documented checks are counterbalancing among separate forces and long periods in which the Ottoman state sustained and supplied its military establishment from the center (Ágoston 2005).

5.5 Catholic monarchies

In the Catholic monarchies of early modern Europe royal authority was intertwined with the Church, and the coercive function was held by royal armies officered by nobles and by purchasers of commissions, by mercenary and contracted companies, by Swiss and other foreign troops, by the papal forces of the Papal States, and in Spain by the lay familiars who assisted the Inquisition. Internal diversity was considerable: Habsburg Spain, Bourbon France and the Papal States differed in fiscal structure, in their relations with Rome, and in the strength of their representative assemblies.

Habsburg Spain fought across Europe with an army that depended on long supply lines and on credit. Parker’s study of the Army of Flanders and the Spanish Road between 1567 and 1659 showed how logistics, finance and pay shaped military operations, and how unpaid troops mutinied (Parker 1972). Thompson examined how the Spanish crown governed war between 1560 and 1620, including its reliance on contractors and the sale of offices (Thompson 1976), and Elliott’s survey places these developments in the history of the monarchy (Elliott 1963). Mutiny is a sanction by soldiers against their paymaster. It is also direct evidence for term (a) of 11: when provisioning failed, so did the willingness of the coercers to coerce on the crown’s behalf.

Bourbon France built one of the largest armies in Europe. Lynn traced its growth between 1610 and 1715 (Lynn 1997). Rowlands showed that under Louis XIV royal service and private interest were intertwined: officers invested their own resources in their units, and the crown relied on that investment while trying to control it (Rowlands 2002). Venal commissions integrate finance and force at the level of the individual officer, who is both a creditor of the state and an agent of its force. Parker’s thesis of a military revolution, in which new fortifications and tactics drove the growth of armies and states, frames these developments (Parker 1988), and Tilly’s account of coercion and capital explains why monarchies with access to commercial credit could field such armies (Tilly 1990).

The Papal States combined spiritual and temporal sovereignty. Their military forces, including the Swiss Guard, are documented from 1796 onward in Alvarez’s history, which drew on the Guard’s archive (Alvarez 2011); the verified sources reviewed for this paper do not cover the papal forces of earlier centuries. The Church also wielded sanctions of its own, including excommunication and interdict, which are ideological sanctions in the sense of 1: they impose relational and reputational costs and rely on belief for their force. In Spain the Inquisition drew on lay familiars who assisted its officials; Kamen’s revisionist history of the Inquisition argues that its scale and severity were smaller than the older black legend held (Kamen 1997).

Contestability came from three sources. Representative assemblies, where they retained the power to grant taxes, could limit the provisioning of armies. Contracted soldiers could refuse service when unpaid. And the Church could withhold legitimation, although in practice the crowns of Spain and France exercised considerable control over the churches within their kingdoms. Concentration of command rose over the period as monarchs replaced contracted forces with standing armies, while venality kept part of the control over units in private hands. Ertman’s comparative account of state building shows how the timing of military competition shaped whether such states became patrimonial or bureaucratic (Ertman 1997). The capture-link test is harder to apply here than in the modern cases, because the documented material concerns the structure of command rather than its use against particular groups. What is documented is a standing risk: venal and contracted command made units partly private property, which gave their owners discretion over a public function. The documented check is the mutiny, in which soldiers withdrew coercion from a crown that failed to pay (Parker 1972), together with the fiscal power of estates where it survived and the limits that dependence on credit imposed on royal war-making.

5.6 Secular democracies

In secular democracies authority is distributed among elected officials, courts and private institutions, and the legitimacy of coercion rests on constitutional law rather than on religious or party doctrine. The coercive function is held by professional armed forces under civilian command, police forces whose intrusive measures require judicial authorization, and intelligence services subject to legislative oversight. The defining feature for this pillar is the attempt to raise every component of \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\) by constitutional design.

The core mechanisms are civilian control of the military, judicial warrants for intrusive policing, and legislative oversight of intelligence. Kohn’s account of how democracies control the military stresses that control is a practice of appointment, budget, doctrine and promotion that civilians must actively exercise (Kohn 1997). Born and Caparini compared the models by which democracies try to subject intelligence services to democratic control (Born and Caparini 2007). These mechanisms map onto components (1), (2) and (6) of 3, and their effectiveness depends on the judicial independence analyzed in Part III. Janowitz’s argument that a professional military should remain integrated with civilian society, rather than isolated from it, describes a further safeguard: a force that shares the values of those it might be ordered against is less likely to obey an order to target them (Janowitz 1960).

The best documented capture episode in this regime type, and the best documented check, come from the same inquiry. In 1975 the United States Senate established a select committee, chaired by Frank Church, to study the government’s intelligence operations. Its final report of April 1976 concluded that “the Government has often undertaken the secret surveillance of citizens on the basis of their political beliefs, even when those beliefs posed no threat of violence or illegal acts on behalf of a hostile foreign power” (United States Senate 1976, Book II, Part I, Section C). The committee examined the surveillance record against the lawful character of the targets’ activities, which supplies the material evidence the capture-link test requires. In the notation of 5, the finding describes a positive allegiance margin among non-violators: \(\psi(0,\mathrm{op}) > \psi(0,\mathrm{al})\). The same report documents the check. A legislature with investigative powers exposed the practice, and a permanent Senate intelligence committee followed.

The same report shows why component (2) of \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\) matters, and how an internal substitute for it failed. It found that since the early 1930s intelligence agencies had “frequently wiretapped and bugged American citizens without the benefit of judicial warrant”. Prior written approval by the Attorney General had been required for warrantless wiretaps since 1940, but the committee found the record “replete with instances where this requirement was ignored and the Attorney General gave only after-the-fact authorization” (United States Senate 1976, Book II, Part I). Approval inside the executive is a check exercised by the same coalition that directs the agencies, so it does not lower \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{C}}\). The committee also recorded that recent court decisions had curtailed these techniques against domestic targets. Judicial authorization, held outside the executive, did what the internal check had not.

Rejali’s study of torture in democracies is the hard boundary case. Democracies that were monitored by a free press and human-rights organizations developed interrogation techniques that left few visible marks, so that public monitoring changed the form of abuse rather than ending it (Rejali 2007). Cross-national evidence nonetheless shows that democracy is associated with less repression of personal integrity rights (Poe and Tate 1994), although Hill and Jones caution that the relationship is stronger for some forms of repression than for others (Hill and Jones 2014). Coups are rare in consolidated democracies, and the dataset of Powell and Thyne permits this claim to be tested rather than assumed (Powell and Thyne 2011).

Concentration in secular democracies varies with constitutional structure. Unitary states with national police forces have higher \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) than federal states with local forces, but both may have high \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\). Integration with the political pillar is limited because civilian appointment is shared among elected bodies and constrained by professional norms of neutrality. The dependency on the judicial pillar runs through warrants and liability, and the dependency on legitimation runs through public acceptance of the constitution and of the police, which Tyler’s research measures directly (Tyler 1990). The main risk specific to this type is that oversight bodies depend on information that the overseen agencies control, which is the principal-agent problem of Feaver’s model applied to intelligence (Feaver 2003). The episodes of this subsection and of 5.3 show that risk realized for decades before legislatures intervened.

6 Mechanisms, failure modes and checks

9 requires that violators be sanctioned more than compliers. It says nothing about whom else the apparatus targets. This section shows that the coercive function and its capture are governed by different parameters, states when they nonetheless trade off, derives the main failure modes, and identifies the checks that interrupt them.

6.1 Conduct and allegiance

Return to the targeting probabilities \(\psi(v,y)\) of 5. In the one-period version of the deterrence lemma, an agent with alignment \(y\) faces the margin \(d = \Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu\), so it violates a rule in \(R^0\) if \(\tau > \Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu\).

Proposition 14 (Separability). Fix a magnitude \(\mu > 0\) and a temptation distribution \(G\). (i) The violation rate of agents with alignment \(y\) is \(1 - G(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu)\), which depends on \(\psi\) only through the conduct margin \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\). (ii) For any common conduct margin \(\bar e \in (0,1)\) and any allegiance margin \(x \in [0, 1-\bar e]\), there is a targeting function with \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{al}) = \Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{op}) = \bar e\) and \(\Delta^{\mathrm{a}}(0) = \Delta^{\mathrm{a}}(1) = x\). In particular, the same deterrence of rule violations is compatible with a zero allegiance margin and with a positive one.

Proof. Part (i) follows from 8 with \(d = \Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu\), resolving ties toward compliance. For (ii), set \(\psi(0,\mathrm{al}) = 0\), \(\psi(1,\mathrm{al}) = \bar e\), \(\psi(0,\mathrm{op}) = x\) and \(\psi(1,\mathrm{op}) = \bar e + x\). All four values lie in \([0,1]\) because \(x \le 1 - \bar e\), and the margins are as stated. ◻

The proposition is the pillar-level form of the series claim that A does not imply B. Proposition A, through 9, constrains the conduct margin. A positive allegiance margin is a necessary ingredient of capture in the coercive pillar, and the theorem places no constraint on it. A collective can have an effective coercive pillar with no allegiance margin, and, if it has enough coercive capacity, the same effectiveness with a substantial one. The existence of police tells us nothing about which case obtains; only the margins do.

A positive allegiance margin is not yet capture. Throughout this paper capture means the source’s three-part capture link (Largo 2026). A capture link \((i \to j, \mathsf{C})\) exists when actor \(i\) has discretion over deployment, \(j\)’s allegiance margin is positive because of that discretion in a way that weakens accountability that would otherwise apply, and material evidence connects the two: for example, records showing that \(i\)’s orders produced surveillance, arrests or prosecutions of opponents who had broken no rule in \(R^0\), at rates that differ from those of supporters with the same conduct. The comparative section applies this test to each episode it discusses.

6.2 Arbitrary sanction and the capacity trade-off

The first failure mode occurs when coercion becomes insensitive to conduct within an alignment group. Its consequences are counterintuitive: coercion increases while the enforcement of rules decreases.

Proposition 15 (Arbitrary sanction). Fix an alignment \(y\), hold \(\psi(1,y)\) and \(\mu\) fixed, and suppose \(G\) has a density \(g\). As \(\psi(0,y)\) rises toward \(\psi(1,y)\): (i) the conduct margin \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\) falls to zero and the violation rate of the group rises toward the unsanctioned rate \(p\); (ii) the expected number of coercive acts per member of the group, \[S(y) = \psi(0,y) + \Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\bigl[1 - G(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu)\bigr],\] is non-decreasing in \(\psi(0,y)\).

Proof. (i) \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y) = \psi(1,y) - \psi(0,y)\) is decreasing in \(\psi(0,y)\) and equals zero when the two probabilities are equal. The violation rate \(1 - G(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu)\) is non-increasing in \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\), so it rises, and at \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y) = 0\) it equals \(1 - G(0) = p\). (ii) Write \(\psi_0 = \psi(0,y)\) and \(D = \psi(1,y) - \psi_0\). A member is targeted with probability \(\psi_0\) if it complies and \(\psi_0 + D\) if it violates, so \(S = \psi_0 + D[1 - G(D\mu)]\). Differentiating in \(\psi_0\), with \(\partial D/\partial \psi_0 = -1\), \[\frac{\partial S}{\partial \psi_0} = 1 - \bigl[1 - G(D\mu)\bigr] + D\mu\, g(D\mu) = G(D\mu) + D\mu\, g(D\mu) \ge 0. \qedhere\] ◻

The proposition explains the source paper’s proposal to measure coercive power by discretionary and asymmetric deployment rather than by spending (Largo 2026, Section 4). Arrest counts, prison populations and security budgets can all rise as the coercive apparatus stops responding to conduct. When the group in question is the opposition and \(\psi(0,\mathrm{al})\) does not change, raising \(\psi(0,\mathrm{op})\) toward \(\psi(1,\mathrm{op})\) raises the allegiance margin \(\Delta^{\mathrm{a}}(0)\) and lowers the conduct margin \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{op})\) at the same time. Targeting opponents without regard to their conduct therefore weakens deterrence among opponents, even though it increases coercion against them.

Separability shows that an allegiance margin can be added without weakening deterrence, but only by adding coercive capacity. When capacity is fixed, the two margins trade off. Let the alignment groups have masses \(n_{\mathrm{al}}, n_{\mathrm{op}} > 0\), and suppose that maintaining the targeting probabilities requires monitoring and enforcement capacity \[\Sigma(\psi) = \sum_{y} n_y\bigl[\kappa_1 \psi(1,y) + \kappa_0 \psi(0,y)\bigr] \le \bar{B},\] with \(\kappa_0, \kappa_1 > 0\): capacity is spent on watching and acting against each status class in each group.

Proposition 16 (Capacity trade-off). Suppose the capacity constraint binds at \(\psi\), and the apparatus raises \(\psi(0,\mathrm{op})\) by \(\varepsilon > 0\) without lowering \(\psi(0,\mathrm{al})\), while \(\bar{B}\) is unchanged. Then \(\sum_y n_y \psi(1,y)\) must fall by at least \(\kappa_0 n_{\mathrm{op}}\varepsilon/\kappa_1\). Consequently the conduct margin of at least one group falls, and that group’s violation rate weakly rises.

Proof. The new targeting function \(\psi'\) must satisfy \(\Sigma(\psi') \le \bar{B} = \Sigma(\psi)\). The change in the \(\kappa_0\) terms is at least \(\kappa_0 n_{\mathrm{op}}\varepsilon\), so the change in \(\kappa_1\sum_y n_y\psi(1,y)\) is at most \(-\kappa_0 n_{\mathrm{op}}\varepsilon\), which gives the bound. Since \(\sum_y n_y \psi(1,y)\) falls, \(\psi(1,y)\) falls for some \(y\). For that \(y\), \(\psi(0,y)\) has not fallen, so \(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\) falls, and by 14(i) its violation rate \(1 - G(\Delta^{\mathrm{c}}(y)\mu)\) weakly rises. ◻

The two propositions together give the paper’s claim about allegiance-conditioned coercion its exact scope. Allegiance targeting weakens the enforcement of ordinary rules when coercive capacity is limited, and when opponents are targeted without regard to their conduct. When a regime adds capacity to fund an allegiance margin while keeping its conduct margins, separability says that rule enforcement can survive; what changes is the cost of opposition. Wintrobe’s model of the dictator’s trade-off between repression and the purchase of loyalty reaches related conclusions by a different route (Wintrobe 1998).

A second failure mode concerns supporters. If \(\psi(1,\mathrm{al})\) falls toward \(\psi(0,\mathrm{al})\), supporters enjoy impunity: their conduct margin falls and their violation rate rises toward \(p\). Allegiance-conditioned enforcement can therefore damage compliance on both sides of the political line. Total coercive activity may rise or fall in this case, depending on the relative size of the two groups and on how much the targeting of opponents increases.

6.3 The guardian problem and coup-proofing

Term (a) of 11 makes the loyalty of provisioned coercers depend on their paymaster, and a coercive apparatus strong enough to protect a ruler is strong enough to remove the ruler. Rulers respond by dividing coercive capacity among several organizations that watch one another. The following comparative static states when they divide more.

Proposition 17 (Coup-proofing). A ruler chooses the number \(k \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) of independent armed organizations. Let \(\phi(k)\) be the probability of a successful coup, non-increasing in \(k\), and \(\ell(k)\) any function giving the loss of external military effectiveness. The ruler minimizes \(f(k; \rho) = \rho\, \phi(k) + \ell(k)\), where \(\rho > 0\) is the expected cost of losing office to a coup relative to the stakes of external effectiveness. If \(\rho' > \rho\), then the largest minimizer at \(\rho'\) is at least as large as the largest minimizer at \(\rho\).

Proof. Let \(k\) be the largest minimizer at \(\rho\) and \(k'\) the largest minimizer at \(\rho'\), and suppose \(k' < k\). Optimality gives \(f(k; \rho) \le f(k'; \rho)\) and \(f(k'; \rho') \le f(k; \rho')\). Adding the two inequalities and cancelling the \(\ell\) terms yields \((\rho' - \rho)\bigl(\phi(k') - \phi(k)\bigr) \le 0\). Since \(k' < k\) and \(\phi\) is non-increasing, \(\phi(k') \ge \phi(k)\), and since \(\rho' > \rho\) the product is non-negative, so it is zero and \(\phi(k') = \phi(k)\). Then \(f(k; \rho') - f(k'; \rho') = \ell(k) - \ell(k') = f(k; \rho) - f(k'; \rho) \le 0\), so \(k\) also minimizes \(f(\cdot\,; \rho')\). This contradicts the choice of \(k'\) as the largest minimizer at \(\rho'\), since \(k > k'\). ◻

The proof uses no property of \(\ell\); the effectiveness cost matters for the level of \(k\) but not for the direction of the comparative static. The parameter \(\rho\) is the salience of the elite threat: it rises with the probability that the ruler’s own armed allies could and would remove the ruler, which can be proxied by recent coup attempts in the polity or its neighbors (Powell and Thyne 2011), and it falls with the stakes of interstate war. The model has one threat parameter and does not represent mass threat. It therefore captures the fragmentation side of Greitens’s argument, that elite threats produce fragmented coercive institutions (Greitens 2016), but not the prediction that mass threats produce unified and intrusive ones. It is consistent with Quinlivan’s account of coup-proofing in Middle Eastern regimes (Quinlivan 1999) and with the finding of Pilster and Böhmelt that coup-proofing has battlefield costs (Pilster and Böhmelt 2011). It describes the Saudi army and National Guard, the Iranian army and Revolutionary Guard, and the division of Soviet coercion among army, security service and police. In democracies the analogous counterweights are civilian ministries, separate services and judicial oversight. The comparative static would be falsified by evidence that the number and independence of armed organizations are unrelated to measured elite threat once external threat is held constant.

Coup-proofing raises \(k\) but does not raise \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\). Counterweights protect the ruler from the guardians; they do not protect citizens from the ruler. In a party-state or a dynastic monarchy, all \(k\) organizations may answer to the same apex. Then the concentration \(H^{\mathsf{C}}_{(1)}\) among the organizations below the apex falls, while \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\), which attributes their control to the apex under 2, stays high, and so does the integration \(C_{\mathsf{P}\mathsf{C}}\). This is the reason the source paper requires both \(U_t\) and \(\mathcal{I}_t\) for a strong classification: a measure of dispersion below the apex would miss the concentration above it.

6.4 Dependent corps and the outsider strategy

Rulers have long tried to solve the guardian problem by recruiting coercers who have no independent social base: foreigners, captives, slaves or men cut off from their families. The Abbasid and Mamluk slave soldiers, the Ottoman kul and the papal Swiss Guard are instances. The following proposition states why the strategy works at first and why it tends to fail later.

Proposition 18 (Dependent corps). A corps receives compensation \(w_t \le \bar{w}\) per period from a ruler whose fiscal capacity bounds \(\bar{w}\). The corps accumulates capacity \(Z_t\): arms, offices, property, marriages and local recruitment. The value to the corps of seizing control is \(\Gamma(Z_t)\), non-decreasing in \(Z_t\), and an attempt costs \(\chi > 0\). The corps remains subordinate in period \(t\) only if \(\sum_{k \ge 0}\delta^k w_{t+k} \ge \Gamma(Z_t) - \chi\). If \(Z_t\) is non-decreasing and \(\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma(Z_t) > \bar{w}/(1-\delta) + \chi\), then there is a finite \(T\) such that for all \(t \ge T\) the corps is not subordinate, unless the ruler lowers \(Z_t\), raises \(\chi\), or raises the ceiling \(\bar{w}\).

Proof. The left side of the subordination condition is at most \(\bar{w}/(1-\delta)\). Because \(\Gamma(Z_t)\) is non-decreasing in \(t\) and its limit exceeds \(\bar{w}/(1-\delta) + \chi\), there is a finite \(T\) with \(\Gamma(Z_T) - \chi > \bar{w}/(1-\delta)\), and the inequality persists for all \(t \ge T\). The condition can be restored only by changing one of \(Z_t\), \(\chi\) or \(\bar{w}\). ◻

The proposition is simple, and its interest lies in what it explains. It treats an attempt as successful; a probability of success below one lowers the expected value of seizure and delays \(T\) without changing the logic. Recruiting outsiders lowers \(Z_0\): a corps with no kin, land or local allies gains little from seizing power because it cannot rule alone. This is the logic Crone attributed to the adoption of military slavery (Crone 1980). Over time the corps acquires exactly the assets that raise \(Z_t\). The value of seizing a polity is bounded by what the polity produces, so the threshold condition, not unbounded growth, is what matters: the corps must come to value control more than the most the ruler can pay. The ruler’s three countermeasures correspond to the three escape clauses. Rotation of commanders, prohibition of property holding and periodic purges keep \(Z_t\) low. Counterweights of the kind analyzed in 17 raise the cost \(\chi\) of an attempt. Fiscal expansion raises \(\bar{w}\). The Mamluks, who became rulers of Egypt and Syria (Irwin 1986), and the Janissaries, whose part in the deposition and killing of Osman II in 1622 marked a lasting change in Ottoman politics (Tezcan 2010), are the paradigm cases. The proposition also applies to modern forces whose economic holdings grow, since business activity raises \(Z_t\) directly. This is the mechanism behind the concern that the economic expansion of Iran’s Revolutionary Guard increases its political autonomy (Wehrey et al. 2009; Alfoneh 2013). In democracies the analogous measures are term limits for commanders, prohibitions on military business and civilian control of promotions.

6.5 Coercion and production

The pair of coercion and production is the primary responsibility of this Part among the fifteen cross-pillar pairs. The dependencies run in both directions, and the integration coefficient \(C_{\mathsf{C}\mathsf{W}}\) measures something narrower than either.

From production to coercion (\(\mathsf{W}\to \mathsf{C}\)), every army and police force consumes labor time. Conscription takes it directly from the producing population as an obligation. Professional forces take it through wages paid from taxes. Slave-soldier systems took it by acquiring people as a resource. The mode of recruitment shapes accountability: a conscript army is drawn from the whole population and depends on its consent, while a professional or outsider force depends on its paymaster. This is a further reason why term (a) of 11 carries political consequences.

From coercion to production (\(\mathsf{C}\to \mathsf{W}\)), sanctions can compel work. The categories must be kept distinct, and this paper uses the bondage spectrum of Part VI (Definition 2.4). Wage labor and taxation leave lawful mobility and personal ownership with the producer; the sanctions behind them are the general law of contract and of revenue. Serfdom ties the producer to land or lord. Compelled state labor, which includes corvée, labor drafts and penal labor, is compelled by sanction for a term. Chattel slavery makes the person property. Only the last three are forms of bondage, and the coercive pillar is directly engaged in each of them. The Soviet camp system combined penal labor with economic planning on a scale that made coercion a direct input to production (Applebaum 2003; Gregory and Lazarev 2003). Following Part VI, the series reserves the word bondage for forms that legally restrict mobility and compel labor, and does not call wage labor or taxation bondage or slavery. Part VI treats the production side of the pair, including the exit and voice that producers can use against dependence (Part VI, Definition 2.5).

A high value of \(C_{\mathsf{C}\mathsf{W}}\) means that the same coalition commands both the coercive apparatus and the allocation of labor. In the extreme case, the deterrence margin of 4 is applied to failures to work. Proposition B for this pair requires such integration together with a positive allegiance margin, so that the obligation to work falls more heavily on opponents, and with low contestability. The Gulag combined all three, since the same party-state directed the security organs that filled the camps and the plans that used their labor, and political offenses were among the grounds of confinement (Applebaum 2003). Universal conscription in a democracy with high \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\) involves the dependency \(\mathsf{W}\to \mathsf{C}\) but neither high integration nor an allegiance margin.

6.6 Checks that interrupt transmission

The source paper’s second proposition holds that effective independent review and voice reduce the probability that attempted capture yields persistent unreviewable outcomes (Largo 2026). In the coercive pillar the checks act on the margins directly. Judicial authorization of intrusive measures raises the evidentiary threshold for coercive acts, which lowers \(\psi(0,y)\) for everyone and so raises the conduct margin. Independent complaint bodies make a positive allegiance margin visible and therefore costly. Individual liability of agents for unlawful force raises the private cost of arbitrary coercion for the coercers themselves. Protected refusal of unlawful orders introduces a veto at the point of execution: when soldiers or police refuse to fire on peaceful crowds, the allegiance margin is cut off at its source, which is the mechanism that Chenoweth and Stephan and Albrecht and Ohl identify in security-force defection (Chenoweth and Stephan 2011; Albrecht and Ohl 2016).

These checks have small-scale analogues. Ostrom’s design principles for long-enduring commons include monitors who are accountable to users and graduated sanctions that begin with small penalties (Ostrom 1990; Ostrom 2010). Graduated sanctions keep the magnitude \(\mu\) proportional to the violation, which limits the damage when \(\psi(0,y)\) is positive by mistake. Accountability of monitors to users is a small-scale version of component (1) of \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\). The reverse dominance hierarchy of foraging bands is a check of the same kind: the collective holds the sanction and directs it against those who try to concentrate it (Boehm 1999). Madison’s argument that ambition must be made to counteract ambition is the constitutional statement of the same principle (Madison 1788).

Checks can fail. Each depends on another pillar: judicial authorization on judicial independence (Part III), complaints and refusal on legitimating beliefs (Part IV), and appointments on the political pillar (Part II). Capture of any of these pillars by the coalition that commands coercion removes the corresponding check. This is the mechanism by which high cross-pillar integration \(\mathcal{I}_t\) turns a concentrated coercive pillar into an unaccountable one, and it is why the coercive pillar cannot be assessed in isolation.

The allegiance margin is the hardest object to measure and the most important. Two designs are available. The first compares the treatment of opponents and supporters with matched conduct, as recorded by a source independent of the coercive apparatus: a court that acquits, a victimization survey, or an archive of the targets’ own activities. The Senate inquiry of 1975 to 1976 is an example of the third kind, since it compared the surveillance record with the lawful character of the targets’ activities (United States Senate 1976). The second design adapts the outcome test from the economics of discrimination. Knowles, Persico and Todd showed that if officers maximize successful searches, the rate at which searches find contraband should be equal across groups in the absence of bias (Knowles et al. 2001). The analogous test here compares the rate at which coercive acts against opponents and supporters are later upheld by independent review. A systematically lower rate for opponents indicates that opponents are targeted on weaker evidence, which is a positive \(\Delta^{\mathrm{a}}(0)\). The test has two known weaknesses. Average rates can differ even without bias if the marginal cases differ across groups, the problem of infra-marginality, so the test is informative only under the conditions that Knowles, Persico and Todd state for equilibrium search behavior. And the independent review may itself be captured, which is the concern of Part III; the test is only as good as the judicial contestability \(Q^{\mathsf{J}}\) of the reviewing body. Repression scales (Poe and Tate 1994; Hill and Jones 2014) and V-Dem indicators (Coppedge et al. 2011) measure the level of coercion against citizens but do not separate the two margins, so they are useful as outcomes rather than as measures of capture. Coup data (Powell and Thyne 2011) measure the guardian problem directly.

7.2 Hypotheses with falsifiers

The hypotheses below refine the source paper’s generic hypotheses H1 to H6 for the coercive pillar; the parent hypothesis is given in brackets. Each states the result that would count against it.

HC1 (threat environment; H3).

Rulers who face greater elite threat build fragmented and loyalty-based coercive institutions; rulers who face mass threat build unified and intrusive ones (Greitens 2016). The first half is motivated by 17; the second half is not modeled here. Falsifier: the number and independence of armed organizations are unrelated to measured elite threat once external threat is controlled.

HC2 (coup-proofing trade-off; H6).

Coup-proofing lowers coup success and also lowers military effectiveness (Quinlivan 1999; Pilster and Böhmelt 2011). Falsifier: higher counterbalancing shows no association with battlefield performance in comparable conflicts.

HC3 (security-force defection; H5).

The cohesion, composition and professionalism of security forces predict whether they defect or repress during mass unrest (Bellin 2012; Albrecht and Ohl 2016; Chenoweth and Stephan 2011). Falsifier: these characteristics show no association with defection across episodes.

HC4 (fiscal basis; H3).

Coercive organizations financed by taxation of a broad population face more contestation than those financed by resource rents or foreign transfers (Tilly 1990; Karaman and Pamuk 2013). The hypothesis is motivated by term (a) of 11, which ties the loyalty of coercers to their provisioning, but the proposition does not model contestation. Falsifier: the source of financing is unrelated to \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\).

HC5 (dependent corps; H2).

Corps recruited for dependence become political actors as their capacity grows, unless rulers rotate, disperse or replace them, raise the cost of an attempt, or raise pay (Crone 1980; Pipes 1981). This is the empirical counterpart of 18. Falsifier: outsider corps whose holdings and offices grow remain subordinate without such countermeasures.

HC6 (democracy and repression; H1).

Democracy and judicial independence reduce repression (Poe and Tate 1994; Hill and Jones 2014). Falsifier: no effect once civil conflict is controlled. Rejali’s evidence on torture in democracies is a boundary case that the hypothesis must accommodate (Rejali 2007).

HC7 (integration of finance and force; H2).

Business activity by coercive organizations raises \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{C}}\) and lowers coercive contestability. Falsifier: military business holdings are unrelated to \(Q^{\mathsf{C}}\). Rival explanation: such holdings supply public goods or substitute for an inadequate budget.

HC8 (checks; H1 and H4).

Independent complaint mechanisms and judicial authorization lower the allegiance margin. Falsifier: the margin is unchanged after such reforms are introduced. As the source paper warns, reforms can raise reported abuses by making them visible, so reporting effects must be separated from changes in behavior (Largo 2026).

HC9 (volume and compliance; H1).

When coercive acts against opponents rise while independently measured compliance with the reference rules falls among opponents, and neither changes among supporters with matched conduct, the allegiance margin is rising and the conduct margin among opponents is falling. The comparison with supporters is needed because a temptation shock, such as a crime wave, also raises both violations and coercive acts with no allegiance margin at all; it affects both groups. This is the pattern 15 predicts when opponents are targeted without regard to conduct. Falsifier: in documented episodes of politically selective repression, opponents’ compliance with the reference rules does not fall relative to supporters’.

HC10 (separability; H6).

Across polities, measured conduct margins and measured allegiance margins are weakly correlated: the absolute correlation is below a preregistered threshold, for example 0.5, and polities with a conduct margin above the sample median include some with an allegiance margin indistinguishable from zero. This is the empirical content of 14 and of the claim that A does not imply B. Falsifier: the two margins are so strongly correlated that effective rule enforcement is never observed without a positive allegiance margin, which would suggest that capture accompanies coercion structurally.

HC10 is the hypothesis whose failure would most damage this paper’s argument. The separability proposition shows that the margins can vary independently, but if they never do in practice, the distinction between necessity and capture would be logical rather than empirical.

8 Revelation 13 as typological image

The series reads Revelation typologically. It does not identify the beast, the mark or Babylon with any present government, party, technology, person, religion or institution. Futurist readings that make such identifications have a long history of failure (Boyer 1992; Fuller 1995; McGinn 1998), and historicist readings that identified the beast with particular churches belong to the history of polemic (Kovacs and Rowland 2004). In the preterist reading, which supplies the historical anchor, the first beast is Roman imperial power and the setting is the imperial cult of the cities of Asia Minor (Bauckham 1993; Friesen 2001; Koester 2014). In the typological reading the series adopts, the beast is a recurring pattern of power that claims ultimate loyalty (Bauckham 1993; Wink 1992). The use of the image here is an interpretive claim of this project, not a settled exegetical result.

Revelation 13 is saturated with coercion. The first beast is given authority to make war: “it was given unto him to make war with the saints, and to overcome them: and power was given him over all kindreds, and tongues, and nations” (Rev. 13:7, KJV). Its worshippers ask, “Who is like unto the beast? who is able to make war with him?” (13:4). The second beast exercises the authority of the first and causes the earth to worship it, and it brings an image to life that should “cause that as many as would not worship the image of the beast should be killed” (13:15). The chapter ends with the mark, without which “no man might buy or sell” (13:17). Kraybill has read these passages against the economic life of the province, in which participation in trade was entangled with the imperial cult (Kraybill 1996).

The coercive content of 13:15 has a precise structure in the terms of 5. Inside the image’s own order, refusal to worship is the violation. But that rule is a rule whose content is allegiance, which is exactly the kind of rule excluded from the reference set \(R^0\). Measured against allegiance-neutral rules, the death sanction of 13:15 is conditioned entirely on allegiance and not at all on conduct, and the enactment of the rule is itself an image of rule-making capture. The economic exclusion of 13:16 to 17 has the same structure: the cost falls on those without the mark, whatever they have done. The text’s image of the beast is, among other things, an image of a coercive apparatus whose conduct margin has been displaced by an allegiance margin.

Another canonical text, Paul’s letter to the Romans, describes the coercive function with the opposite conditioning. Romans 13 says that “rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil,” and that the ruler “beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil” (Rom. 13:3 to 4, KJV). The sword in Romans 13 is conditioned on conduct: it falls on the one who does evil. The sword in Revelation 13 is conditioned on worship: it falls on the one who will not bow. Readers have long asked how the two chapters fit together. Yoder read Romans 13 as a call to be subject to authorities whose power is itself ordered under God, not as an endorsement of whatever those authorities do (Yoder 1972), a reading that leaves room for Revelation’s judgment on authority that demands worship. In the framework of this paper the chapters describe the same function under different parameters. Romans 13 describes a sanction regime with a positive conduct margin and no allegiance margin. 9 shows that every persistent collective needs the first property; the second is what distinguishes accountable coercion from captured coercion. Revelation 13 describes a regime in which the allegiance margin has displaced the conduct margin, and [prop:coercive-power:arbitrary,prop:coercive-power:capacity] show why such a regime weakens the enforcement of the ordinary rules it claims to protect. The reading is consistent with the series distinction between Proposition A, the universal function, and Proposition B, its contingent capture.

The same chapter addresses those who suffer coercion: “He that leadeth into captivity shall go into captivity: he that killeth with the sword must be killed with the sword. Here is the patience and the faith of the saints” (13:10). The KJV wording follows one textual tradition. The verse echoes Jeremiah 15:2, its wording varies among manuscripts, and many modern editions and commentators read it as a call to the faithful to accept captivity and death rather than as a statement that the coercer will meet the same fate; the commentaries discuss the problem at this verse (Aune 1998; Koester 2014). Because the reading is uncertain, this paper draws no institutional inference from it. On either reading the verse rules out armed resistance by the faithful, which places the response it commends in endurance and witness rather than in counter-coercion.

Revelation 17 supplies a closer image of the guardian problem of 6. The ten kings “have one mind, and shall give their power and strength unto the beast” (17:13), and yet “the ten horns which thou sawest upon the beast, these shall hate the whore, and shall make her desolate and naked” (17:16). Allies who lend their force to a power can turn it against the power they served. Read typologically, the passage is an image of the dependent-corps dynamic of 18, in which those who supply coercion come to hold it on their own account. This too is the project’s interpretive claim, not an exegetical result. Collins argued that the Apocalypse addressed a perceived crisis and offered its readers a form of catharsis (Collins 1984), and Thompson questioned whether the Domitianic period saw the systematic persecution that older readings assumed (Thompson 1990). On either reading the text’s concern is not the existence of the sword but its direction: whom it strikes, and why.

9 Objections and scope conditions

The first objection is that the necessity theorem is trivial, since any cost can be called a sanction. The definition blocks this. A sanction must respond to an observed or alleged violation, and the deterrence margin is the difference in continuation payoffs between compliance and violation. Both are observable. The theorem can fail, and 3.5 states the observation that would refute it, together with the requirement that temptation be measured independently of sanction practices so that no compliant collective can be reclassified after the fact. Its content lies in Ax5 and the bridge assumption, which are empirical claims about temptations, and in the conclusion that the sanction regime must shift at least \(p - \theta\) of probability mass toward compliance.

The second objection is Hobbesian: order requires a sovereign with a monopoly of force, so the distributed sanctions of stateless societies are a primitive stage rather than a functional equivalent. The evidence of 1 is against this. Distributed sanctions sustained order in many societies over long periods, and the theorem requires a positive margin, not a concentrated holder. The objection has more force at scale, and 11 explains why: as collectives grow, term (d) weakens, and where sanctions are costly public goods and higher-order sanctions are absent (12), coercion must be provisioned or legitimated. That is a reason for specialization, not for concentration without accountability.

The third objection comes from the opposite direction. Clastres, Graeber and Wengrow, and anarchist anthropology more generally, argue that hierarchy is not inevitable and that many societies deliberately prevented the emergence of coercive authority (Clastres 1987; Graeber and Wengrow 2021). The paper agrees about hierarchy and disagrees about coercion. The societies they describe sanction those who try to command; they do not lack sanctions. Their achievement is a coercive pillar with \(H^{\mathsf{C}}\) near \(1/n\) and contestability near one, which is the clearest empirical evidence that Proposition B is contingent.

The fourth objection is Marxian: coercion in class societies serves the ruling class, so the distinction between rule enforcement and capture is ideological. The framework accommodates the claim as a hypothesis about the allegiance margin. If coercion is applied more heavily to one class for the same conduct, or if the rules themselves were written to protect one class’s property, the first case shows up as a positive allegiance margin indexed by class, and the second belongs to the analysis of rule-making in Part II. In both cases the claim becomes measurable. What the framework rejects is the inference from the existence of police to the existence of class capture, which is the inference from A to B.

The fifth objection is the public-goods objection: concentrated coercion provides security, which benefits everyone, so it should not be treated with suspicion. The paper accepts the premise. The source paper’s fourth proposition and hypothesis H6 state that concentration with high-quality review can coexist with competent services and low arbitrary sanctioning (Largo 2026), and the social democracies of 5.3 are the leading illustration. The margins framework is designed to distinguish this case from capture rather than to presume either.

Five scope conditions limit the results. The capacity trade-off of 16 rests on a linear capacity constraint, which is an assumption about the technology of enforcement rather than a derived result. The model treats a single rule with a binary violation decision, while real polities enforce many rules of different gravity, and the tolerance \(\theta\) differs among them. Equilibria in repeated games are typically multiple, so the theorem establishes that some sanction regime is necessary without predicting which one arises. The analysis concerns enforcement inside a polity; war between polities enters only through the guardian problem and the fiscal basis of coercion. And the conduct margin can be measured only with an independent record of conduct, which is often unavailable precisely where capture is most likely.

10 Conclusion

Every governance system settles who enforces its rules, because when members are sometimes tempted to break rules, compliance requires that violators’ prospects fall because of what they did. The answer need not be a police force. Gossip, ridicule, ostracism and coalition action satisfy the deterrence lemma as well as armies do, and the societies that seem to lack coercion are those in which the collective holds it and directs it against concentration. The function is universal; its institutional form varies.

Capture in the coercive pillar requires a change in what coercion responds to. The conduct margin measures enforcement of rules, the allegiance margin measures enforcement of loyalty, and the two are separable: necessity constrains the first and is silent about the second. When capacity is limited, allegiance targeting weakens deterrence for at least one group; when opponents are targeted regardless of conduct, it also raises the volume of coercion against them. That is why spending and arrest counts mismeasure coercive power, and why the contrast between Romans 13 and Revelation 13 concerns the direction of the sword rather than its existence.

The comparison of six regime types shows the same function distributed in different ways: through private producers and civilian control in market-capitalist societies, party organs in communist states, ombudsmen, parliamentary oversight and conscription in social democracies, counterbalanced and dependent corps in Islamic states, venal and contracted command in Catholic monarchies, and constitutional oversight in secular democracies. What remains uncertain is the size of the two margins in any of them. Measuring the allegiance margin, against an independent record of conduct, is the task on which the empirical claims of this paper depend.
