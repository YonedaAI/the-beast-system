Who interprets the law? Part III of the Beast System series derives the judicial function from a simple fact: general rules cannot settle particular disputes by themselves, because every rule admits more than one reasonable reading at its edges.

Under three explicit assumptions (open texture produces disputes the parties cannot settle alone, self-help is costly to the collective, and exit is costly), any collective that persists indefinitely resolves all but finitely many such disputes through a determination procedure that neither party controls. The derivation requires a function, not an office. Leopard-skin chiefs, Barotse moots, Ju/'hoansi talking-out, qadi courts, the Parlement of Paris and modern constitutional courts all perform it.

Two results narrow the claim. When exit is costless the forcing argument fails, which matches the thin adjudication of immediate-return foragers. When rules are executed by code, interpretation relocates to the classification of inputs instead of disappearing.

The paper then operationalizes judicial contestability as a chain of disclosure, access, independence and compliance by the powerful, and proves that the probability a captured ruling persists depends on the product of these links. An additive index can score a judiciary well while a single broken link leaves capture unchecked. Across six regime types, from market capitalism to historical Catholic monarchies, the function is found everywhere, with wide variation in concentration and in coupling to the other pillars. Eight hypotheses state what would count against the capture claims.

The ideology changes. The institutional functions remain.
