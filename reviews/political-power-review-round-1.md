# Peer Review: *Political Power and Rule Making (Part II of The Six Pillars of the Beast System)*

## Summary Evaluation

This paper isolates the rule-making function ($\pP$) within a six-pillar model of governance, distinguishes the functional necessity of rule making (Proposition A) from the concentration and capture of rule-making authority (Proposition B), formalizes a de jure baseline for concentration using Shapley–Shubik power indices, models three mechanisms of oligarchic drift and status-quo lock-in, and compares six regime types qualitatively before stating seven testable hypotheses and a typological reading of Revelation 13 and 17.

The central analytical distinction—separating whether a collective must make binding decisions from how concentration and contestability are distributed across procedures—is sound and productive. However, the manuscript has formal gaps in its definitions and proofs, leans heavily on unreferenced companion papers for core definitions and parent hypotheses, leaves its quantitative apparatus unapplied, violates the house style in several multi-topic and signposting paragraphs, and contains systematic LaTeX and bibliography defects.

## 1. Thematic Evaluation

### Mathematical Correctness
The proofs of Proposition 2.4 (lines 161–171) and Proposition 3.8 (lines 323–325) are elementary and valid as written, including the permutation-exchange argument for veto players in Proposition 2.4(e) (line 170). Several formal definitions and proofs, however, require correction or tightening:
1. **Regularity gap between Definition 2.2(b), Lemma 3.5, and Theorem 3.7 (lines 104, 245, 266, 282):** Definition 2.2 requires that $\pP$ be implemented "with nontrivial regularity within $T$." Assumption 3.1 explicitly includes "with nontrivial regularity" for collective choices (line 240), and Lemma 3.4 invokes it (line 261). By contrast, Assumption 3.2 posits only a single pair of periods $s < s'$ with disjoint viability sets $\mathcal{V}(e_s) \cap \mathcal{V}(e_{s'}) = \emptyset$ (line 245), and Lemma 3.5 proves only that $R_t$ changes *at least once* on $T$ (line 270). Theorem 3.7 (line 282) then claims that the collective implements $\pP$ in sense (b) of Definition 2.2. A single rule change on $T$ does not satisfy "with nontrivial regularity."
2. **Improper simple games in Definition 2.3 and Corollary 2.5 (lines 144, 177):** Definition 2.3 defines a simple game $\Win$ solely by $\emptyset \notin \Win$, $N \in \Win$, and monotonicity, omitting *properness* ($S \in \Win \implies N \setminus S \notin \Win$). In an improper simple game (for example, $\Win = \{S \subseteq N : |S| \ge 1\}$), disjoint coalitions can simultaneously win and enact contradictory alternatives from $X$, failing to select a well-defined collective decision under Definition 2.1.
3. **"Preferences diverge" vs. absence of a unanimous winner in Theorem 3.7 (lines 104, 240, 282):** Assumption 3.1 defines non-unanimity precisely: for every $x \in X$, some member strictly prefers another $y \in X$ to $x$ (no unanimously top-ranked alternative). Definition 2.2(a) and Theorem 3.7 state only that "members' preferences diverge." Preferences over $\mathcal{V}(e_{t+1})$ can diverge on lower-ranked alternatives while every member agrees on the unique best rule system $R^\ast$, in which case Lemma 3.4 does not apply.
4. **Tautological structure of Proposition 6.5 (lines 553–565):** Because cross-pillar integration $C_{mn,t}$ is left without a mathematical formula in Section 2.1 (line 91) and deferred to Part VII (line 551), Proposition 6.5 assumes (i) $C_{\pP n} \ge \min\{s^{\pP}(S), \kappa_n(S)\}$ and (ii) $\kappa_n(S) \ge \lambda\alpha_{S,n}$, then deduces $C_{\pP n} \ge \min\{s^{\pP}(S), \lambda\alpha_{S,n}\}$. This is a one-line substitution into a monotone function of two assumed inequalities rather than a substantive theorem derived from the network primitives $A^m_t$.
5. **Boundary indifference and member payoff modification in Proposition 6.2 (lines 506, 511, 515):** First, at the boundary $\beta\rho\delta = m$, the opposition faction's net expected payoff from monitoring is $\beta\rho\delta - m = 0$, making it indifferent between monitoring and not monitoring; strict incentive requires $\beta\rho\delta > m$ or an explicit tie-breaking assumption. Second, introducing an opposition faction that takes share $\beta$ of $\rho$ upon replacing the leadership alters the ordinary members' returned-rent share from $\rho/n$ to $(1-\beta)\rho/n$, which should be stated explicitly in the model setup.
6. **Notation collision on $n$ (lines 86, 91, 99, 144, 203, 506, 555):** The symbol $n$ denotes both the cardinality of the collective ($|N|=n$) in Sections 2.2, 2.3, and 6.1 and a generic pillar index ($n \in \Mset$) in Sections 2.1, 2.5, and 6.3.

### Clarity and Completeness
The paper is not self-contained. It cites specific numbered sections, definitions, and propositions in five companion papers—Part I (lines 93, 221, 352, 397), Part III (lines 71, 75, 210, 273, 305, 547), Part IV (lines 217, 225, 273, 358, 632), Part V (lines 75, 210, 227), and Part VII (lines 69, 184, 236, 519, 551)—none of which appear in the bibliography. In Section 7.2 (lines 614–620), hypotheses HP1–HP7 reference "Parent: H1," "Parent: H2," "Parent: H3," and "Parent: H6" from `largo2026hbs` without stating what H1, H2, H3, or H6 are. Finally, while the paper defines $H^{\pP}$, $Q^{\pP}_t$, $\alpha_{S,n}$, and $U_t$, it provides not even a single worked numerical example (for instance, computing $H^{\pP}$ de jure for the U.S. federal legislative process or the Iranian legislative-vetting process) to demonstrate how the formal apparatus operates on an actual constitution.

### Logical Structure
The section ordering separates the formal apparatus into two disconnected halves: Section 2 defines the network layer, Section 3 proves necessity, Section 4 reviews the literature, Section 5 conducts the six-regime historical comparison, and Section 6 returns to formal propositions on mechanisms. Consequently, Section 5 repeatedly relies on formal results that have not yet been stated or proved: line 405 invokes "the loyalty parameter of Proposition 6.5" (`prop:political-power:appointment-bound`), and line 445 invokes "the trade-off between them [that] is the subject of Proposition 6.4" (`prop:political-power:veto`). Moving Section 6 ("Mechanisms, failure modes and checks") ahead of Section 5 ("Rule making across six regime types") would eliminate these forward dependencies and allow the comparative case studies to test the formal mechanisms in sequence.

### LaTeX Quality
The LaTeX source has several technical and typographical flaws:
- **Unformatted math in the abstract (line 59):** Variables and expressions (`n`, `k`, `1`, `1/k`) are set in plain text rather than math mode (`$n$`, `$k$`, `$1$`, `$1/k$`).
- **Unused preamble packages and environments (lines 12–15, 18–19, 24, 34):** `graphicx`, `xcolor`, `tikz`, `longtable`, `listings` (with `\lstset`), `cleveref`, and `\newtheorem{remark}[theorem]{Remark}` are loaded or declared but never used.
- **Hyphens in place of en-dashes:** Every page range, issue range, and date range in the bibliography (lines 665–1075) uses a single hyphen (`100-111`, `1981-1991`, `4-5`) instead of an en-dash (`--`). Compound eponyms in the text (`Herfindahl-Hirschman` on line 91; `Shapley-Shubik` on lines 142, 144, 217, 595; `Cauchy-Schwarz` on line 164) also use hyphens instead of en-dashes, and line 634 writes `(Rev.\ 17:12 to 13)` instead of `(Rev.\ 17:12--13)`.
- **Corrupted punctuation and missing diacritics in the bibliography (lines 665–1075):** Thirty-nine bibliography entries have double periods (`..`) after an author's middle initial (e.g., `Bartels, Larry M.. 2008.` on line 687; `Hart, H. L. A.. 1961.` on line 837), four entries have `?.` after question marks in titles (lines 675, 717, 1008, 1065), and several entries italicize editors, translators, publishers, and page numbers inside `\emph{...}` (lines 708, 714, 732, 846, 876, 948, 966, 1056). Diacritics are omitted in `Jacques-Benigne` (line 714), `Gosta` (line 786), `Janos` (line 891), and `Luhrmann` (line 936).

### Prose Quality and House Style Alignment
Much of the prose is commendably direct, avoids ornate jargon, and keeps sentence structure lean. However, the manuscript violates the house style in four recurring ways:
1. **Multi-claim residual paragraphs:** Several section-ending paragraphs (e.g., lines 329, 431, 441, 651) bundle three to five distinct claims—methodological caveats, historiography, empirical checks, and literature disputes—into a single paragraph rather than developing one identifiable claim per paragraph.
2. **Metadiscourse and roadmap signposting:** Paragraph 7 of the Introduction (line 77) is a pure table-of-contents roadmap ("The contributions are the following. The paper proves... It shows... It proves... It compares... It states... It closes...").
3. **Qualification or objection stated before the finding:** In Section 9 (lines 641–649), five consecutive paragraphs open by stating a hypothetical objection ("The necessity theorem may seem vacuous...", "The boundary... may seem artificial...") and delay the author's actual claim to a mid-paragraph formula ("The reply is that...").
4. **Repetitive passive templates:** Section 2.6 (lines 223, 225, 227, 229) opens four consecutive paragraphs with the identical passive construction "Rule making and [pillar] are coupled through..."

## 2. Weakest Three Paragraphs and Model Rewrite

### Weakest Paragraph 1 (Line 77)
> The contributions are the following. The paper proves a necessity lemma for rule making from the plurality axiom, states its scope, and tests it against the stateless and consensus societies that are the strongest apparent counterexamples (Section~\ref{sec:necessity}). It shows with elementary results on power indices that necessity is compatible with every level of concentration from $1/n$ to $1$, which is the rule-making instance of the non-implication of Proposition B by Proposition A (Section~\ref{sec:layer}). It proves three conditional propositions that identify mechanisms by which rule making can drift toward capture and the checks that interrupt them (Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms}). It compares six regime types on the same questions (Section~\ref{sec:regimes}). It states seven hypotheses with falsifiers (Section~\ref{sec:measurement}). It closes with a typological reading of Revelation 13 and 17 (Section~\ref{sec:revelation}).

**Why it fails the house style:** This paragraph contains no substantive claim supported by evidence or reasoning; it is pure signposting ("The contributions are the following. The paper proves... It shows... It proves... It compares... It states... It closes...") and lists sections out of numerical order.

### Weakest Paragraph 2 (Line 329)
> The coding $H^{\pP}=1/n_a$ is a de jure lower bound. Consensus in ethnographic description often coexists with unequal influence by age, gender, skill, and kinship, so actual concentration is higher than the formal minimum. The ethnographic record is also limited in another way. It is drawn largely from groups observed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many of them in marginal environments and in contact with states, and the variation among foragers is large \citep{kelly1995}. Graeber and Wengrow argue that prehistoric societies moved deliberately between egalitarian and hierarchical arrangements, sometimes seasonally \citep{graeber2021}, and their reading of the evidence is disputed. Their argument, if accepted, strengthens rather than weakens the separation between the necessity of the function and the contingency of its concentration. Scott's account of upland Southeast Asia describes peoples who chose political forms that kept rule making dispersed in order to avoid incorporation by lowland states \citep{scott2009}, and his account of the earliest states describes the opposite movement \citep{scott2017}. In every one of these cases collective decisions are made and rules change. What varies is who makes them.

**Why it fails the house style:** This paragraph violates the rule of one identifiable claim per paragraph by combining four distinct points: (1) informal inequalities of age, gender, skill, and kinship raise de facto concentration above the de jure floor $1/n_a$; (2) nineteenth- and twentieth-century ethnographies suffer from marginal-habitat and state-contact bias; (3) prehistoric societies may have alternated seasonally between hierarchy and equality; and (4) upland Southeast Asian polities dispersed rule making to evade lowland states.

### Weakest Paragraph 3 (Line 431)
> The type is internally diverse. Sunni and Shia theories of authority differ, and Shia jurists have held positions ranging from political quietism to the activism of Khomeini's doctrine \citep{arjomand1988}. Explanations of governance outcomes in Muslim-majority states also diverge. Kuran points to particular institutions of Islamic law, Kuru to an alliance between the ulama and the state \citep{kuran2011,kuru2019}, and Rubin to the terms on which rulers bargained for religious legitimacy \citep{rubin2017}. The documented checks include the jurists' independent authority over much of the classical law, contested elections in Iran, and the negotiated character of Ottoman and Saudi rule. Militant challenges to the Saudi state show the limits of religious legitimation when religious actors contest the ruler's claim \citep{hegghammer2010}.

**Why it fails the house style:** This is a catch-all paragraph assembling four unrelated topics: sectarian differences in juristic theory, competing political-economy explanations of development in Muslim-majority countries, a summary list of checks across four regimes, and jihadist insurgency in Saudi Arabia. None of the four claims is developed sufficiently to establish its connection to $H^{\pP}$ or $Q^{\pP}_t$.

### Model Rewrite of Weakest Paragraph 2 (Line 329)
To adhere to the house style—one identifiable claim per paragraph, findings stated directly before qualification, no filler—line 329 should be split so that the measurement claim stands alone and the historical variation in concentration forms its own focused paragraph:

> Formal consensus sets only a lower bound ($H^{\pP}=1/n_a$) on the actual concentration of rule making in stateless societies. Even where every adult holds a formal veto, differences in age, gender, hunting skill, and kinship ties give some speakers greater weight in camp discussions, raising de facto concentration above the equal-share baseline. Nor is low concentration fixed across time or ecological setting: foraging and non-state peoples have shifted deliberately between dispersed and hierarchical rule making across seasons \citep{graeber2021} and across political frontiers, dispersing authority to evade lowland states or concentrating it during state formation \citep{scott2009,scott2017}. Although twentieth-century ethnographies overrepresent groups pushed into marginal habitats by neighboring states \citep{kelly1995}, the historical and ethnographic record supports the same structural conclusion: collective rule making is invariant, whereas its concentration is contingent.

## 3. Structured Feedback by Severity

### Critical Issues
None. The paper contains no fatal mathematical falsehoods or fabricated evidence that would invalidate its core theoretical framework.

### Major Issues
1. **Mismatch between "nontrivial regularity" in Definition 2.2(b) and a single rule change in Assumption 3.2, Lemma 3.5, and Theorem 3.7 (lines 104, 245, 250, 266, 282):** Definition 2.2 requires that rule replacement occur "with nontrivial regularity within $T$." Assumption 3.2 assumes only one pair $s < s'$ with $\mathcal{V}(e_s) \cap \mathcal{V}(e_{s'}) = \emptyset$, and Lemma 3.5 proves only that $R_{t+1} \neq R_t$ for at least one $t \in T$ (which also tacitly requires that $t \in T \implies t+1 \in T$, i.e., $s' < \max T$). Either modify Assumption 3.2 to posit recurring environmental incompatibility over $T$ or revise Definition 2.2(b) so that implementation over $T$ requires at least one rule change when viability sets become disjoint.
2. **Omission of properness in simple games (lines 144, 177):** Definition 2.3 allows improper simple games where $S \in \Win$ and $N \setminus S \in \Win$ simultaneously. When preferences diverge (Assumption 3.1), an improper game allows disjoint coalitions to enact mutually exclusive decisions at once, violating Definition 2.1. Add the properness condition ($S \in \Win \implies N \setminus S \notin \Win$) to Definition 2.3 and note that all games used in Proposition 2.4(c)–(d) and Corollary 2.5 (for $k \ge 1$ or majority rule with $q > n/2$) are proper.
3. **Imprecise condition on preferences in Theorem 3.7 (lines 282, 286):** Theorem 3.7 states "whenever the replacement rule system is drawn from a set of at least two viable systems over which members' preferences diverge, the replacement is itself a selection among divergent preferences." Mere divergence of preference orderings ($\succ_i \neq \succ_j$) does not imply Lemma 3.4; Lemma 3.4 requires that no viable rule system in $\mathcal{V}(e_{t+1})$ be unanimously preferred to all others (the condition of Assumption 3.1). State this explicitly.
4. **Unoperationalized cross-pillar integration in Section 2.1 and circularity of Proposition 6.5 (lines 91, 551–565):** Line 91 introduces $C_{mn,t} \in [0,1]$ purely verbally, and line 551 defers its formal definition to Part VII. Proposition 6.5 then assumes the bound $C_{\pP n} \ge \min\{s^{\pP}(S), \kappa_n(S)\}$ as hypothesis (i) to prove $C_{\pP n} \ge \min\{s^{\pP}(S), \lambda\alpha_{S,n}\}$. Define $C_{mn,t}$ explicitly in Section 2.1 in terms of the shared objects of the model, and derive condition (i) of Proposition 6.5 rather than assuming it.
5. **Lack of self-containment regarding companion papers and parent hypotheses (lines 69, 71, 75, 93, 184, 210, 217, 221, 225, 227, 236, 273, 305, 352, 358, 397, 519, 547, 551, 614–620):** The manuscript cites specific definitions, propositions, and sections in Parts I, III, IV, V, and VII, none of which appear in the bibliography, and Section 7.2 refers to "Parent: H1," "H2," "H3," and "H6" without stating what those hypotheses are. Add full bibliographic entries for all cited companion papers, state the relevant definitions/hypotheses briefly in the text so the paper can be read independently, and spell out parent hypotheses H1, H2, H3, and H6 in Section 7.2.
6. **Absence of a worked formal application (lines 79, 138–208, 387):** While a full cross-national coding may await a separate empirical study, introducing a formal apparatus ($H^{\pP}$, $Q^{\pP}_t$, $\alpha_{S,n}$) without calculating even a single de jure example leaves a wide gap between Sections 2/6 and Section 5. Include at least one worked de jure calculation (e.g., comparing $H^{\pP}$ for a unicameral parliamentary majority, the U.S. bicameral-presidential veto system, and the Iranian Majles–Guardian Council–Leader procedure) to anchor the qualitative comparisons in Section 5.
7. **Section ordering creates forward references to unproved propositions (lines 382–494 vs. 495–578):** Section 5 ("Rule making across six regime types") relies on Proposition 6.4 (veto players, cited on line 445) and Proposition 6.5 (appointment bound and loyalty parameter $\lambda$, cited on line 405) before Section 6 introduces or proves them. Place Section 6 before Section 5 so the theoretical mechanisms precede the comparative case studies.
8. **House style violations in multi-claim and signposting paragraphs (lines 77, 329, 431, 441, 641–649, 651):** Rewrite or eliminate the roadmap paragraph in the Introduction (line 77); split the multi-topic paragraphs at lines 329, 431, 441, and 651 so each paragraph advances a single claim; and restructure Section 9 (lines 641–649) so that each paragraph opens with the governing analytical principle rather than the objection ("X may seem Y... The reply is that...").
