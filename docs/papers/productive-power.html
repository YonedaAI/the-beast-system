1 Introduction

This paper asks who produces the wealth that sustains everything. The question is older than political economy, but it is usually asked in one of two narrow forms. Economists ask how output is divided among wages, profits, rents and taxes. Political scientists ask when those who pay taxes obtain representation. Both forms take for granted something this paper makes explicit: that every governance system, whatever its ideology, runs on a flow of goods and services that its rulers do not themselves produce in sufficient quantity, and that the people who do produce it therefore hold a form of power that no ruler can abolish, only channel, divide or suppress.

The paper is Part VI of a seven-part series on the six functions that every governance system must perform. The series follows Largo (2026), who proposed a multiplex network model of five institutional functions under the name “Hierarchy of the Beast System.” The series splits Largo’s rule function into rule-making and adjudication, giving six pillars: financial power \(\mathsf{F}\) (who controls the resources), political power \(\mathsf{P}\) (who makes the laws), judicial power \(\mathsf{J}\) (who interprets the laws), ideological power \(\mathsf{I}\) (who shapes the beliefs), coercive power \(\mathsf{C}\) (who enforces the rules) and productive power \(\mathsf{W}\) (who produces the wealth that sustains everything). The thesis of the series is that the ideology changes and the institutional functions remain. “Beast System” names that universal six-function structure. It is never a label for a religion, a people, a profession, a regime or a person.

Two propositions organize the series and must be kept apart. Proposition A, proved in general in Part VII (Long 2026c), states that every functioning governance system contains all six functions together with dependency relations among them. Proposition B describes capture: the functions become concentrated or coordinated so that independent accountability fails. Proposition B is contingent and measurable, and Part VII proves that A does not imply B. Each pillar paper derives its own pillar from one axiom, shows the pillar’s minimal form in stateless and consensus societies, compares how the function is distributed across six regime types, and states when coordination in the pillar becomes capture.

For production the derivation is short and the consequences are not. The maintenance axiom says that the group and its institutions consume resources that someone produces. From it we prove that a persistent collective must implement production at a positive average rate (Lemma 12), that it must reproduce its producers through care labor or import producers raised elsewhere (Lemma 14), that systems apparently freed from their producers by rents, tribute or machines have relocated production rather than escaped it (Lemma 13), and that dependence on production gives producers a latent veto over the system’s persistence whenever outside revenue falls short of the maintenance requirement (Proposition 15). The dependence is mutual. Producers depend on access to land, tools, employers, markets and protection; rulers depend on the flow those producers generate. Largo insists that the working population is active rather than passive and that taxation is not automatically extraction (Largo 2026). The latent veto is the formal content of that insistence.

Whether the latent veto becomes effective bargaining power depends on a small number of quantities that differ sharply across regimes. A bargaining model in Section 6 identifies them: the producers’ outside option, their organized bargaining weight, the concentration of control over productive participation, the share of the ruling coalition’s revenue that comes from outside production, and whether access to exchange is conditioned on allegiance. The model yields five main results with proofs. Mean outside options fall linearly with the Herfindahl concentration of employers (Lemma 18). Restricting producers’ mobility pays exactly when the value of free exit exceeds the cost of enforcement, which unifies Domar’s land-labor hypothesis with Brenner’s class-organization critique (Proposition 22). Organized producers obtain a share above their exit floor and a premium from the threat of withdrawal (Proposition 20). Non-tax revenue removes that premium only up to the point where it covers the maintenance requirement (Proposition 23). A participation gate drives unorganized producers toward their autarky value (Proposition 24).

The model also locates capture. Every instrument that depresses producers’ terms (mobility restriction, concentration of productive means, suppression of organization, control of exchange, command over rents) is exercised in a pillar other than \(\mathsf{W}\). The capture of production is therefore never a fact about production alone. It requires coupling between \(\mathsf{W}\) and at least one of \(\mathsf{C}\), \(\mathsf{F}\), \(\mathsf{P}\) or \(\mathsf{I}\) (Corollary 27), which is why Largo’s strong classification demands high cross-pillar integration as well as concentration (Largo 2026).

The comparative analysis in Section 5 applies the same questions to market-capitalist societies, communist party-states, social democracies, Islamic states, historical Catholic monarchies and secular democracies. Each has the productive function. The regimes differ in who sets the terms of participation, how concentrated that control is, which other pillars it is coupled to, and which channels of exit and voice remain open. Documented episodes of capture appear in several regime types, and so do documented checks. Neither list tracks ideology.

The paper closes with a typological reading of Revelation 13 and 18. The image of a mark without which “no man might buy or sell” (Rev 13:17) is a precise picture of the limiting case of the gate proposition: access to exchange conditioned on allegiance, with exit closed. The reading identifies no present-day government, technology or person with the image, and it is presented as an interpretive claim of the project rather than as exegesis.

The contributions are four. The paper derives the productive pillar from the maintenance axiom and states the falsifier of that derivation. It gives a bargaining model whose comparative statics connect labor economics, fiscal sociology, rentier-state theory and the history of unfree labor in one notation. It compares the six regime types on equal terms. And it states nine hypotheses, each with the observation that would refute it.

2 The productive layer

2.1 Shared notation

The objects in this subsection are defined by Largo (2026) and used throughout the series without redefinition. The polity at time \(t\) is a directed multiplex network \(G_t=(V_t,E_t)\) whose vertices are organizations or coherent actor groups. Each pillar \(m\) in the index set \(\mathcal{M}=\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{J},\mathsf{I},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{W}\}\), with \(M=|\mathcal{M}|=6\), contributes a layer with adjacency \(A^m_t\) and edge weights \(a^m_{ij,t}\in[0,1]\) that record the documented capacity of actor \(i\) to constrain a feasible action of actor \(j\) through mechanism \(m\). The role map assigns each vertex a set of pillars, many to many. In pillar \(m\), actor \(i\) holds a control share \(s^m_{i,t}\) with \(\sum_i s^m_{i,t}=1\); concentration is the Herfindahl index \(H_{m,t}=\sum_i (s^m_{i,t})^2\) and contestability is \(Q_{m,t}\in[0,1]\). The illustrative unaccountable-concentration index is \(U_t=\sum_m \omega_m H_{m,t}(1-Q_{m,t})\) with preregistered weights \(\omega_m\ge 0\) summing to one. Cross-pillar integration \(C_{mn,t}\in[0,1]\) measures the extent to which one coalition commands appointments, budgets, sanctions or agenda in both \(m\) and \(n\), and the integration statistic is \(\mathcal{I}_t=\frac{2}{M(M-1)}\sum_{m<n}C_{mn,t}\) over fifteen pairs. A capture link \((i\to j,m)\) is an influence that gives privileged access or discretion, weakens otherwise applicable accountability or contestability, and is tied by material evidence to an institutional output. The state vector \(x_t=(F_t,P_t,J_t,I^{\mathrm{cap}}_t,C_t,W_t)\) collects aggregate pillar capacities, with dynamics \(x_{t+1}=B_tx_t+D_tz_t+\epsilon_t\).

Two conventions avoid clashes. Pillar labels are sans-serif, so \(\mathsf{I}\) is the ideological pillar and \(\mathcal{I}_t\) is the integration statistic. No symbol for the wage is introduced; \(W_t\) is the productive entry of the state vector and \(\mathsf{W}\) is the pillar label.

2.2 Pillar-specific definitions

Definition 1 (Maintenance function). The maintenance function of a collective is the set of activities that produce, pay for and reproduce the material means on which the collective and its institutions run. It includes paid and unpaid labor, cultivation and herding, craft and manufacture, extraction, trade that moves goods to where they are consumed, enterprise, the payment of taxes, tithes, tribute and dues, consumption that sustains producers and their enterprises, and the household work of care and child-rearing that reproduces the producers themselves. The productive pillar \(\mathsf{W}\) is the layer of \(G_t\) in which these activities and the claims on them are organized.

The definition is deliberately wide. Largo’s fifth function already includes households, workers, taxpayers, small businesses, consumers and caregivers (Largo 2026). The household is included because a collective that persists over generations must reproduce its members, and historians of social reproduction have documented how much of that work is unpaid and gendered (Laslett and Brenner 1989). Large holders of capital appear in \(\mathsf{W}\) in their capacity as organizers of production and in \(\mathsf{F}\) in their capacity as allocators of resources; the role map is many to many and does not force a choice.

Definition 2 (Maintenance requirement and persistence). Let \(K_t\) be the institutional maintenance requirement: the resources consumed in period \(t\) by the institutions that realize the other five functions (\(\mathsf{F}\), \(\mathsf{P}\), \(\mathsf{J}\), \(\mathsf{I}\) and \(\mathsf{C}\)), including the subsistence of their full-time personnel; the cost of production itself is borne by the producers. Let \(Z_t\) be the subsistence of all other members, and \(K^{\mathrm{tot}}_t=Z_t+K_t\) the collective’s total consumption. Let \(X_t\ge 0\) be the stock of usable resources held by the collective at the start of period \(t\) and \(Y_t\ge 0\) the inflow of usable resources during \(t\); the stock evolves as \(X_{t+1}=X_t+Y_t-K^{\mathrm{tot}}_t\). The collective persists on a horizon \(\{0,\dots,T\}\) if \(X_t\ge 0\) for every \(t\) in the horizon, and persists indefinitely if this holds for every \(T\). Within the stock, the institutions hold a part \(X^S_t\ge 0\) (a treasury, granary or endowment) that receives the transfers \(T_t\) from producers and any non-producer revenue \(R_t\) and evolves as \(X^S_{t+1}=X^S_t+T_t+R_t-K_t\); the institutions persist while \(X^S_t\ge 0\).

Definition 3 (Producers, extraction and non-producer revenue). A producer is an agent whose activity in period \(t\) contributes to \(Y_t\), together with any agent who owns, operates or maintains equipment that does. In the stationary model of Section 6 there are \(n\) producers, each producing \(y>0\) per period. A governing coalition \(S\) receives a fraction \(\chi\) of output (taxes, rents, dues, profits or procurement deliveries, net of services returned) and the producer retains \((1-\chi)y\). The coalition also receives non-producer revenue \(R_t\ge 0\) that does not come from its own producers: rents from resources sold abroad, tribute, foreign aid or remittances. In the stationary model the producers’ transfer is \(T_t=\chi ny\), and the coalition’s producer dependence is \(\xi_t=T_t/(T_t+R_t)\).

The extraction share \(\chi\) is net of services returned. A tax that finances schooling or roads which producers value is not extraction in the sense used here, and the model treats such services as part of what producers receive. This follows Largo’s warning that taxation is not automatically extraction and that incidence must be established before a flow is called extractive (Largo 2026).

The series uses the word slavery only in Largo’s disciplined, explicitly metaphorical sense, and the comparisons below depend on keeping forms of labor apart. Table 1 defines the forms that recur in the comparative analysis by four criteria: whether the producer may lawfully leave, whether the obligation rests on contract or on sanction, whether the producer’s person is owned, and whether the producer keeps the remainder of output after the obligation is met.

Definition 4 (Bondage spectrum). A form of productive obligation is classified by the four criteria of Table 1. Wage labor and taxation leave lawful mobility and personal ownership with the producer. Serfdom ties the producer to land or lord and compels dues or labor. Compelled state labor (corvée, labor drafts, penal labor) is compelled by sanction for a term. Chattel slavery makes the person property. The word “bondage” is reserved for forms that legally restrict mobility and compel labor; wage labor and taxation are not called slavery in this paper.

Hirschman’s distinction between exit and voice is the standard vocabulary for the options of members facing a declining organization (Hirschman 1970). Largo adopts it for the productive function (Largo 2026), and this Part owns its use in the series.

Definition 5 (Exit, voice and loyalty in the productive layer). A producer’s outside option \(o\) is the per-period value available if the producer stops participating on the current terms: another employer, free land, migration, a subsistence plot, or a cooperative. Exit is the exercise of that option. Voice is collective or individual action to change the terms without leaving (petitions, lawsuits, strikes, tax refusal, boycotts, votes); its effectiveness in the model is the organized bargaining weight \(\eta\in[0,1]\), with \(\eta=0\) for atomized producers. Loyalty is attachment that raises the threshold at which a producer exits, which can make voice possible by keeping members inside long enough to use it (Hirschman 1970).

Definition 6 (Productive contestability). The contestability of the productive pillar, \(Q^{\mathsf{W}}_t:=Q_{\mathsf{W},t}\), is a preregistered aggregate of five components in \([0,1]\): bargaining coverage \(q^{\mathrm{bar}}\) (the share of producers whose terms are set or reviewed collectively), mobility \(q^{\mathrm{mob}}\) (lawful and practical ability to change employer, residence or occupation, including portability of benefits), legal access \(q^{\mathrm{leg}}\) (access to independent adjudication of disputes over terms), income protection \(q^{\mathrm{inc}}\) (replacement of income during unemployment, illness and old age), and voice \(q^{\mathrm{voi}}\) (lawful ability to organize, strike, petition and vote over the terms of taxation).

The five components follow Largo’s list for the productive function: bargaining, legal protection, mobility, representation and income protection (Largo 2026). They are separate because they fail separately. A worker can have the vote and no union, a union and no portable pension, or a labor court and no means to reach it. Legal access belongs to the judicial pillar as well. Part III (Long 2026e) owns the analysis of legal mobilization (its Proposition 4.3) and the components of judicial contestability (its Definition 2.4): disclosure of a defective decision, access to a challenge, independence of the reviewing forum, and compliance with its ruling.

The concentration \(H_{\mathsf{W},t}\) is computed on shares of decision-relevant control over the terms of productive participation. In a labor market the natural proxy is the employment or vacancy share of each employer, which is how Azar et al. (2022) measure labor-market concentration. Where the state is the dominant employer or the sole purchaser of farm output, its share approaches one. Where every household controls its own labor and land is open, shares are close to \(1/n_h\) for \(n_h\) households.

2.3 The production game

The mechanisms of Section 6 and the comparisons of Section 5 use one stationary model, defined here and analyzed in Section 6.

Definition 7 (Production game). In each period a coalition \(S\) governs \(n\) identical producers. A participating producer produces \(y>0\). The coalition and the producers agree on terms \(u\), the per-producer value the producer retains (consumption plus the money value of services and concessions received); the extraction share is \(\chi=1-u/y\), which is negative when producers receive net transfers. The coalition’s revenue is \(n(y-u)+R\), where \(R\ge 0\) is non-producer revenue, and it must meet the institutional maintenance requirement \(K>0\) of Definition 2. Producers’ own subsistence is covered by \(u\), so \(K\) contains only the cost of the institutions. A producer who does not participate obtains the outside option \(o\), with \(y_0\le o\le y\), where \(y_0\) is the autarky value of a producer cut off from exchange.

The coalition objective of Largo (2026), \(\Pi=B(p)+\rho(c)-\kappa(g)-\phi(\cdot)\), is the general form of which the coalition’s payoff here is a special case. The net revenue \(n(y-u)\) is a component of the material benefit \(B\), and the mobility restriction \(g\) defined next is an instance of the source’s coercive measures with cost \(\kappa(g)\).

Definition 8 (Instruments on the outside option). A mobility restriction \(g\in[0,1]\) is the probability that a producer who tries to leave is prevented: tying peasants to land, internal passports, residence permits tied to an employer. A participation gate \(\gamma\in[0,1]\) is the probability that a producer who withholds allegiance from the coalition retains access to exchange (buying inputs, selling output, credit, being hired); otherwise the producer falls to the autarky value \(y_0\). Employment shares \(s_j=s^{\mathsf{W}}_j\) of employers \(j=1,\dots,J\) define the concentration \(H_{\mathsf{W}}=\sum_j s_j^2\).

Definition 9 (Composite outside option). Let \(o_1>y_0\) be the value of an offer from a competing employer. The outside option of a producer at employer \(j\) is \[o_j=y_0+\gamma\,(1-g)\,(1-s_j)\,(o_1-y_0),\] and the employment-weighted mean outside option is \(\bar o=\sum_j s_jo_j=y_0+\gamma(1-g)(1-H_{\mathsf{W}})(o_1-y_0)\).

Each factor of the composite form is a probability that the producer’s exit succeeds: the producer is not blocked from leaving (\(1-g\)), keeps access to exchange (\(\gamma\)), and finds a different employer (\(1-s_j\), justified in Lemma 18). When an exit fails the producer receives the fallback \(y_0\). Unrestricted producers facing many small employers obtain nearly \(o_1\); a closed gate, a complete restriction or a single employer each holds them at \(y_0\).

3 Necessity of the pillar

3.1 The maintenance axiom

Part VII owns the axiom set from which the series derives Proposition A. The sixth axiom concerns this pillar.

Assumption 10 (Ax6, maintenance). There are constants \(k\ge k_S>0\) with \(K^{\mathrm{tot}}_t\ge k\) and \(K_t\ge k_S\) for every \(t\): the group and its institutions consume resources in every period. The initial stocks \(X_0\) and \(X^S_0\) are finite.

The axiom is empirical, not logical. A population that consumed nothing would not need production, and the derivation below would fail for it. No human collective satisfies that description, since members eat and institutions occupy time that could otherwise produce food. The finite initial stock excludes inexhaustible inheritances.

Assumption 11 (No manna). Inflows are produced: \(Y_t>0\) only if some agent, inside or outside the collective, performs productive activity in period \(t\) or owns, operates or maintains equipment that does.

The no-manna assumption converts the accounting identity of Definition 2 into a claim about activity. Wild foods require gathering, oil requires extraction, and a gift requires a giver who produced or acquired what is given. The assumption fails only for resources that arrive with no human act at any point in the chain, and the scarcity axiom of the series (Ax1, which states that resources are finite and that claims on them conflict) makes any such free stock finite.

3.2 Necessity and relocation

Lemma 12 (Necessity of the maintenance function). Under Assumptions 10 and 11, if a collective persists indefinitely then for every \(T\ge 1\) \[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}Y_t\;\ge\;k-\frac{X_0}{T},\] so the long-run average inflow is at least \(k>0\), and productive activity occurs in infinitely many periods.

Proof. Iterating the stock equation gives \(X_T=X_0+\sum_{t<T}(Y_t-K^{\mathrm{tot}}_t)\). Persistence requires \(X_T\ge 0\), hence \(\sum_{t<T}Y_t\ge\sum_{t<T}K^{\mathrm{tot}}_t-X_0\ge Tk-X_0\). Dividing by \(T\) gives the displayed bound, and its right side tends to \(k\) as \(T\to\infty\). If productive activity occurred in only finitely many periods, Assumption 11 would make \(Y_t=0\) from some period on, so \(\sum_{t<T}Y_t\) would be bounded while \(Tk-X_0\) grows without bound, a contradiction. ◻

The lemma establishes the existence of the activity, not of any institution that organizes it. Wage labor, serfdom, the household farm, the kolkhoz and the cooperative are alternative ways of implementing the same function. This is the distinction Hempel (1959) required of functional analysis: a functional requirement does not entail any particular institution, because functional equivalents may satisfy it. Nor does the lemma explain why a given regime has the productive institutions it has. Elster (1983) showed that explanation by beneficial consequences needs a feedback mechanism from the effect to the trait. The lemma is a condition of persistence, and it makes no causal claim about the origin of institutions.

A collective can persist while its own members produce little or nothing. The next lemma shows what such persistence requires.

Lemma 13 (Relocation). Split the inflow as \(Y_t=Y^{\mathrm{in}}_t+Y^{\mathrm{out}}_t\), where \(Y^{\mathrm{in}}_t\) is produced by members and \(Y^{\mathrm{out}}_t\) by non-members and transferred to the collective. Under Assumptions 10 and 11, if a collective persists indefinitely and \(Y^{\mathrm{in}}_t=0\) for all \(t\ge t_0\), then non-members produce for the collective in infinitely many periods, and for every \(T>t_0\) the transferred output satisfies \(\sum_{t_0\le t<T}Y^{\mathrm{out}}_t\ge (T-t_0)k-X_{t_0}\).

Proof. Apply Lemma 12 to the collective from period \(t_0\) with initial stock \(X_{t_0}\), which is finite because each \(X_t\) is finite. From \(t_0\) on, \(Y_t=Y^{\mathrm{out}}_t\), so the bound becomes the displayed inequality, and productive activity by non-members occurs in infinitely many periods. ◻

The transfer in Lemma 13 is an edge of the productive layer between the collective and producers outside its membership. It can take the form of tribute extracted by force, rents paid by foreign buyers for oil extracted by migrant workers, alms and endowments given to a religious community, or foreign aid financed by taxpayers elsewhere. Each moves the producers out of the membership and usually out of the polity’s channels of voice. None removes them.

Automation is the hardest version of the relocation argument. If equipment performs all productive activity, Definition 3 still assigns the producer role to whoever owns, operates or maintains the equipment. Control over the productive layer then coincides with ownership of equipment, a relation that also belongs to \(\mathsf{F}\), and \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\) tracks the concentration of that ownership. The function survives full automation. The bargaining power of human labor, analyzed below, does not survive it automatically: it depends on human activity remaining necessary somewhere in the chain. That dependence is an empirical condition of present technology, not a consequence of the axiom, and the paper’s claims about labor are narrowed accordingly.

The producers must themselves be produced. A collective that persists beyond the working lives of its present members depends on the care and child-rearing that Laslett and Brenner (1989) call social reproduction, most of it unpaid. The next lemma states the dependence.

Lemma 14 (Reproduction). Let the number of producers evolve as \(n_{t+1}=(1-d)n_t+bh_t+m_t\), where \(d\in(0,1]\) is the rate at which producers leave work through death, age or illness, \(h_t\ge 0\) is care labor performed in period \(t\), \(b>0\) is the number of future producers sustained per unit of care, and \(m_t\ge 0\) is the net arrival of producers raised elsewhere. If \(h_t=m_t=0\) for all \(t\ge t_0\), then \(n_{t_0+j}=(1-d)^jn_{t_0}\), and members’ output \(n_ty\) falls below any fixed \(k>0\) after finitely many periods. A collective that persists indefinitely on members’ production therefore requires care labor, or the arrival of producers raised elsewhere, in infinitely many periods.

Proof. With \(h_t=m_t=0\) the recursion is \(n_{t+1}=(1-d)n_t\), whose solution is \((1-d)^jn_{t_0}\); this tends to zero because \(0\le 1-d<1\), so \(n_ty<k\) for all large \(t\). If care and arrivals were zero from some period on, members’ production would fall below \(k\) from some period on, and Lemma 12 would require inflows produced by non-members, contradicting persistence on members’ production. ◻

The lemma puts unpaid household work inside the maintenance function on the same footing as paid work. It also shows a second form of relocation. A labor regime that imports adult migrants (\(m_t>0\)) draws on care performed in the migrants’ places of origin, so the cost of reproducing its producers is borne outside the polity, as the cost of producing its rents is in Lemma 13.

3.3 The latent veto

Dependence on production runs in both directions. Producers depend on land, tools, employers, buyers and protection. The coalition that governs depends on the flow that producers generate. The next proposition states the second dependence as a bound.

Proposition 15 (Latent veto). Suppose Assumption 10 holds, the institutions’ non-producer revenue satisfies \(R_t\le\bar r\) for all \(t\) with \(\bar r<k_S\), and from period \(t_0\) on the producers withdraw completely, so that their transfers are \(T_t=0\). Then the institutions fail to persist no later than period \(t_0+\lfloor X^S_{t_0}/(k_S-\bar r)\rfloor+1\).

Proof. For \(t\ge t_0\) the institutional stock of Definition 2 satisfies \(X^S_{t+1}=X^S_t+R_t-K_t\le X^S_t-(k_S-\bar r)\). After \(j\) periods of withdrawal, \(X^S_{t_0+j}\le X^S_{t_0}-j(k_S-\bar r)\), which is negative for the smallest integer \(j\) exceeding \(X^S_{t_0}/(k_S-\bar r)\), that is, for \(j=\lfloor X^S_{t_0}/(k_S-\bar r)\rfloor+1\). ◻

The proposition is elementary, and its content lies in what it rules out. No governance system whose non-producer revenue stays below its institutional maintenance requirement can survive the complete and permanent withdrawal of its producers. The producers therefore hold, collectively, a veto over persistence. The veto is latent because exercising it requires coordination that atomized producers lack (Olson 1965), because the coalition may meet withdrawal with coercion, and because the producers bear their own costs of withdrawal. Section 6 prices these obstacles. When \(R_t\ge K_t\) the bound fails, and the veto passes to whoever produces the rents (Lemma 13).

Largo states the same mutual dependence in prose: the working population is active, dependency is not powerlessness, and concentration elsewhere translates into material dependency only where workers lack bargaining, legal protection, mobility and representation (Largo 2026). Proposition 15 supplies the reason the population is active. It is part of the structure on which everything else rests.

3.4 Minimal form in stateless and consensus societies

Proposition A predicts that each function exists in societies with no state, no chiefs with coercive power and strong egalitarian norms. For production the prediction is easy to confirm and the interesting question is different: why does production in these societies so rarely support a class that extracts from producers? The formal part of the answer is an exit argument.

Proposition 16 (Minimal form). Consider a band in which every household controls its own labor, any household can leave the camp and obtain the same output \(y\) elsewhere on open land, and leaving costs nothing. Then (a) the maintenance function is implemented by the households themselves, and (b) no member or coalition can sustain a positive extraction share \(\chi>0\) from any household that is free to leave.

Proof. Part (a) is Lemma 12 applied to the band, with producers identified as household members. For (b), a household asked to surrender \(\chi y>0\) obtains \((1-\chi)y\) by staying and \(y\) by leaving, so leaving is strictly better. A demand for \(\chi>0\) is therefore not met by any household that is free to leave. ◻

The ethnography supplies the premises and shows where they fail. Table 2 summarizes the cases the series uses.

Among the Ju/’hoansi, production by both sexes is the basis of daily life, and the terms on which its results circulate are governed by sharing rules, gift partnerships and talk (Lee 1979; Lee 2013; Marshall 1976). Wiessner (2005) documented how the Ju/’hoansi enforce such norms, largely through criticism and talk rather than physical punishment. In the vocabulary of this paper, open access and costless fission keep \(o\) close to \(y\), and leveling sanctions keep it there. Woodburn (1982) generalized the point: in immediate-return societies equality is an achievement of institutions that prevent accumulation, not a default. Boehm (1999) called the same pattern a reverse dominance hierarchy, in which the group as a coalition dominates would-be dominants.

Clastres’s Amazonian chiefs are the most striking case for this pillar. The chief has no power to command, is expected to be generous, and works harder than others to supply what he gives away (Clastres 1987). In the model’s terms, the leader’s extraction share is negative. Clastres read this as a society actively preventing power from separating from it. That reading supports the distinction between Propositions A and B. The productive function is present, and its capture is prevented.

Ostrom’s commons supply the cleanest institutional image of the maintenance function at small scale. Her design principles require congruence between appropriation rules and provision rules, so that those who draw on an irrigation system or a fishery also contribute the labor that maintains it, under monitoring by the users themselves (Ostrom 1990; Ostrom 2010). A review of later studies found broad, though not uniform, support for the principles (Cox et al. 2010). Provision rules are the productive pillar in miniature.

The ethnographic record has limits that bear on the claim. Contact-era ethnographies describe groups living in marginal environments, often already in contact with states and markets, and foraging societies vary widely (Kelly 1995). Sahlins’s argument that hunter-gatherers met their needs with modest labor (Sahlins 1972) corrects any picture of constant struggle, but it does not remove production; it lowers its hours. The minimal-form proposition needs only that someone produces and that, where exit is open and costless, no one can take a share.

3.5 Counterexample attempts

The falsifier of Lemma 12 is a governance system that persists indefinitely while supported by no production and no replenishment of its stock. Six candidates come close and fail.

A community living from an endowment consumes a finite stock unless the endowment earns a return, and a return is someone’s production. Monasteries and mendicant orders that live by alms relocate production to donors. A raiding or tributary state relocates it to the raided and the tributary, as Lemma 13 predicts; the Spanish colonial labor draft discussed in Section 5 is a documented instance in which the producers were outside the ruling community and inside its coercive reach (Dell 2010). A rentier state relocates production to the workers who extract the resource and the foreign buyers who pay for it; in the Gulf monarchies much of the extracting and building labor is done by migrants without citizenship (Hanieh 2011; Kapiszewski 2001). Full automation, as argued above, relocates the producer role to owners and maintainers of equipment. The original affluent society lowers labor hours without eliminating them (Sahlins 1972).

The strongest attempt is the last of these taken to its limit: a society of self-maintaining machines serving a population that does nothing. The function would then be performed by equipment owned by someone, and the claim survives in its functional form. What would not survive is any claim that human labor holds a latent veto. The paper’s claims about producer power are therefore conditional on human activity remaining necessary in production. That narrowing is the honest result of the counterexample.

4 Literature

Four bodies of work bear on the productive pillar, and they rarely cite one another. Each answers part of the guiding question, and each leaves a gap that the framework of this series is meant to fill.

The first asks who creates value and how it is divided. For Marx (1976), labor applied to means of production owned by others yields a surplus appropriated as profit, and the compulsion that keeps wage workers at work is economic rather than legal. Polanyi’s account of the nineteenth-century market society adds that labor, land and money are fictitious commodities, not produced for sale, and that treating them as commodities provoked a protective countermovement (Polanyi 1944). Both accounts locate power in production, but neither supplies a comparative instrument that applies equally to market and command economies. Recent distributional accounting measures who receives national income without asking how the terms were bargained (Piketty et al. 2018). Work on social reproduction widens the category of producers to include unpaid household labor (Laslett and Brenner 1989).

The second body of work locates producers’ power in their options and their organization. Hirschman’s exit, voice and loyalty frame the options of members of any organization in decline (Hirschman 1970). Freeman and Medoff (1984) applied the frame to trade unions, which have a monopoly face that raises wages and a collective-voice face that lets workers change conditions without quitting. Wright (2000) distinguished associational power, which comes from organization in unions and parties, from structural power, which comes from workers’ position in tight labor markets or strategic sectors. Silver (2003) used the distinction to trace labor unrest across a century of relocating industries.

Power resources theory explains welfare-state variation by the organization of wage earners and their parties (Korpi 1983; Esping-Andersen 1985). The employer side of the same relation is monopsony: when workers cannot move costlessly between employers, firms set wages below the competitive level (Manning 2003), and local labor markets are often concentrated enough for this to matter (Azar et al. 2022). These studies measure producer power well within capitalist democracies and say little about serfs, kolkhoz members or migrant workers under sponsorship.

The third body of work is fiscal sociology, which treats the tax relation as the core of state formation (Campbell 1993; Martin et al. 2009). Levi (1988) modeled rulers as revenue maximizers constrained by their bargaining power relative to constituents, by transaction costs and by their discount rates, and showed that compliance is quasi-voluntary: taxpayers comply when they believe others do and the ruler keeps bargains. Bates and Lien (1985) argued that owners of mobile assets can extract representative concessions. Ross (2004) tested the claim that taxation leads to representation and found that the relevant variable is taxation relative to the services government provides, and Timmons (2005) found that governments trade services for revenue with the groups that pay.

Rentier-state theory supplies the converse: revenue that does not come from domestic taxpayers frees rulers from bargaining with them (Mahdavy 1970; Beblawi and Luciani 1987; Ross 2001). The converse is contested. Haber and Menaldo (2011) find no evidence that resource reliance promotes authoritarianism over time within countries. Part I (Long 2026b) treats rents (its Sections 5.4 and 6.3) on the allocation side; this Part treats them as a change in producers’ bargaining position.

The fourth body of work explains when producers are bound. Domar (1970) proposed that serfdom or slavery arises where land is abundant relative to labor, because free peasants could otherwise leave to farm free land, and that free land, free peasants and non-working landowners cannot coexist. Brenner (1976) answered that the outcomes of the late-medieval demographic crisis differed across Europe with the same land-labor ratios, and that the balance of organized class power decided them. Dell (2010) showed that the Spanish colonial mining draft in Peru left lower household consumption and worse child health in the districts subject to it, centuries after its abolition. Scott (1976), Scott (1985) showed that peasants who cannot exit or speak openly resist through foot-dragging, evasion and gossip, and that they judge extraction against a subsistence ethic.

The gap is a common instrument. Labor economics, fiscal sociology, rentier theory and the history of bondage each study one or two of the quantities that set producers’ terms, in one or two regime types. The model in Section 6 places the outside option, organization, employer concentration, non-producer revenue and conditional access to exchange in one notation that applies to a band, a kolkhoz, a Gulf labor camp and a unionized factory. It does not replace the specialized literatures. It shows where they meet.

5 Comparative analysis

Producers’ terms were worst, in every regime type that produced such cases, where concentrated control over production was coupled to coercion and to control of exchange; ideology did not decide where this happened. The evidence comes from asking the same questions of each regime type: who produces and pays, who sets the terms of participation, how concentrated that control is, which other pillars it is coupled to, and which channels of exit and voice remain open.

Two conventions govern the comparison. Where market-capitalist and secular-democratic institutions coexist in one country, the first type is read through the organization of the economy (private capital, labor markets, the structural power of business) and the second through the political organization of the fiscal relation (citizen taxpayers, universal suffrage, social insurance law). Judgments of concentration are qualitative, because no country has been coded under a preregistered scheme, and no value of \(U_t\) or \(\mathcal{I}_t\) is reported.

5.1 Market-capitalist societies

In market-capitalist societies most producers are wage earners, the self-employed and small businesses, and the terms of production are set mainly by employers in labor markets and by the state through tax and labor law. Consumers sustain enterprises by their purchases and pay a large share of indirect taxes. The holders of the pillar are therefore many, but control over terms is not evenly spread. Azar et al. (2022) found that a large share of local labor markets in the United States are highly concentrated by the standard used in antitrust, and that concentration is associated with lower posted wages. Monopsony theory explains why: when moving between employers is costly, employers set wages below the competitive level (Manning 2003). In the notation of this paper, \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\) varies across local markets and is high in many of them.

The structural power of business is the distinctive coupling of this regime type. Lindblom (1977) argued that because investment decisions are private and governments depend on growth, business holds a privileged position that requires no organization: governments anticipate the reaction of investors. Przeworski (1985) drew the consequence for labor parties, which must sustain profits to sustain employment. This is the exit option of mobile capital, and Bates and Lien (1985) predicted that the owners of mobile assets obtain concessions. The coupling \(C_{\mathsf{F}\mathsf{W}}\) is high wherever those who allocate capital also set the terms of employment. Part I gives the primary treatment of that pair in its Section 5.

Accountability in the pillar has rested on three channels: collective bargaining, labor mobility and the vote. Their strength has varied over time. Farber et al. (2021) assembled survey data across the twentieth century and found that unions compressed the income distribution when they were strong. Western and Rosenfeld (2011) attributed a substantial part of the rise in American wage inequality after the 1970s to the decline of unions, including through the erosion of norms of fair pay in non-union firms. Piketty et al. (2018) documented the same period’s shift of pre-tax income toward the top of the distribution. These are documented changes in \(q^{\mathrm{bar}}\) and \(q^{\mathrm{voi}}\), not evidence of capture by themselves. A decline in bargaining coverage can follow from changes in industry composition and trade as well as from employer action and law.

Documented checks are visible in the same record. Union contracts, minimum-wage laws, antitrust enforcement in labor markets and the expansion of social insurance in the twentieth century all raised producers’ terms through voice. Polanyi (1944) treated the protective legislation of the nineteenth century as the countermovement of a society defending itself against the commodification of labor. Documented capture is harder to establish than decline. The source’s capture-link criterion requires material evidence linking privileged access to an output (Largo 2026). Accounts of how organized business reshaped American labor and tax policy from the 1970s (Hacker and Pierson 2010) supply candidate links, and they remain contested in the way Part II (Long 2026f) describes in its Section 5.2 for the wider literature on unequal political responsiveness.

5.2 Communist party-states

In the Soviet Union after collectivization and in China under the planned economy, the party-state was the dominant employer, the sole legal purchaser of most farm output and the owner of nearly all productive assets. Its share of control over the terms of productive participation approached one, so \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\) was near its maximum. The coupling of \(\mathsf{W}\) to the other pillars was also high, because the same apparatus set prices and procurement quotas (\(\mathsf{F}\)), wrote labor law (\(\mathsf{P}\)), staffed the courts (\(\mathsf{J}\)), ran the official unions and the press (\(\mathsf{I}\)), and policed mobility (\(\mathsf{C}\)).

Collectivization is the most fully documented case in which every instrument of Definitions 8 and 9 was used at once: a single purchaser, restricted mobility, and access to exchange conditioned on membership of the collective farm. Peasants resisted it in 1930 by slaughtering livestock, petitioning and open revolt (Viola 1996). After collectivization, the kolkhoz delivered grain at state-set prices, and procurement in the years 1931 to 1933 continued in regions where famine was spreading (Davies and Wheatcroft 2004). Mobility was restricted, and peasants adapted with strategies of evasion and survival that Fitzpatrick (1994) documented in detail. Forced labor in the Gulag formed a further tier of compelled state labor (Applebaum 2003), and economic historians have argued that it was expensive to run and less productive than free labor (Gregory and Lazarev 2003). In the terms of Table 1, collective farmers were tied to the farm for long periods and Gulag prisoners were compelled state laborers. Neither group was chattel property.

Soviet industrial workers held more power than the formal structure suggests, because labor was scarce. Chronic labor shortage, the soft budget constraint of enterprises (Kornai 1986; Kornai 1992) and managers’ need to meet plans gave workers an informal bargaining position. Workers in the 1930s used it through high turnover, absenteeism and control over the pace of work, despite harsh labor-discipline laws (Filtzer 1986). Even the Stakhanovite campaign of record-setting provoked conflict on the shop floor as well as productivity gains (Siegelbaum 1988).

Chinese workers under the planned economy faced a more complete dependence on the enterprise. Housing, rations and welfare came through the work unit and through personal ties to superiors, which made the unit a site of dependence rather than of bargaining (Walder 1986). The household registration system closed the main exit, tying rural people to their place of birth and restricting their access to urban employment and services (Cheng and Selden 1994).

The reform era changed the instruments without removing them. Migration from the countryside expanded producers’ outside options, and labor protest grew. Lee (2007) compared the protests of laid-off state-sector workers in the northeastern rustbelt with those of migrant workers in the southern export zones, who used the courts and labor law as well as street protest. The official union federation remains tied to the party, which limits voice outside party channels (Friedman and Lee 2010). Documented checks in this regime type are therefore mainly informal: evasion, turnover, petitioning, and the regime’s own fear of unrest. That they existed matters for the comparison. Even under the most integrated arrangement in the record, the latent veto of Proposition 15 operated through everyday resistance.

5.3 Social democracy and market socialism

In the Nordic social democracies, producers are wage earners and the self-employed in market economies, but their terms are set to an unusual degree by collective bargaining at the national and sectoral level and by universal social insurance. Union density and bargaining coverage have been high by international standards for most of the postwar period. Power resources theory explains the arrangement as the result of the organization of wage earners in unions and their alliance with social democratic parties (Korpi 1983; Esping-Andersen 1985). Esping-Andersen (1990) measured its consequence as decommodification, the degree to which people can maintain a livelihood without relying on the labor market. Huber and Stephens (2001) traced the development of the welfare state to partisan government over decades.

The distinctive coupling here is between \(\mathsf{W}\) and \(\mathsf{P}\), through corporatist bargaining in which unions, employers and the state set wages and social policy together. By the source’s own standard this coupling is not capture (Largo 2026). It is a coalition commanding agenda in two pillars, and in this case its effect was to raise producers’ contestability rather than to lower it. Corollary 27 formalizes the asymmetry: capture requires instruments that lower producers’ terms, and corporatist coupling has mainly strengthened voice. Universalism also has a fiscal logic. Korpi and Palme (1998) found that universal programs redistribute more than targeted ones, because they build coalitions of support that enlarge the budget. Rothstein (1996) showed how the Swedish administrative model made universal programs implementable.

The limits of the model are documented as well. Przeworski (1985) argued that social democratic parties remain structurally dependent on private investment, and Pontusson (1992) traced the failure of Swedish wage-earner funds, the most ambitious attempt to transfer ownership to labor. Rueda (2005) argued that social democratic parties have incentives to protect labor-market insiders rather than outsiders in temporary or precarious employment. That cleavage is a candidate failure mode inside the pillar: high \(Q^{\mathsf{W}}\) for insiders can coexist with low \(Q^{\mathsf{W}}\) for outsiders.

Market socialism and workers’ self-management are the variants of this regime type in which producers formally own the enterprise. Kornai (1992) argued that reform socialism in Hungary and Yugoslavia retained soft budget constraints and party control of appointments, which limited the autonomy of self-managed firms. Worker cooperatives in market economies are the small-scale analogue. Dow (2003) surveyed the theory and evidence on labor-managed firms, and Craig and Pencavel (1993) studied the objectives of worker cooperatives in the plywood industry of the Pacific Northwest. In the model, a cooperative raises producers’ outside option, since it is an employer that the producers themselves control, and it removes extraction within the firm while leaving taxation to the state.

5.4 Islamic states

Islamic states share a source of political legitimacy, the claim to govern according to religious law, and differ in nearly every other respect. The comparison here covers the Ottoman Empire, modern Iran and Saudi Arabia, and the productive arrangements in each are not derived from Islamic law alone. Some instruments are legal categories of the tradition, such as alms taxes and land taxes. Others, such as tax farming or migrant sponsorship, are fiscal and labor-market institutions that belong to particular states and periods.

In the Ottoman Empire, organized crafts gave producers a channel of voice. The taxpaying subjects were mainly peasants, artisans and merchants, and urban crafts were organized in guilds that regulated entry, prices and quality while also serving the state as administrative and fiscal intermediaries (Baer 1970). The guild was thus a site of coupling between \(\mathsf{W}\) and \(\mathsf{P}\): guilds petitioned authorities and brought disputes to the courts. Studies of Ottoman and early Republican workers document strikes and labor organization in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (Quataert and Zürcher 1995).

Control over Ottoman producers’ terms was dispersed among intermediaries. Karaman and Pamuk (2010) compared Ottoman state finances with those of European states and found that Ottoman revenue per capita remained low and collection decentralized, with heavy reliance on tax farming. Barkey (2008) described Ottoman rule as brokerage with intermediaries rather than direct command. In the vocabulary of this paper, control over the terms of production was dispersed among intermediaries, so \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\) was moderate. Ibn Khaldun had already observed that dynasties begin with low tax rates that yield large revenues and end with high rates that yield little, as producers withdraw effort (Ibn Khaldun 1958). The observation states the latent veto in fourteenth-century terms.

Islamic legal categories of taxation and charity, including the alms tax and endowments, shaped the fiscal relation and the provision of welfare. Singer (2008) surveyed charity in Islamic societies, including endowed public kitchens in the Ottoman Empire. The economic consequences of Islamic commercial law are debated. Kuran (2011) argued that rules of partnership, inheritance and endowment held back the formation of durable private enterprises in the Middle East; Rubin (2017) attributed divergence instead to the way rulers relied on religious authorities for legitimacy, and Pamuk (2000) offered a monetary and fiscal history that does not reduce Ottoman outcomes to law.

Modern Iran shows producers’ power at both extremes. Oil workers’ strikes were central to the revolution of 1978 to 1979, and workers’ councils briefly took over the management of factories before the new regime absorbed or suppressed them (Bayat 1987). In the following decades the urban poor pursued what Bayat (1997) called the quiet encroachment of the ordinary: unorganized, cumulative claims on land, services and public space. Mahdavy (1970) coined the concept of the rentier state using the Iranian case: oil revenue reduces the state’s need to tax, and with it the bargaining weight of taxpayers.

Saudi Arabia is the clearest rentier case. Oil rent finances the state, citizens receive public employment and transfers, and much of the private-sector labor force consists of migrants without citizenship (Chaudhry 1997; Hertog 2010). The migrant labor system ties a worker’s legal residence to a sponsoring employer, which restricts the worker’s ability to change employer or leave (Hanieh 2011; Kapiszewski 2001). On Table 1 this is a restriction on mobility within wage labor, not chattel slavery; in the model it is a mobility restriction \(g>0\) applied to a group with high outside options, exactly the case in which Proposition 22 predicts that restriction pays. The early oil industry also produced organized protest: Vitalis (2007) documented the segregated labor hierarchy of the oil camps and the strikes it provoked. For citizens, the rentier bargain lowers producer dependence \(\xi\), and Proposition 23 predicts that their withdrawal premium is small.

5.5 Catholic monarchies

In Habsburg Spain, Bourbon France and the Papal States, producers were mainly peasants owing seigneurial dues, the tithe and royal taxes, together with urban artisans and merchants. The privileged estates, clergy and nobility, were exempt from important direct taxes. The Church was simultaneously a landholder, a tithe receiver and a source of the monarch’s legitimacy, so its coupling to \(\mathsf{W}\) was material as well as ideological. The tithe had a long history as an ecclesiastical claim on agricultural output (Constable 1964), and Ekelund et al. (1996) analyzed the medieval Church as an economic organization that collected revenue for services.

In Bourbon France, privilege sorted the fiscal burden. The heaviest direct tax fell on commoners, so the distribution of the burden was itself an output of political privilege; when the eighteenth-century monarchy tried to tax the privileged through new direct taxes, the resulting conflicts politicized taxation (Kwass 2000). In Languedoc the revenue drawn from producers was shared between the crown and provincial elites, whose collaboration made absolutism work (Beik 1985).

The same fiscal interest produced a documented check. The monarchy strengthened village communities in Burgundy in order to tax them, and those communities then used royal courts to contest seigneurial dues (Root 1987). The crown’s need for revenue thus raised the peasants’ organized weight \(\eta\) against their lords.

In Castile, communal lands and municipal institutions mattered to peasant livelihoods (Vassberg 1984), and ordinary people litigated in large numbers in the royal courts (Kagan 1981). The Cortes bargained with the crown over new taxes, and the taxes paid by Castilian producers sustained the crown’s borrowing (Drelichman and Voth 2014). In the Americas the same monarchy organized compelled state labor. The Potosı́ mining draft required indigenous communities in designated districts to send a share of their men to the mines, and Dell (2010) found persistent lower consumption and child growth in those districts today. In the Papal States, the pope was both temporal sovereign and head of the Church, which made the coupling of \(\mathsf{I}\) with fiscal administration direct (Prodi 1987).

Brenner (1976) supplies the comparative lesson for this regime type. Seigneurial power, village organization and the fiscal needs of the crown interacted differently in France, England and eastern Europe, with different outcomes for peasant tenure. Productivity followed politics as much as technique: French agricultural output grew in some periods and regions and stagnated in others, and war, taxation and access to markets mattered more than peasant conservatism (Hoffman 1996). Catholic legitimation was common to several of these cases and did not determine the outcome. Village communities, royal courts, the Cortes and periodic revolt were the producers’ channels of voice; flight to towns and colonies was their exit.

5.6 Secular democracies

In secular democracies, the citizen is a taxpayer and a voter at once, and the fiscal relation is the most general channel through which producers affect the state. The fiscal contract argument holds that states which depend on taxes must bargain with taxpayers, and that the bargain produces representation and services (Bates and Lien 1985; Moore 2004; Timmons 2005). Evidence for the link is conditional. Ross (2004) found that it is the cost of government relative to the services provided, rather than the level of taxation alone, that predicts democratization. Levi (1988) found that compliance in established democracies is quasi-voluntary: it depends on taxpayers’ belief that others pay and that the government keeps its side of the bargain.

The modern tax state depends on fiscal capacity, the ability to tax incomes broadly, which Besley and Persson (2011), Besley and Persson (2013) treat as an investment that states make when they expect common-interest spending such as defense. Scheve and Stasavage (2016) found that steeply progressive taxation in the twentieth century followed wars of mass mobilization, in which arguments that the wealthy should compensate for the sacrifices of conscripts proved persuasive, rather than following the extension of the franchise alone. This is the latent veto at work in a democratic register: those who supplied the soldiers and the war production obtained fiscal concessions.

The working class appears in the comparative-historical literature as a force for democratization. Rueschemeyer et al. (1992) argued that organized workers were the most consistent supporters of full democracy, against landlords and often against the bourgeoisie. Voice in secular democracies therefore includes the franchise, unions and the courts, and Chenoweth and Stephan (2011) found that, in their data on twentieth-century campaigns, nonviolent campaigns relying on mass non-cooperation, including strikes and boycotts, succeeded more often than violent ones. Welfare regimes differ within this regime type. Esping-Andersen (1990) classified liberal, conservative and social democratic regimes, and the liberal regime offers the weakest income protection.

Capture episodes in secular democracies take the form of privileged access to tax and labor policy. Their documentation is subject to the source’s three-part test, and the main empirical claims about unequal responsiveness are contested (Part II, Section 5.2). Documented checks include tax administration under judicial review, labor law enforced in independent courts, and the electoral reversal of fiscal policies. Part III treats the courts, including the contest between repeat players and occasional litigants (its Proposition 4.3).

5.7 Social reproduction across regimes

Every regime type relied on unpaid care to reproduce its producers, and the regimes differed mainly in how far the state or the market took over part of that work. In market economies the cost of raising children falls largely on parents, and disproportionately on mothers, even though the next generation of workers and taxpayers benefits everyone (Folbre 1994). Social democracies socialized more of the cost through public care services and policies that supported women’s paid employment, which Esping-Andersen (1990) and Huber and Stephens (2001) treat as part of the social democratic welfare state. The Soviet Union drew women into paid work on a large scale while leaving most domestic work to them, a combination documented by Lapidus (1978).

The household’s internal terms followed the same logic as the producers’ terms elsewhere. Kandiyoti (1988) compared the household systems of sub-Saharan Africa with those she called classic patriarchy, found in a belt running from North Africa through the Middle East to South and East Asia, and showed that women bargained within each system according to its constraints. Her comparison concerns kinship and property systems, not religious doctrine, and it cuts across the regime types of this paper. Laslett and Brenner (1989) trace the historical variation in how families, markets and states divided the work of reproduction. In the model’s terms, these are differences in carers’ fallback \(o_h\) and standing \(\eta_h\), set by property and family law in \(\mathsf{P}\) and \(\mathsf{J}\) and by norms in \(\mathsf{I}\).

5.8 Summary across regimes

The comparison yields one regularity and one negative finding. The regularity is that producers’ terms were worst where control over production was concentrated and coupled to coercion and to control of exchange: collectivization, the colonial mining draft, Gulag labor and the sponsorship of migrants. The negative finding is that this pattern does not follow ideology. It appears under a communist party, a Catholic monarchy and a modern monarchy legitimated by Islam, and its opposite, producers with strong voice, appears under social democracy, in Ottoman guilds and among Burgundian villagers. 6 Mechanisms, failure modes and checks

Producers’ terms in the production game of Definition 7 are set by five quantities: what producers can obtain by leaving, how well they are organized, how concentrated control over their participation is, how much of the coalition’s revenue comes from elsewhere, and whether access to exchange is conditioned on allegiance. Each is a parameter of the game, and the results below give their comparative statics. The model is deliberately simple so that the mechanisms stay separate, and its parameters are not estimated here.

6.1 Exit and concentration

Lemma 17 (Exit floor). If producers are atomized, so that each decides alone whether to participate and the coalition sets \(u\), the coalition’s optimal terms are \(u^*=o\) and the extraction share is \(\chi^*=1-o/y\).

Proof. A producer participates if and only if \(u\ge o\). The coalition’s revenue \(n(y-u)+R\) is decreasing in \(u\), so it chooses the smallest \(u\) at which producers participate, which is \(u=o\). ◻

The exit floor is the whole of atomized producers’ power. It is zero extraction when \(o=y\), as in the band of Proposition 16, and maximal extraction when \(o=y_0\).

Lemma 18 (Concentration lowers the outside option). Suppose a producer who leaves employer \(j\), and is neither blocked nor gated, receives one new offer, from employer \(k\) with probability \(s_k\); an offer from a different employer is worth \(o_1\) and an offer from the employer just left is worth no more than the fallback \(y_0<o_1\). Then the outside option at employer \(j\) is the composite \(o_j\) of Definition 9, and the employment-weighted mean is \(\bar o=y_0+\gamma(1-g)(1-H_{\mathsf{W}})(o_1-y_0)\). Under the exit floor, the employment-weighted mean extraction share is \[\bar\chi=1-\frac{y_0+\gamma(1-g)(1-H_{\mathsf{W}})(o_1-y_0)}{y},\] which increases linearly in \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\).

Proof. An exit succeeds with probability \(\gamma(1-g)\); given success, the offer comes from a different employer with probability \(1-s_j\). The expected value is \(y_0+\gamma(1-g)(1-s_j)(o_1-y_0)=o_j\). Then \(\sum_js_jo_j=y_0+\gamma(1-g)(o_1-y_0)\sum_j s_j(1-s_j)=y_0+\gamma(1-g)(1-H_{\mathsf{W}})(o_1-y_0)\), since \(\sum_js_j=1\). Each employer sets \(u_j=o_j\) by Lemma 17, so \(\chi_j=1-o_j/y\) and the weighted mean is \(1-\bar o/y\). ◻

The lemma gives the source’s concentration index a mechanical role in this pillar. Under its assumptions, average extraction rises linearly with \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\) at the rate \(\gamma(1-g)(o_1-y_0)/y\): the value of a competing offer net of the subsistence floor, scaled by output and by the probability that an exit is neither blocked nor gated. A single employer, \(H_{\mathsf{W}}=1\), holds every producer at the fallback; this is the company town, and in the limit it is the party-state as sole employer before reform. Many small employers, \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\to 0\), give unrestricted producers the competing value \(o_1\). The association between labor-market concentration and lower wages found by Azar et al. (2022) is consistent with the lemma, though random rematching simplifies search in real labor markets (Manning 2003).

6.2 Voice and the withdrawal premium

Organized producers do not accept the exit floor. They bargain collectively, and their threat is withdrawal: a strike, a tax refusal, a boycott, a delivery strike by farmers, or mass flight. If the coalition’s non-producer revenue does not cover its maintenance requirement, withdrawal imposes a cost beyond lost revenue, because the institutions cannot be paid. Let \(\psi\ge 0\) be the cost per unit of that shortfall, so that at total revenue \(T+R\) the coalition’s payoff is \[V_S(T)=T+R-K-\psi\,(K-T-R)^+,\qquad (x)^+=\max(x,0).\]

Assumption 19 (Agreement covers maintenance). The parameters satisfy \(n(1-\eta)(y-o)\ge(1+\eta\psi)(K-R)^+\).

The assumption says that the surplus over producers’ exit floor is large enough for an agreement to pay for the institutions. It holds automatically when \(R\ge K\). When \(R<K\) it fails for \(\eta\) close to one, so fully organized producers facing a coalition without outside revenue lie outside the scope of the next result.

Proposition 20 (Voice share). Let producers bargain collectively with the coalition by the asymmetric Nash bargaining solution with producer weight \(\eta\in[0,1]\), where disagreement gives producers \(no\) and the coalition \(V_S(0)=R-K-\psi(K-R)^+\). Under Assumption 19, the agreed terms are \[u^*=o+\eta(y-o)+\Omega,\qquad \Omega=\frac{\eta\,\psi\,(K-R)^+}{n},\] and the agreement leaves no maintenance shortfall: \(n(y-u^*)+R\ge K\).

Proof. Write \(\Psi=\psi(K-R)^+\) and \(u_K=y-(K-R)^+/n\), the largest terms at which the coalition’s revenue still covers \(K\). For \(u\le u_K\) the coalition’s payoff is \(V_S=n(y-u)+R-K\), which is linear; for \(u>u_K\) it is lower by \(\psi(K-n(y-u)-R)>0\). Consider first the linear problem, which maximizes \(N(u)=(n(u-o))^{\eta}(n(y-u)+\Psi)^{1-\eta}\), the product of the gains \(n(u-o)\) and \(n(y-u)+R-K-V_S(0)=n(y-u)+\Psi\). For \(0<\eta<1\) the logarithm of \(N\) is strictly concave, and the first-order condition \(\eta\,(n(y-u)+\Psi)=(1-\eta)\,n(u-o)\) gives \(nu^*=\eta ny+(1-\eta)no+\eta\Psi\), the stated formula; for \(\eta=0\) and \(\eta=1\) the solution gives the whole gain to the coalition or to the producers, which the formula also covers. At \(u^*\), \(n(y-u^*)+R-K=n(1-\eta)(y-o)-\eta\Psi-(K-R)\), which is non-negative when \(R\ge K\) (then \(\Psi=0\)) and, when \(R<K\), equals \(n(1-\eta)(y-o)-(1+\eta\psi)(K-R)\ge 0\) by Assumption 19. So \(u^*\le u_K\). The true Nash product with the payoff \(V_S\) is never larger than \(N(u)\) and equals it for \(u\le u_K\). Hence \(u^*\), which maximizes \(N\) and lies where the two coincide, also maximizes the true Nash product. ◻

The terms have three parts. The exit floor \(o\) is what producers obtain without organization. The bargained share \(\eta(y-o)\) is what organization adds by splitting the surplus over the floor. The withdrawal premium \(\Omega\) is what organization adds because the coalition cannot afford a withdrawal; it is the price of the latent veto of Proposition 15. Organization is necessary for both of the latter parts. An individual producer’s withdrawal costs the coalition only that producer’s contribution \(y-u\), which in a large population is too small to threaten the maintenance requirement. That is why the latent veto stays latent without collective action (Olson 1965). Unions, guilds, village communities, peasant assemblies, tax revolts and cooperatives are the institutions that raise \(\eta\) above zero.

Corollary 21 (Organization flattens the concentration gradient). Suppose each employer \(j\) bargains with its own workers as in Proposition 20, with common weight \(\eta\), common premium \(\Omega\) and outside option \(o_j\) from Definition 9. Then the employment-weighted mean terms are \(\bar u=\bar o+\eta(y-\bar o)+\Omega\), and \[\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial H_{\mathsf{W}}}=-(1-\eta)\,\gamma\,(1-g)\,(o_1-y_0)\le 0 .\]

Proof. Averaging \(u_j=o_j+\eta(y-o_j)+\Omega\) with weights \(s_j\) gives \(\bar u=(1-\eta)\bar o+\eta y+\Omega\). By Lemma 18, \(\partial\bar o/\partial H_{\mathsf{W}}=-\gamma(1-g)(o_1-y_0)\), and the stated derivative follows. ◻

Hirschman’s loyalty has an ambiguous place in the model. Loyalty that raises the threshold for exit lowers the effective outside option and with it the floor; loyalty that keeps members inside while they organize raises \(\eta\) (Hirschman 1970). Which effect dominates depends on whether the loyal can speak.

The same bargaining logic applies inside the household, where most care labor is done. Agarwal (1997) argued that a household member’s bargaining power depends on the fallback position available outside the household: property, paid employment, access to common resources, and support from kin, community or state. In the notation of the model, a carer’s terms are \(o_h+\eta_h(y_h-o_h)\), where \(y_h\) is the value of the carer’s contribution to household production (paid and unpaid, including care), \(o_h\) is that fallback and \(\eta_h\) the carer’s organized or normative standing. The terms rise with \(o_h\) whenever \(\eta_h<1\), so stronger fallbacks predict a more even division of household work. Property rights, divorce law, public childcare and access to paid work all act on \(o_h\). The paper does not develop the household game further, but the instruments that set \(o_h\) are, like those in Assumption 26 below, held largely in \(\mathsf{P}\), \(\mathsf{J}\) and \(\mathsf{I}\).

6.3 The bondage condition

A coalition can lower the outside option directly by restricting mobility. Write \(o(g)=y_0+(1-g)(o_0-y_0)\), where \(o_0\) is the outside option at \(g=0\) given by Definition 9. Enforcement costs \(\kappa(g)\) per producer, with \(\kappa(0)=0\) and \(\kappa\) increasing and convex. Enforcement is carried out by coercive institutions, so by Definition 2 its cost is part of the institutional maintenance requirement, which becomes \(K+n\kappa(g)\). To avoid a kink we consider two cases: rents fall short of maintenance at every \(g\) (\(R<K\)), with cost factor \(c=1+\eta\psi\), or rents cover it at every \(g\) (\(R\ge K+n\kappa(1)\)), with \(c=1\).

Proposition 22 (Domar-Brenner condition). Suppose the coalition chooses \(g\) and then sets terms, with producers bargaining with weight \(\eta\in[0,1)\) as in Proposition 20 (the atomized case is \(\eta=0\)), that rents satisfy either \(R<K\) or \(R\ge K+n\kappa(1)\), and that Assumption 19 holds at every \(g\) with \(K\) replaced by \(K+n\kappa(g)\). Then in each of the two cases the coalition’s payoff, net of terms that do not depend on \(g\), is \(n[(1-\eta)g(o_0-y_0)-c\,\kappa(g)]\).

  1. If \(\kappa(g)=\kappa g\) is linear, restriction is chosen (\(g^*=1\)) when \((1-\eta)(o_0-y_0)>c\,\kappa\) and not chosen (\(g^*=0\)) when \((1-\eta)(o_0-y_0)<c\,\kappa\).

  2. If \(\kappa\) is differentiable and strictly convex with \(c\,\kappa'(0)<(1-\eta)(o_0-y_0)<c\,\kappa'(1)\), then \(g^*\in(0,1)\) is the unique solution of \(c\,\kappa'(g^*)=(1-\eta)(o_0-y_0)\), and \(g^*\) is increasing in \(o_0-y_0\) and decreasing in \(\eta\) (and, when \(R<K\), in \(\psi\)).

Proof. By Proposition 20, the producers’ terms at outside option \(o\) are \(u^*=o+\eta(y-o)+\Omega\), where now \(\Omega=\eta\psi(K+n\kappa(g)-R)^+/n\), and the agreement leaves no shortfall, so the coalition’s payoff is \(n(y-u^*)+R-K-n\kappa(g)=n[(1-\eta)(y-o)-\Omega]+R-K-n\kappa(g)\). When \(R<K\), \(n\Omega=\eta\psi(K-R)+\eta\psi n\kappa(g)\); when \(R\ge K+n\kappa(1)\), \(\Omega=0\). Substituting \(o(g)=o_0-g(o_0-y_0)\), the \(g\)-dependent part is \(n(1-\eta)g(o_0-y_0)-n(1+\eta\psi)\kappa(g)\) in the first case and \(n(1-\eta)g(o_0-y_0)-n\kappa(g)\) in the second, which is the stated form. In case (a) the payoff is linear in \(g\) with slope \(n[(1-\eta)(o_0-y_0)-c\,\kappa]\), so the optimum is at \(g=1\) when the slope is positive and at \(g=0\) when it is negative. In case (b) the payoff is strictly concave, its derivative is positive at \(g=0\) and negative at \(g=1\), so the unique maximizer satisfies the first-order condition. Because \(\kappa'\) is strictly increasing, a larger ratio \((1-\eta)(o_0-y_0)/c\) requires a larger \(g^*\); the ratio increases in \(o_0-y_0\) and decreases in \(\eta\) and \(\psi\). ◻

The proposition excludes the middle range \(K\le R<K+n\kappa(1)\). There the \(g\)-dependent payoff is \(n(1-\eta)g(o_0-y_0)-n\kappa(g)-\eta\psi(K+n\kappa(g)-R)^+\), so the effective cost factor equals \(1\) for restrictions whose enforcement rents still cover and \(1+\eta\psi\) beyond the point \(\hat g\) where \(n\kappa(\hat g)=R-K\). The payoff is still concave, since \(\kappa\) is convex and increasing, so an optimum exists and is unique when \(\kappa\) is strictly convex, but the threshold is piecewise rather than a single inequality.

The proposition places two classic explanations of bondage in one inequality. Domar’s land-labor hypothesis concerns \(o_0\): where land is abundant, a free peasant can leave and farm independently, so \(o_0\) is high and binding the peasant pays (Domar 1970). Where land is scarce and labor abundant, \(o_0\) is already close to \(y_0\), the wage market holds producers near the floor, and restriction adds little. Brenner’s objection concerns \(\kappa\) and \(\eta\): organized peasant communities raise the cost of enforcing restriction and bargain over terms, so the same land-labor ratio produced serfdom in one region and free tenure in another (Brenner 1976). The two accounts are not rivals in this form. They name different terms of the same condition, and the empirical question is which term varied more across the cases at issue.

Organization also lowers the return to restriction, since a coalition facing organized producers captures only the fraction \(1-\eta\) of any reduction in their outside option. That gives a reason for coercive suppression of organization to accompany mobility restriction, a joint pattern visible in collectivization and in the sponsorship system for migrants, where workers’ organization was also restricted. Part V (Long 2026a) gives the primary treatment of this pair in its Section 6.5, including the allegiance margin of sanctions (its Definition 2.5) on which coercive enforcement of a gate depends.

6.4 Rents

Proposition 23 (Rentier comparative static). In Proposition 20, the withdrawal premium \(\Omega\) is non-increasing in non-producer revenue \(R\). It falls at rate \(\eta\psi/n\) while \(R<K\) and is zero for all \(R\ge K\). The exit floor and the bargained share do not depend on \(R\).

Proof. \(\Omega=\eta\psi(K-R)^+/n\). For \(R<K\) its derivative in \(R\) is \(-\eta\psi/n\le 0\); for \(R\ge K\) it is identically zero. The other two terms, \(o\) and \(\eta(y-o)\), contain no \(R\). Raising \(R\) relaxes Assumption 19, so the result stays within its scope. ◻

The proposition refines the rentier-state argument. Rents do not lower producers’ terms by making producers less productive or less organized. They remove the part of producers’ bargaining power that rested on the coalition’s need for their contribution, and they remove it only up to the point where rents cover the maintenance requirement. Beyond that point additional rent changes nothing in this model. Within a country, the effect of rents on concessions to producers should therefore be concentrated near the threshold at which rents cover the cost of running the state, and small far above or below it. Across countries, a null average effect such as that reported by Haber and Menaldo (2011) is consistent with the model if most variation lies away from the threshold. Measuring the maintenance requirement is difficult, and Section 7 proposes a proxy.

For the citizens of a rentier state, the premium is near zero and their terms are set by the coalition’s choice to distribute rent, not by their capacity to withhold. The producers of the rent are elsewhere, and Lemma 13 says where: in the extraction workforce, which in the Gulf monarchies is largely foreign, and among foreign buyers.

6.5 The participation gate

A coalition can lower producers’ outside options without restricting their movement, by conditioning access to exchange. A producer who cannot buy inputs, sell output, obtain credit or be hired has nowhere to go even if free to walk away.

Proposition 24 (Gate). Let \(o\) denote the outside option at \(\gamma=1\), so that under the gate of Definition 8 it is \(o_\gamma=y_0+\gamma(o-y_0)\). Under Assumption 19, the agreed terms are \(u^*(\gamma)=o_\gamma+\eta(y-o_\gamma)+\Omega\), and \[\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial\gamma}=(1-\eta)(o-y_0)\ge 0 .\] If the gate is closed (\(\gamma=0\)), producers are atomized (\(\eta=0\)) and rents cover the maintenance requirement (\(R\ge K\)), producers are held at the autarky value, \(u^*=y_0\), and the coalition takes \(n(y-y_0)\).

Proof. Substitute \(o_\gamma\) for \(o\) in Proposition 20. Since \(do_\gamma/d\gamma=o-y_0\) and \(u^*\) depends on the outside option through \((1-\eta)o_\gamma\), the derivative follows. With \(\gamma=0\), \(o_\gamma=y_0\); with \(\eta=0\), \(\Omega=0\) and \(u^*=o_\gamma=y_0\), and the coalition’s revenue from producers is \(n(y-y_0)\). ◻

The closed gate with atomized producers is the most complete form of producer dependence in the model. Nothing about it requires the producers to be owned, bound to land, or imprisoned. It requires only that access to exchange pass through a checkpoint controlled by the coalition and that the checkpoint test allegiance. The coalition needs control over exchange (markets, credit, licenses, employment) to build the checkpoint, a criterion of allegiance to apply at it, and enforcement to keep producers from trading around it. Part IV (Section 6.6) treats the coupling of belief and production from the side of belief, including the quasi-voluntary compliance of taxpayers. Section 8 returns to this configuration.

6.6 Capture of the productive pillar

Definition 25 (Capture of the productive pillar). A productive capture link is a capture link \((S\to j,\mathsf{W})\) in the sense of Largo (2026) in which the privileged discretion held by \(S\) is control over one of the parameters through which the production game sets producers’ terms: their exit (through \(g\) or the concentration \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\)), their organization (through \(\eta\)), their access to exchange (through \(\gamma\)), or the coalition’s exposure to their withdrawal (through non-producer revenue \(R\) or the shortfall cost \(\psi\)). Material evidence must tie the exercise of that control to the terms \(u\) or the extraction share \(\chi\). The productive pillar is captured at \(t\) when productive capture links persist and the pillar’s term \(H_{\mathsf{W},t}(1-Q^{\mathsf{W}}_t)\) in \(U_t\) is high.

The definition inherits the source’s three conditions, including the requirement of material evidence. Low wages, high taxes or concentrated employment do not by themselves establish capture. They may reflect low productivity, the cost of public goods that producers value, or economies of scale. Capture requires a documented instrument and a documented link from the instrument to the terms.

Assumption 26 (Assignment of instruments). The instruments that change the parameters of the production game are exercised in the following pillars. Mobility restriction \(g\) requires enforcement in \(\mathsf{C}\). Concentration of control over productive means, which raises \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\), is an allocation of resources in \(\mathsf{F}\). Lowering producers’ organized weight \(\eta\) requires prohibition in \(\mathsf{P}\), enforcement in \(\mathsf{C}\) or delegitimation in \(\mathsf{I}\). Closing the gate requires control of exchange in \(\mathsf{F}\), an allegiance criterion supplied in \(\mathsf{I}\) (the legitimation function of Part IV, Definition 2.1, the practices that produce and validate the beliefs about rules and authority on which members’ coordination depends (Long 2026d)), and enforcement in \(\mathsf{C}\). Raising non-producer revenue \(R\) is an allocation in \(\mathsf{F}\). Lowering the shortfall cost \(\psi\) requires reserves accumulated in \(\mathsf{F}\) or the lawful hiring of replacement producers, whose permission is set in \(\mathsf{P}\).

Corollary 27 (Capture requires coupling). Under Assumption 26, any action by a coalition \(S\) that lowers producers’ terms \(u^*\) below their value at the baseline parameters \((H_{\mathsf{W}}^0,g=0,\eta_0,\gamma=1,R_0,\psi_0)\) is taken in at least one pillar \(n\ne\mathsf{W}\). Hence a captured productive pillar implies \(C_{n\mathsf{W},t}>0\) for some \(n\in\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{I},\mathsf{C}\}\), with \(S\) the coalition in question.

Proof. By Proposition 20, Proposition 24 and Lemma 18, \(u^*\) is non-decreasing in \(o\), \(\eta\), \(\gamma\) and \(\psi\) and non-increasing in \(R\), with \(o\) non-increasing in \(g\) and \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\) by Definition 9. Holding output \(y\) and the maintenance requirement \(K\) fixed, a reduction of \(u^*\) therefore requires raising \(g\) or \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\), lowering \(\eta\), \(\gamma\) or \(\psi\), or raising \(R\). By Assumption 26 each of these is exercised in a pillar other than \(\mathsf{W}\). If \(S\) exercises it and also holds the terms of production, \(S\) commands decisions in both \(\mathsf{W}\) and that pillar, which is \(C_{n\mathsf{W},t}>0\). ◻

The corollary depends on the assignment, which is a modeling claim rather than a theorem. The hardest case for it is the hiring of replacement workers during a strike, which happens in the labor market itself; the assignment places it in \(\mathsf{P}\) because whether replacement is lawful is decided there. The assignment is defensible because each instrument in the comparative record was in fact exercised outside the productive layer: passports and residence permits by police and ministries, procurement prices by planners, union prohibitions by legislatures and courts, and allegiance tests by the authorities that define loyalty. Its consequence is that capture of production is never a fact about production alone. A society in which producers work long hours for low pay under many competing employers, free to organize and to leave, has a poor productive pillar but not a captured one. This is the pillar-level form of Largo’s requirement that a strong classification show both concentration and cross-pillar integration (Largo 2026), and of Part VII’s result that Proposition A does not imply Proposition B. The function is implemented at every parameter value of the production game. Capture is a region of the parameter space.

6.7 Failure modes and checks

Each instrument in the production game defines a failure mode, and each failure mode has a check that interrupts it.

Concentration of employment or purchase lowers the outside option (Lemma 18). Antitrust enforcement in labor markets, the entry of new employers and public employment of last resort raise \(o\). Cooperatives add an employer that producers themselves control (Dow 2003).

Mobility restriction lowers the outside option directly (Proposition 22). Portability of benefits, abolition of tied residence and the right to change employer interrupt it. The condition in Proposition 22 also shows when the check is under most pressure: when producers’ free outside options are high, the coalition gains most from restriction.

Suppression of organization lowers \(\eta\) and with it both the bargained share and the withdrawal premium (Proposition 20). Legal protection of association and strikes, independent courts that enforce it (Part III, Section 6), and union democracy interrupt it. Michels’s argument that organizations develop oligarchic leaderships applies to unions as well (Michels 1915), and Part II states it as a conditional tendency (its Proposition 6.2) rather than a law. The International Typographical Union studied by Lipset et al. (1956) is the classic documented exception, a union that kept a two-party internal democracy.

Rents remove the withdrawal premium (Proposition 23). Fiscal rules that route rents into funds outside the coalition’s discretion, and taxation that keeps the state dependent on domestic producers, are the checks the model suggests. Their effectiveness is an empirical question this paper does not settle.

The gate lowers outside options through control of exchange (Proposition 24). Open markets, multiple sources of credit and employment, and the legal separation of economic access from political allegiance keep \(\gamma\) close to one. The check is a separation of pillars: the actors who test allegiance must not control exchange.

The fiscal hypotheses, HW2, HW3 and HW8, test the coalition’s dependence on producers. The voice hypotheses, HW5, HW6 and HW9, test what happens to producers whose organized weight is low. Two identification problems apply to all nine. Organization is endogenous to the terms it is supposed to explain, since producers organize more where they expect gains. Rents, concentration and gates are chosen by coalitions that anticipate producers’ responses. Event studies around reforms that change one instrument at a time, of the kind Largo proposes for oversight reforms (Largo 2026), are the most direct remedy. Examples include the abolition of tied residence, the legalization of independent unions and the creation of a sovereign fund that removes rents from current budgets.

8 The Revelation reading

The series reads Revelation 13 and 17 to 18 typologically: the beast is a recurring pattern of power that claims ultimate loyalty, and Babylon is the idolatrous city of every age (Bauckham 1993; Beale 1999). The reading is anchored in the text’s first-century setting, the Roman province of Asia, where the imperial cult was a civic institution woven into local life (Price 1984; Friesen 2001). Futurist readings that identify the beast or the mark with a coming government, person, product, technology or payment system are excluded. Their reception history is a record of failed identifications (Boyer 1992; Fuller 1995; McGinn 1998). Historicist readings that identified the beast with a particular church or ruler belong to the history of polemic and are excluded for the same reason. The mapping of images to pillars below is a heuristic that aids comparison. It is an interpretive claim of this project, not a settled exegetical result.

The passage most directly relevant to this pillar is Revelation 13:16 and 17 (KJV): “And he causeth all, both small and great, rich and poor, free and bond, to receive a mark in their right hand, or in their foreheads: And that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark, or the name of the beast, or the number of his name.” The verse does not describe a form of labor. It describes a condition on participation in exchange. Its scope runs across every category of the social order, “free and bond” included, so the condition is not the bondage of a class but the gating of everyone’s access to buying and selling by a sign of allegiance.

In the terms of Section 6, the verse depicts a closed participation gate, \(\gamma=0\), applied to all who withhold allegiance. Proposition 24 says what such a gate requires and what it yields. It requires control over exchange, a criterion of allegiance and enforcement. In the vision these are distributed across figures: the first beast holds authority and makes war (13:2, 13:4, 13:7); the second beast speaks, performs signs and causes the image of the first to be worshipped (13:12 to 13:15); and the restriction on buying and selling is its economic instrument. The series’ heuristic maps these to \(\mathsf{P}\) and \(\mathsf{C}\), to \(\mathsf{I}\), and to the coupling of \(\mathsf{F}\) with \(\mathsf{W}\). The gate also yields a precise result: producers without organization or exit are held to their autarky value. The vision’s image of economic exclusion as the sanction for refused allegiance is the limiting case of the model, and it is a limiting case of capture (Proposition B), not of the productive function itself.

The preterist anchor gives the image economic content. Kraybill (1996) argued that participation in the maritime trade and trade guilds of Asia Minor entangled Christians in the imperial cult, and that John’s vision calls them to withdraw from commerce on those terms. Bauckham (1991) read the lament over Babylon in Revelation 18 as an economic critique of Rome. The cargo list of 18:11 to 18:13 runs through luxury goods and ends with “slaves, and souls of men,” an indictment of a trade that included human beings. This is literal chattel slavery in the Roman economy. It is the extreme of Table 1 and must not be read as a metaphor for wage labor or taxation. The list also illustrates the relocation lemma: the goods that sustained the imperial center were produced across the provinces and beyond them, and the kings, merchants and shipmasters who lament Babylon’s fall (18:9 to 18:19) are those whose wealth depended on that flow.

The text’s response to the gate has the shape of Hirschman’s triad, though it was written for different purposes. The imperative “Come out of her, my people” (18:4) is a call to exit. Faithful witness (11:3 to 11:13) and the “patience and the faith of the saints” (13:10) are a form of voice that refuses the demanded loyalty. Loyalty itself is the contested object: the vision depicts a demand for worship, and the faithful are those who give their allegiance elsewhere. Rossing (1999) read the contrast between Babylon and the New Jerusalem as a choice between two cities, with an economic critique of imperial Rome at its center. Wink (1992) generalized the beast into a domination system present in institutions of every age, a reading congenial to the series’ claim that the structure is universal.

The analogy has limits that must be stated. Revelation is apocalyptic literature, and its resolution is divine judgment, not producers’ bargaining. Collins (1984) argued that the book addresses a crisis that was partly perceived, written to produce catharsis in its readers, and Thompson (1990) questioned the extent of persecution under Domitian. The verses supply an image of the limiting case of capture in this pillar, and the model supplies the conditions under which that limit is approached. Neither depends on the other. The analytic claims of this paper stand without the theological reading, and the reading identifies no present institution with the beast.

9 Objections and scope conditions

A Marxian reader will object that production is not one pillar among six but the base on which the others rest, and that the framework flattens class relations into a network. The paper’s own results partly concede the point. The latent veto gives production a status that no other pillar has: every other function consumes what this one supplies. But Proposition A asserts only that all six functions exist and depend on one another, not that they carry equal causal weight. Whether production determines the other pillars is a claim about the dependency matrix \(B_t\), and it is an empirical question. Largo describes the framework as mid-range institutional theory that treats producers as active rather than as a passive base (Largo 2026). The functional-explanation debate in Marxism (Cohen 1978; Elster 1985) is a reminder that claims of determination need mechanisms, which is what the production game attempts to supply.

A liberal reader will object that the extraction share mislabels returns to capital and payments for public services, and that market exchange is voluntary. The model accommodates the objection rather than rejecting it. The extraction share is net of services returned. Under competitive conditions, with many employers and free mobility, Lemma 18 gives producers the competitive value of their labor, and the liberal benchmark is the case \(H_{\mathsf{W}}\to 0\). Capture requires a documented instrument and a documented link to terms. What the model denies is only that voluntariness is a property of exchange as such. It depends on the outside option, and the outside option is shaped by concentration, restriction and gates.

A functionalist objection runs the other way: the paper may have explained the existence of production by its usefulness. It has not. Lemma 12 is a condition of persistence. It says that persistent collectives produce, not that their productive institutions exist because they help the collective persist. This is the standard of Hempel (1959) and Elster (1983), and the series adopts it throughout. Merton (1968) supplies the further point that functional alternatives exist: the same function is met by foraging bands, serf villages, kolkhozes and labor markets.

Unpaid household labor enters the formal results in two places only: the reproduction lemma, which makes care a condition of persistence, and the household application of the bargaining model. The production game itself treats its \(n\) producers as identical and sets their terms with the coalition, so it does not derive how care is divided within households or how that division interacts with the terms of paid work. A full treatment would solve the household game jointly with the production game, with each member’s fallback set by property, divorce law and access to paid work. This paper states the household game’s structure and one hypothesis (HW9) but does not solve it jointly.

The comparative statics are monotone in the parameters, and that monotonicity, not the functional forms, carries the paper’s qualitative claims. The functional forms are simple. The production game is stationary, its producers are identical within a group, and the asymmetric Nash solution summarizes rather than models the process of strikes and concessions. Real coalitions face heterogeneous producers, such as citizens and migrants or capital and labor, so the model must be applied group by group.

Two simplifications bear directly on the size of the effects. Reserves and borrowing let coalitions survive withdrawal longer than the stationary shortfall cost suggests, which shrinks the withdrawal premium. The concentration lemma assumes random rematching in proportion to employment shares, which fixes the linear form of the concentration gradient. Relaxations that preserve monotonicity leave the signs of the results intact; their magnitudes require estimation.

The claims about producer power are conditional on human activity remaining necessary for production. Lemma 13 keeps the function in place under full automation, but it moves the producer role to the owners and maintainers of equipment, and the latent veto of labor goes with it.

10 Conclusion

Production is the one function every other function consumes. From the maintenance axiom, a persistent collective must produce at a positive average rate, and rents, tribute and machines relocate production without removing it. Producers therefore hold a latent veto over any governance system whose outside revenue falls short of its maintenance requirement. The production game prices that veto. Producers’ terms rise with their outside option and their organization, fall linearly with the concentration of control over their participation, and lose their withdrawal premium once rents cover the cost of running the state. Restriction of mobility pays exactly when free exit is valuable and enforcement cheap.

Every instrument that lowers producers’ terms is exercised in another pillar. The capture of production is therefore always a coupling of production with coercion, allocation, rule-making or legitimation, and never a property of production alone. The comparison of six regime types found the productive function in all of them and its worst forms where control over production was concentrated and coupled to coercion and to control of exchange: under a communist party, a Catholic monarchy and a modern monarchy legitimated by Islam alike. Producers’ voice was strongest under social democracy, in some Ottoman guilds and among Burgundian villagers using royal courts. Ideology did not sort the cases. The instruments did.

What remains uncertain is quantitative. None of the model’s parameters has been estimated here, and the sharpest prediction, that rents matter most where they nearly cover the cost of the state, awaits a test. The structural claim does not wait on that test. Every governance system in the record depends on those who produce for it, and the image of a world in which no one may buy or sell without the mark describes the condition in which that dependence has been turned entirely against them.
