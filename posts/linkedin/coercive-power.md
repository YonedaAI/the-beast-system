Who enforces the rules? Part V of the Beast System series derives the coercive function from one axiom, that members sometimes have reason to break rules, and compares how six regime types distribute it.

A deterrence theorem shows that when the share of agents tempted to violate a rule exceeds the violation rate the collective can tolerate, compliance requires that violators' continuation payoffs fall because of their own conduct. A sanction regime of some kind is therefore necessary, but no particular institution is. Gossip, ostracism and coalition action against would-be dominants meet the requirement as well as police do, as the ethnography of the Nuer, the Ju/'hoansi, Amazonian societies and Ostrom's commons documents. Where sanctions are costly public goods and higher-order sanctions are absent, coercion at scale depends on provisioning or on shared norms of punishment.

The paper then separates two properties of any enforcement apparatus: its conduct margin, how much more likely a violator is to be sanctioned than a complier, and its allegiance margin, how much more likely an opponent is to be sanctioned than a supporter. The margins vary independently, so the necessity of coercion does not imply its capture. When capacity is limited, allegiance targeting weakens deterrence for at least one group. When opponents are targeted regardless of conduct, it also raises the volume of coercion against them, so spending and arrest counts mismeasure coercive power.

The regime evidence is qualitative, and the paper specifies how both margins can be measured. Applied evenly to capitalist, communist party-state, democratic socialist, Islamic, historical Catholic monarchical and secular democratic systems, the framework asks the same two questions of each.

The ideology changes. The institutional functions remain.
