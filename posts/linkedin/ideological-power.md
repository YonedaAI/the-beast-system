Who shapes the beliefs that cooperation depends on? Part IV of the Beast System series treats this as the ideological pillar of a six-pillar model of governance and asks whether the pillar is necessary and when it becomes an instrument of capture.

From a coordination axiom, together with membership turnover and the cross-cultural variability of rules, we derive that every persistent collective implements a recognition function: a recurring public practice that makes the operative rules common knowledge. This establishes the necessity of a function, not of any institution, and it does not establish that belief in the rightness of rules is necessary. Stateless and egalitarian societies implement it through talk, ridicule, ritual and kinship idiom, without a designated office.

A Bayesian endorsement model then yields three results. A captured authority's endorsement carries no information. Independent authorities raise expected public confidence in sound rules and lower it for unsound ones. And coordinated control of several authorities lowers the probability that an error is disclosed below the level reached by independent control with the same average independence.

We compare how academia, religious institutions, media, schools and medical and scientific bodies hold the function in market-capitalist, communist party-state, social-democratic, Islamic, Catholic-monarchical and secular-democratic regimes. The codings come from the historical and scholarly record, and hypotheses state what a quantitative estimate of concentration and integration would check.

The ideology changes. The institutional functions remain.
